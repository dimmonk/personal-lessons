# Identifying Psychological Patterns — A Practical Course

**Purpose**
Not a diagnosis manual. Not a vocabulary list for insults. The ability to watch a real interaction, or read a real passage of dialogue or behavior, and correctly name what's happening — a reasoning distortion, an interpersonal tactic, or a personality pattern — using stable criteria instead of pop-psychology buzzwords or however unpleasant someone seems.

**How to use**
Work through each lesson. Answer the drills *before* checking the key. Write your reasoning down. If the label is right but the reasoning is wrong, count it wrong. The goal is diagnostic skill you can actually use on your own life, not a list of words to throw at people you dislike.

**A caution before you start**
This course teaches you to think more precisely about psychological patterns. It does **not** qualify you to diagnose anyone — including yourself. Real diagnosis needs a licensed clinician, a structured interview, time, and the ruling-out of other explanations. Treat every label in this course as a hypothesis to hold loosely, not a verdict to hand down. The [Honest caveats](#honest-caveats) section at the end is not an afterthought — read it before you use any of this on a real person.

## Lesson 1 — The Diagnostic Mindset

Before any specific pattern, you need the general discipline. Almost every misread in this territory happens because someone skips straight to a label. The five questions below slow that down.

| # | Question | Why it discriminates |
|---|---|---|
| **D1** | **What kind of thing is this?** <br>a moment of reasoning / a move in an interaction / a stable way someone is | The fastest discriminator — it decides which key you even need |
| **D2** | **Pervasive and stable, or situational and reactive?** | Separates a trait or disorder from an ordinary bad day |
| **D3** | **What need, fear, or contradiction does it seem to resolve?** | Reveals the function, not just the behavior |
| **D4** | **What's the nearest look-alike — and what specifically separates them?** | Forces precision instead of a vibes-based label |
| **D5** | **What would falsify this read?** | Keeps the label falsifiable instead of sticky |

### D1 is doing most of the work

Answer D1 and you've already picked which part of this course applies:

- **A mind justifying itself** → the reasoning / self-justification family (Lesson 2): dissonance, confirmation bias, motivated reasoning, sunk cost.
- **A move in an interaction** → the tactics family (Lesson 5): gaslighting, DARVO, love-bombing, projection.
- **A stable way someone consistently is** → the personality-pattern family (Lessons 3–4): narcissism and its look-alikes.

These are genuinely different kinds of things, and this course does not force them into one flat checklist. A single confusing moment with someone is not evidence of a personality disorder, and a personality disorder is not just "someone who did a manipulative thing once." Conflating the three is the single most common error in casual psychological talk — the direct analogue of classifying a political movement by its methods instead of its content.

### The trap: pathologizing a moment

Everyone rationalizes sometimes. Everyone gets defensive sometimes. Everyone wants to be noticed sometimes. **D2 exists specifically to stop you from taking one data point and naming a disorder.** A pattern needs to be pervasive — across time, across relationships, across contexts — before "disorder" language is earned. One bad night is a moment. The same shape for five years, in every relationship, is a pattern.

**Drill 1.1** — For each, name what *kind* of thing this is (D1): a moment of reasoning, a move in an interaction, a stable way someone is — or none of those.

1. "I know I said I'd never date someone who does that again, but he's different" — said while listing three of the exact behaviors she swore off.
2. A coworker denies ever agreeing to the deadline, then says *you're* the one who's always disorganized — in the same conversation where the email trail says otherwise.
3. Ten years, every job, every friendship: he is the misunderstood genius, and everyone else eventually turns out to be an idiot or an enemy.
4. "I've put four years into this degree, I can't switch majors now" — said about a major she dislikes every class of.
5. She's withdrawn and short-tempered this week. She just found out her father is sick.

**Drill 1.2**
Two people both said something hurtful, then denied saying it. One turns out to have genuinely misremembered under stress. The other has done this exact thing in every serious relationship for a decade, always followed by the other person apologizing. Which question (D1–D5) separates them fastest, and what does it turn on?

## Lesson 2 — Cognitive Dissonance and the Self-Justification Family

"Everyone's a hypocrite" is not an analysis. This lesson gives you the actual mechanism, and the three biases that travel with it.

### Core definition

**Cognitive dissonance** (Festinger): the discomfort of holding two contradictory cognitions — a belief and a contradicting behavior, or two contradicting beliefs — and the drive to resolve that discomfort. Critically, the resolution does not require *honestly changing your mind*. There are four moves, and only one of them is that.

### The four moves

1. **Change the belief** to match the behavior or evidence — genuine, if it actually happens.
2. **Change the behavior** to match the belief — also genuine, if it actually happens.
3. **Add a justifying cognition**, without changing either — "I only lied because he would have overreacted." Belief and behavior both stay exactly the same; a permission slip gets written after the fact.
4. **Discredit the source** of the contradicting information — the easiest move, and the most damaging, because it protects the belief by attacking the evidence instead of engaging it.

**Tell:** dissonance reduction almost always shows up *after* the behavior, as a repair job. The behavior or decision came first; the story came second.

### Confirmation bias

Not "believing what you want to believe" in general — specifically **asymmetric scrutiny**: evidence that fits the existing belief gets accepted quickly and lightly; evidence that contradicts it gets held to a much higher standard, picked apart, or waved off as an exception.
**Tell:** ask how much scrutiny each *type* of evidence received. If confirming evidence sailed through and disconfirming evidence got cross-examined, that's the signature.

### Motivated reasoning

The conclusion is fixed **before** the reasoning starts; the reasoning is reverse-engineered to arrive somewhere it was already going. This is the easiest thing in the family to confuse with dissonance reduction, and timing is what separates them.
**Tell:** dissonance reduction responds to discomfort that arrives *after* a behavior. Motivated reasoning starts *with* the destination already chosen — often before any specific behavior has even happened yet ("I've decided to hire my friend's kid; now let me find the reasons he's the best candidate").

### Sunk cost / escalation of commitment

Justifying further investment — money, time, a relationship — by appeal to what's already been spent, rather than by what the future investment is actually worth. Frequently *powered by* dissonance: admitting the first four years were wasted is exactly the discomfort avoided by spending a fifth.
**Tell:** the stated reason for continuing points backward ("I've already put in so much") instead of forward ("this is still the best use of the next year").

### The common error to kill now

**Changing your mind is not a bias.** If someone updates a belief because the evidence genuinely warranted it — and they can say what would have moved them the other way too — that's reasoning working correctly, not a distortion. The tell is direction: did the evidence lead to the conclusion, or did the conclusion lead the search for evidence? Treating *all* belief change as suspect is itself a mistake, and it's the mirror image of never updating at all.

**Drill 2.1** — Classify: (a) dissonance reduction (b) confirmation bias (c) motivated reasoning (d) sunk cost / escalation of commitment (e) genuine belief revision

1. "I already told everyone I was vegan, so I'm not going to make a big deal out of the fact that the sauce probably has fish stock in it."
2. He reads three studies supporting his diet closely and enthusiastically, and dismisses the one contradicting study as "funded by industry" — without checking who funded the other three.
3. She decided to promote her friend before the interviews even happened, and spent the interviews taking notes that supported that outcome.
4. "We've spent two years and forty thousand dollars on this renovation, we can't stop now" — said after they can no longer afford to finish it properly.
5. "I used to think the policy would work. Turnout data from the three cities that tried it changed my mind, even though I really wanted it to work."
6. A manager who prides himself on being data-driven quietly stops checking the metric that turned negative last quarter, while still citing the ones that are positive.

**Drill 2.2**
A friend swears they're not upset about a decision, then spends twenty minutes explaining, unprompted, all the reasons the decision was actually fine. Is this necessarily dissonance reduction? What would you need to know to be sure, and what's the falsifier (D5)?

## Lesson 3 — The Narcissism Spectrum

Confidence, self-esteem, and narcissism get used interchangeably in casual speech. They are three different things, and conflating them is a large part of why "narcissist" has become mostly an insult.

### Not one dial

- **Confidence** — situational belief in your ability to do a specific thing. Can coexist with humility about everything else.
- **Self-esteem** — a general, stable sense of your own worth. Healthy self-esteem is compatible with genuine warmth, real empathy, and taking criticism without collapsing or attacking.
- **Narcissism** — a self-concept organized around being exceptional, requiring external admiration to maintain it, and structurally low in empathy for anyone whose function isn't to provide that admiration.

**The discriminator is not confidence level. It's what happens to the person's regard for *other people* once the confidence gets tested.**

### Grandiose narcissism

The stereotype, and the easier one to spot: overt, entitled, dominance-seeking, requires admiration, name-drops achievements unprompted, reacts to criticism with contempt or rage (**narcissistic injury**), treats relationships instrumentally.
**Tell:** the mask cracks *outward*, as aggression toward whoever threatened it.

### Vulnerable narcissism

The one people miss constantly, because it doesn't look like the stereotype at all: covert, hypersensitive to any perceived slight, chronically feels under-appreciated and wronged, avoids risk rather than seeking dominance — but it is still fundamentally organized around the self, and still runs low on empathy for others' independent reality. The grandiosity is present but internal ("I deserve better than this, and no one sees it") rather than performed.
**Tell:** the mask cracks *inward*, as withdrawal, self-pity, and quiet resentment — but the underlying structure (the world owes recognition to my unmet specialness) is the same as the grandiose form.

### The actual clinical criteria, stated plainly

A pervasive pattern, present by early adulthood and across contexts, of: grandiosity (in fantasy or behavior), need for excessive admiration, and lack of empathy — typically alongside several of: sense of entitlement, interpersonal exploitation, envy of others (or the belief that others envy them), arrogant or haughty behavior, preoccupation with fantasies of unlimited success or power, and a belief in being special or unique.

**The two words that matter most: pervasive and impairing.** Most people show two or three of these sometimes. That is not the disorder. The disorder requires the pattern to be the person's stable operating mode, not their behavior under stress, threat, or grief.

**Drill 3.1** — Classify: (a) healthy confidence/self-esteem (b) narcissistic traits, non-clinical (c) grandiose narcissistic pattern (d) vulnerable narcissistic pattern (e) insufficient evidence

1. She's proud of the promotion, thanks the people who helped her get it, and is visibly happy for a colleague who got promoted the same week.
2. He redirects every conversation back to his own achievements within about ninety seconds, regardless of topic, and has for as long as anyone's known him.
3. Passed over for a role he wanted, he tells people privately that the company "isn't ready for someone like him," and that the person who got it will fail within a year.
4. A new employee snaps at a reasonable correction in her first stressful week on the job, then apologizes the next day.
5. He never raises his voice and never asks for anything — and has spent fifteen years quietly certain nobody has ever appreciated what he's actually capable of, growing colder toward anyone who seems to be doing better than him.
6. "That's such a narcissistic thing to say" — about a friend who mentioned being proud of finishing a marathon.

**Drill 3.2**
A public figure is famously boastful in interviews. Is that sufficient to conclude grandiose narcissism? What additional evidence — and which diagnostic question — would you need before that label is earned rather than guessed?

## Lesson 4 — Look-Alikes and False Friends

Narcissism is the most over-applied label in casual psychological speech. This lesson is the antidote: the disorders it actually gets confused with, and what specifically separates them.

### Narcissistic vs. borderline pattern

| | Narcissistic pattern | Borderline pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Core fear | Being ordinary / unadmired | Being abandoned |
| Self-image | Fixed grandiosity (or its vulnerable mirror) | Unstable — swings, often within the same relationship |
| View of others | Instrumental — valued as a supply of admiration | Idealized, then devalued, sometimes re-idealized ("splitting") |
| Reaction to conflict | Contempt, dismissal, cutting off | Panic, frantic repair attempts, or sudden intense devaluation |
| Capacity for attachment | Often genuinely limited | Usually intense and real — the terror of losing it *is* the problem |

**Tell:** if the driving emotion is "don't leave me," it pulls toward borderline. If it's "how dare you not admire me," it pulls toward narcissistic.

### Narcissistic vs. histrionic pattern

Histrionic is about being *noticed*, not necessarily admired for superiority. More emotionally expressive and theatrical in the moment; less overt entitlement; often genuinely warm and suggestible (easily influenced by others), where narcissism resists influence that doesn't flatter it.
**Tell:** histrionic wants an audience. Narcissistic wants a mirror. The difference shows up the moment attention shifts to someone else: histrionic tends to escalate for attention; narcissistic tends to devalue whoever now has it.

### Narcissistic vs. antisocial pattern / psychopathy

Antisocial centers on disregard for rules and other people's rights — impulsivity, routine deceit, no remorse for concrete harm done. It doesn't require grandiosity or a fragile ego at all; many antisocial patterns are simply indifferent to whether they're admired. Psychopathy specifically adds a more totalizing absence of empathy — not the *selective* withholding narcissism shows (reserved for people who don't serve it), but a flatter, more instrumental view of people generally.
**Tell:** a narcissist wants you to see them as superior. An antisocial or psychopathic pattern usually doesn't care what you think of them at all — only what you can be used for.

### The trap that matters most here

**Unpleasantness is not a diagnosis.** A genuinely selfish, unkind, or difficult person is not automatically narcissistic — and someone shy, avoidant, or prone to occasional self-pity is not automatically anything either. Diagnose the *structure* (self-other model, what's feared, what happens under threat) — never the *severity of how much you dislike them*. This is the psychological equivalent of the political-ideology horseshoe error: judging by how uncomfortable the behavior makes you, instead of by its actual content.

### Words that are not diagnoses

"Toxic," "narc," "sociopath" (not a clinical term — colloquial for antisocial/psychopathic patterns), "gaslighting" applied to any disagreement, "main character energy." These are increasingly used as pure insults. Analytic use and insult use are different activities — the same warning the ideology course gives about "fascist" and "socialist."

**Drill 4.1** — Which pattern, if any, fits best: (a) narcissistic (b) borderline (c) histrionic (d) antisocial/psychopathic (e) not a disorder — just a hard moment

1. Ghosted after two dates, someone spirals into "I always ruin everything, please just tell me what I did," texts eleven times in an hour, then sends one furious message calling the other person evil.
2. A salesman lies fluidly and without visible discomfort about a product's safety record, has done so at every job he's held, and shrugs when confronted: "that's just business."
3. Interrupts every meeting to redirect focus to a personal story, dresses more dramatically than the context calls for, is delighted by any attention and wounded by none.
4. A partner is warm and attentive as long as they're the one being praised, and goes cold and dismissive for days after their partner gets praised for something instead.
5. Someone snapped at a friend after a genuinely awful week at work, apologized the next morning — and it hasn't happened before or since.

**Drill 4.2**
"My ex is a narcissist" is one of the most common sentences in modern breakup conversations. What two questions from Lesson 1 should you ask before treating that sentence as a diagnosis rather than a description of pain?

## Lesson 5 — Manipulation Tactics and Defense Mechanisms in the Wild

A tactic is not a diagnosis. Anyone — narcissistic, borderline, or perfectly healthy under enough stress — can reach for one of these. What matters is naming the move itself, and whether it's a pattern or a moment.

### Defense mechanisms, briefly

Everyone uses defense mechanisms; the question is which ones, how rigidly, and how much reality-distortion they require.

- **Denial** — refusing to register a fact at all.
- **Projection** — attributing your own unacceptable feeling or impulse to someone else ("you're the one who's angry," said by the angry person).
- **Rationalization** — a plausible-sounding but false reason, built after the fact (this is dissonance reduction's usual delivery mechanism — see Lesson 2).
- **Displacement** — redirecting an emotion toward a safer target than its actual cause.
- **Intellectualization** — draining the emotion out of something by discussing it purely abstractly.

None of these, alone, is pathological. Everyone does all five sometimes. The question is always D2: occasional, or the person's only mode?

### Gaslighting

Specifically: a *pattern*, over time, of denying another person's memory, perception, or feelings in order to make them distrust their own judgment — not a single instance of being wrong, and not ordinary disagreement.
**Tell:** repetition + escalation + the functional effect that the other person starts pre-emptively doubting themselves. A single "that's not what happened" is not gaslighting. Being told that, about many different things, for years, until you stop trusting your own memory, is.

### DARVO

**D**eny, **A**ttack, **R**everse **V**ictim and **O**ffender — a specific three-part sequence: deny the act happened, attack the credibility of the person raising it, and reframe the original accuser as the real aggressor. Named and studied by psychologist Jennifer Freyd.
**Tell:** it's the *sequence*, not just defensiveness. All three parts, in that order, inside one exchange, is the signature — not any one part alone.

### Love-bombing → devaluation

Rapid, intense idealization and attention early on — disproportionate to how long or how well the person is actually known — that creates dependency, followed by withdrawal, criticism, or coldness once the attachment is secured.
**Tell:** the *speed and intensity relative to actual depth of knowledge*, and its conditionality. Genuine early enthusiasm in a new relationship is common and not a red flag by itself; the tell is that it functions as an investment called in later.

### The trap: everything becomes manipulation

Most defensiveness in an argument is not a tactic — it's just defensiveness. Most disagreement is not gaslighting. Most early-relationship excitement is not love-bombing. **The discriminator is pattern + function, not a single uncomfortable moment.** Calling every instance of someone being wrong about a shared memory "gaslighting" empties the word of the thing it was built to name — the same erosion the ideology course warns about with "fascist."

**Drill 5.1** — Name the tactic, or say it's not one: (a) gaslighting (b) DARVO (c) love-bombing/devaluation (d) projection (e) not a tactic

1. Confronted with a text message proving he said it, he says "you're twisting my words like you always do" — and by the end of the call, she's the one apologizing.
2. Three days into dating, he's talking about their future together, mirrors all her interests, and says she's "not like anyone else" he's met. Two months later, once she's turned down plans with friends for him twice, the compliments have stopped and the criticism has started.
3. A roommate forgets to pay their share of rent twice, gets called out, and says "I don't know why you're being so aggressive about this."
4. For the third year running, whenever an old disagreement gets brought up, he insists it never happened at all — not "I don't remember it that way," but that it definitely, factually never occurred — until she now checks her phone to confirm her own memories before raising anything with him.
5. Someone who is chronically jealous accuses their partner, unprompted and without evidence, of flirting with a coworker.
6. Caught lying about an expense, someone immediately brings up an unrelated mistake their accuser made months ago, and the conversation ends up being about that instead.
7. A friend cancels plans once, apologizes, and reschedules.

**Drill 5.2** — Faulty claims. What's wrong with each?

1. "He disagreed with my version of events, that's such gaslighting."
2. "She's a total narcissist, she posted a selfie."
3. "That's literally DARVO" — about someone who calmly explained their side after being wrongly accused.
4. "Everyone has narcissistic traits, so the word doesn't mean anything."
5. "My coworker is a sociopath" — based on one blunt email.
6. "He love-bombed me" — about a partner who was consistently affectionate for two years before a difficult breakup.

## Lesson 6 — Mixed Identification Drill

For each scenario: name what's happening, cite the diagnostic questions (D1–D5) that decided it, and name one thing that would falsify your reading. Some of these deserve "insufficient evidence" — say so when it's true. Over-diagnosis is the more common failure with this material, not under-diagnosis.

1. "I know I promised myself I'd leave if it happened again, but this time really is different — he explained why it wasn't his fault."

2. He'd already decided to invest before he asked anyone's opinion. Every "due diligence" conversation after that was really just him listening for agreement, and getting irritated at anyone who raised a concern.

3. The report cites six analysts who back her thesis and dismisses the two who don't as "not understanding the sector" — a phrase she doesn't apply to any of the six, several of whom have less sector experience than the two she dismissed.

4. Three years into a PhD she no longer wants, she tells her advisor she'll finish anyway: "I can't have wasted three years for nothing."

5. For years he assumed the new manufacturing process was more expensive. When the plant finally ran the actual numbers, it was cheaper — he was the first to say so in the meeting, and asked for the analysis to be circulated.

6. Every time she brings up something he said, he tells her it never happened — not "I don't remember it that way," but that she's making it up. It's happened often enough that she now records important conversations, just for herself.

7. Caught having lied on his expense report, he told HR the real problem was that his manager had been targeting him for months — and somehow the meeting ended with HR asking the manager to explain themselves.

8. The first month, he called her his soulmate, learned her whole family's names, and said no one had ever understood him the way she did. By the third month, once she'd stopped seeing her college friends most weekends to be with him, the compliments had turned into comments about how she "used to be more fun."

9. He's never once, in twenty years, said "I was wrong." When a project he championed fails, it's always because the team executed poorly. When it succeeds, he tells the story of his original vision to anyone who'll listen, unprompted.

10. She never asks for anything and never complains out loud. But she's kept a mental ledger for a decade of every time she trained someone who then got promoted over her, and she's quietly stopped speaking to three of them, one by one, without ever telling them why.

11. Any time he seems even slightly less available, she calls him nonstop until he answers, alternates between "I'm sorry, I'm the worst" and "you clearly don't care about me" within the same conversation, and has done this with every partner she's had since her teens.

12. He's warm, funny, and the first to offer help — right up until a favor isn't returned fast enough, at which point he'll casually mention, to mutual friends, something private and embarrassing you told him in confidence months earlier. He doesn't seem angry when he does it. He seems entertained.

13. She cried at her coworker's small mistake in a meeting once. Someone on the team called her "obviously borderline" in the group chat afterward.

14. He explains, calmly and specifically, why the criticism of his proposal is factually wrong, citing the actual numbers. Two people on the thread reply that he's "being defensive" and "can't take feedback."

## Answer Key

### Drill 1.1

1. Reasoning family — a mind justifying itself. The contradiction is between her stated rule and her current choice; watch for this to resurface as dissonance reduction in Lesson 2.
2. Tactics family — a move in an interaction (deny + attack; watch for the reversal that would confirm DARVO in Lesson 5).
3. Pattern family — a stable way someone is: consistent across a decade and multiple contexts.
4. Reasoning family — sunk cost specifically.
5. None of the three. A proportionate reaction to a real external stressor. Not everything needs this course's vocabulary — that's the point of D2.

### Drill 1.2

**D2** separates them fastest. One is a single instance under identifiable stress (situational); the other is a decade-long pattern with a consistent shape and a consistent aftermath (pervasive). Identical surface behavior — denying something hurtful was said — completely different classification once D2 is asked.

### Drill 2.1

1. (a) Dissonance reduction — behavior (eating the sauce) conflicts with stated identity (vegan); resolved by minimizing rather than by changing behavior or belief.
2. (b) Confirmation bias — textbook asymmetric scrutiny: three studies accepted lightly, one dismissed on a rationale (funding) never applied to the other three.
3. (c) Motivated reasoning — the conclusion (promote the friend) preceded the evidence-gathering (the interviews).
4. (d) Sunk cost / escalation of commitment — the stated reason points backward at money already spent, not forward at whether finishing is still worth it.
5. (e) Genuine belief revision — updated by external data, stated as such, no defensive move present.
6. (b) Confirmation bias, edging toward denial — selectively stops tracking the metric that contradicts the "data-driven" self-image while continuing to cite the ones that flatter it. Real cases are often this compound.

### Drill 2.2

Not necessarily — could also be someone processing out loud, or genuinely fine and enthusiastic to explain. What you'd need: whether the explanation was solicited or came entirely unprompted and at an intensity mismatched with "not upset," and whether the same over-explaining shows up whenever this person is mildly bothered (more diagnostic) versus being a one-off. **Falsifier:** asked directly "did that bother you?", a calibrated and proportionate answer — instead of an unprompted monologue — makes dissonance reduction much less likely.

### Drill 3.1

1. (a) Healthy confidence/self-esteem — pride without diminishing others, genuine happiness at someone else's success.
2. (b) Narcissistic traits — pervasive and cross-context (D2 is satisfied: "as long as anyone's known him," "regardless of topic"), but the evidence covers only one criterion (admiration-seeking/self-centering). Empathy, entitlement, and reaction-to-threat aren't shown yet, so a full pattern isn't earned from this alone — pervasive is not, by itself, sufficient.
3. (c) Grandiose narcissistic pattern — the specific shape (private contempt for the organization, confident prediction that the person who "should have" lost will fail) is narcissistic injury: preserving grandiosity by devaluing both the decision and the person who benefited from it.
4. (e) Insufficient evidence, leaning not-a-pattern — first week, high stress, self-corrected with an apology. D2 fails on both counts: not pervasive, not unprovoked.
5. (d) Vulnerable narcissistic pattern — fifteen years, quiet, quietly certain of unrecognized superiority, cooling toward anyone outperforming them. No overt grandiosity, but the same structure turned inward.
6. None of the five — the faulty-claim case. Pride in a personal accomplishment is not narcissism.

### Drill 3.2

No. Boastfulness in a public, performance-context interview is confounded by the format itself (D3: what function does the boastfulness serve here — genuine grandiosity, or the expected register for that stage?). You'd need D2 evidence: does the same pattern show up in low-stakes, private, unobserved contexts, and does it come with the other criteria — lack of empathy, entitlement, exploitation, reaction to criticism — not just public confidence. Public performance is exactly the context most likely to produce a false positive.

### Drill 4.1

1. (b) Borderline pattern — abandonment terror, rapid escalation, and the swing from self-blame to fury at the other person inside one episode is the classic shape. Confirming this fully would still require D2 evidence across more than one instance.
2. (d) Antisocial/psychopathic pattern — fluent, comfortable deceit, repeated across jobs, zero discomfort, explicit instrumental justification ("that's just business").
3. (c) Histrionic pattern — attention-seeking, theatrical presentation, delight at any attention rather than specifically admiration for superiority (contrast with narcissistic).
4. (a) Narcissistic pattern — warmth is explicitly conditional on being the one receiving praise; coldness is triggered specifically by someone else's success, not by any actual wrongdoing. This is the "mirror, not audience" tell from Lesson 4.
5. (e) Not a disorder — a single instance, an identifiable stressor, an apology, no recurrence. D2 fails cleanly.

### Drill 4.2

**D2** (pervasive and stable across the whole relationship and other relationships, or specific to the breakup itself, when everyone looks worse) and **D4** (nearest look-alike — ordinary self-centeredness, a bad match, incompatible needs — versus the actual clinical structure). Breakups generate enormous motivated reasoning in the person doing the describing, too (Lesson 2) — "my ex is a narcissist" is often true, but it's also exactly the sentence dissonance reduction would produce whether or not it's true, which is why it earns the same scrutiny you'd apply to anyone else's claim.

### Drill 5.1

1. (b) DARVO — deny ("twisting my words"), attack (implies she always does this), reverse (she ends up apologizing) — all three, in sequence, in one exchange.
2. (c) Love-bombing → devaluation — speed (three days) and intensity relative to actual knowledge, followed by conditional withdrawal once dependency (declining friend plans) was established.
3. (e) Not a tactic — a single instance of a reasonable person being annoyed at being reasonably confronted; no denial of the underlying fact, no reversal, no repeated pattern.
4. (a) Gaslighting — repeated (three years), escalated to factual denial of the event itself rather than a memory disagreement, and has the functional effect named in the definition: she now checks her phone before trusting her own memory.
5. (d) Projection — the accusation of flirting, made by the jealous party without evidence, reads more as their own feeling or impulse than as a report about the partner.
6. (b) DARVO, partially — deny is implicit (changing the subject rather than addressing it) and attack is present (the unrelated past mistake), but no explicit victim/offender reversal is claimed. The honest call is "attack and deflect," a DARVO-adjacent move missing the full reversal — a genuinely ambiguous boundary case.
7. (e) Not a tactic — a single cancellation with an apology and a reschedule is just normal life.

### Drill 5.2

1. Disagreement about a single fact is not gaslighting; gaslighting requires a pattern that functions to make someone doubt their own judgment generally, not one contested memory.
2. Category error — posting a selfie is not diagnostic of anything. No pervasiveness, no lack-of-empathy evidence, nothing beyond a normal act being read as pathology.
3. DARVO specifically requires denying the act *and* reversing victim and offender. Calmly explaining your side after being wrongly accused is just defending yourself accurately — not every defense is DARVO.
4. True that many people show narcissistic traits sometimes — but that's exactly why the word still needs the pervasive/impairing distinction (D2), not a reason to discard it. "Some people have a fever sometimes" doesn't make "fever" meaningless.
5. One blunt email is a single data point. "Sociopath" isn't even a clinical term, and its real-world analogue (an antisocial pattern, or psychopathy) needs a pervasive disregard for others' rights, not one terse message.
6. Sustained, consistent affection for two years is not love-bombing — love-bombing is specifically disproportionate to actual relationship depth, and functions to secure dependency fast. Two years of consistency is closer to the opposite: evidence of genuine investment.

### Drill 6 — Mixed Identification

1. **Dissonance reduction.** D1: a mind justifying itself. D3: the contradiction between her prior resolution and her current inaction is resolved by exempting this instance, not by changing behavior. *Falsifier:* if "this time is different" is true by some independent, checkable criterion — not just his explanation — this stops being dissonance reduction and becomes an accurate update.

2. **Motivated reasoning.** The decision-before-reasoning timing is explicit in the text itself — unusually clean. *Falsifier:* if he'd genuinely have walked away given strong enough disconfirming evidence, and can name what that evidence would have been, this is closer to ordinary — if biased — diligence.

3. **Confirmation bias.** Unequal scrutiny, applied by a stated criterion (sector experience) that, checked, doesn't actually track who got dismissed. *Falsifier:* if the two dismissed analysts had some other real, specific, checkable methodological flaw, the dismissal could be earned rather than biased.

4. **Sunk cost / escalation of commitment.** The stated reason points backward at time already spent, not forward at whether two more years is worth it. *Falsifier:* if finishing genuinely unlocks something forward-looking she independently wants — not just avoiding the feeling of waste — continuing could be a reasoned choice.

5. **Genuine belief revision — not a bias.** Evidence arrived, belief updated, no defensive move, no resistance. *Falsifier:* if he'd been quietly blocking that analysis from being run for years beforehand, this would look more like reluctant capitulation than genuine openness.

6. **Gaslighting.** Repeated, escalated to flat factual denial (not memory disagreement), and the functional effect — she no longer trusts her memory enough to skip recording — is the definitional tell. *Falsifier:* a documented memory condition, or this having happened exactly once, would substantially weaken the "pattern" claim.

7. **DARVO.** Deny is implicit in the reframe, attack is present (accusing the manager of targeting him), and the reversal (HR investigating the manager) completes the sequence. *Falsifier:* if the manager actually had a documented history of unfair treatment before this incident, the "attack" would be a legitimate grievance rather than a reversal tactic.

8. **Love-bombing → devaluation.** Speed and intensity of idealization relative to actual time known, followed by conditional withdrawal once dependency (reduced outside friendships) was in place. *Falsifier:* if the reduced friend time was mutual and voluntary, and the "used to be more fun" comment was an isolated remark rather than the start of a pattern, this reads as an ordinary rocky patch instead.

9. **Grandiose narcissistic pattern.** Pervasive (twenty years), cross-context, entitlement to credit without accountability for failure, admiration-seeking (unprompted retelling). *Falsifier:* real, private accountability that simply never surfaces publicly would point toward image management rather than a genuine empathy/entitlement deficit — worth downgrading toward "traits."

10. **Vulnerable narcissistic pattern.** No overt grandiosity or demand, but a decade-long, cross-relationship pattern organized around unrecognized specialness, expressed as silent withdrawal rather than confrontation. *Falsifier:* if she has, in fact, raised these concerns directly and been repeatedly and genuinely dismissed by a dysfunctional workplace, this could be a proportionate response to real unfairness rather than a narcissistic pattern.

11. **Borderline pattern.** Abandonment-driven, splitting ("worst" / "don't care about me" in the same breath), pervasive across relationships and time. *Falsifier:* if this intensity were new and followed one specific, recent, identifiable trauma — rather than "every partner since her teens" — it might be a situational reaction rather than a stable pattern. That detail is what pushes this into pattern territory; without it, the item would belong with 13 and 14.

12. **Antisocial pattern, leaning toward the psychopathic end.** Instrumental warmth (functions to extract things, not genuine connection), a breach of confidence used as casual leverage, and — the key tell — no emotional charge behind the betrayal (entertained, not angry), which argues against ordinary grudge or narcissistic-injury reactions. *Falsifier:* real, consistent remorse afterward, and a one-time lapse rather than a repeated move, would drop this out of "pattern" territory.

13. **Insufficient evidence.** One emotional reaction in one meeting establishes nothing — not pervasiveness (D2), not the defining feature of borderline (abandonment-driven instability across relationships), nothing. The correct move is to refuse the label, not to guess.

14. **Insufficient evidence — and the mirror-image trap of 13.** Calm, specific, evidence-based disagreement is being mislabeled as a "defensiveness pattern" by people who dislike being contradicted. Nothing here indicates a reasoning distortion, a tactic, or a personality pattern — it indicates someone correctly defending an accurate position. This is the direct analogue of the ideology course's horseshoe error: mistaking the discomfort of being disagreed with for evidence of a disorder in the other person.

## Scoring your own reasoning

Grade the process, not just the final label:

- Did you reach for **D1 and D2 first**? Naming a pattern before checking whether it's even pervasive is a fail, even if the label turns out to be right.
- Did you separate **the tactic from the person**? A single gaslighting-shaped moment doesn't prove a narcissistic pattern, and a narcissistic pattern doesn't require every tactic in Lesson 5 to be present.
- Did you **withhold classification** when the evidence was thin? (Items 13 and 14 are the test of this.) Over-diagnosis is the more common failure in casual use of this material, not under-diagnosis.
- On any item involving someone you'd already formed an opinion about, did you check whether *your own* motivated reasoning (Lesson 2) was doing some of the classifying for you?
- Did you check the label against the actual criteria, not against how unpleasant the person's behavior made you feel? Severity of your discomfort is not diagnostic.

## Honest caveats

- **This is not a diagnostic tool, and it does not make you one.** Real diagnosis of any personality disorder requires a licensed clinician, a structured clinical interview, collateral history, and — critically — ruling out other explanations (medical conditions, substance use, an acute stressor, trauma responses that can mimic personality pathology). Nothing in this course substitutes for that.
- **Personality disorder criteria require pervasiveness, early onset, and functional impairment.** A handful of matching traits is not a disorder. Most people show narcissistic, histrionic, borderline, or antisocial traits occasionally without meeting criteria for anything. Treat the marker lists as family resemblances, the way the ideology course treats fascism's markers — not a checklist where any three ticked boxes equal a diagnosis.
- **The categories themselves are contested even among clinicians.** The DSM-5's own alternative model (Section III) treats personality pathology as dimensional — a matter of degree — rather than the categorical yes/no boxes used here for teachability. Comorbidity and diagnostic overlap between Cluster B disorders is common, and trained clinicians disagree on borderline cases regularly.
- **These words are also weapons.** "Narcissist," "gaslighting," "toxic," and "sociopath" are used constantly online to condemn rather than describe. Analytic use (this course) and rhetorical use (an insult) are different activities — keep them separate in your own speech, the same way the ideology course asks you to separate analytic and rhetorical uses of "fascist."
- **Diagnosing at a distance is unreliable.** Public figures, exes, coworkers you've never had a clinical conversation with — pattern judgments made from the outside, without access to someone's private life, internal experience, or history, are guesses wearing clinical language. Confidence should scale down accordingly, not up.
- **Watch your own motivated reasoning while using this course.** It's very easy to open a course like this already looking for confirmation of a suspicion you'd already formed about a specific person (Lesson 2 — this applies to you too). Take the "insufficient evidence" option seriously, especially about people you already have strong feelings about.
- **Cultural and contextual variation is real.** What reads as appropriate confidence, emotional expressiveness, or assertiveness varies significantly by culture, profession, and context. Don't let this course turn cultural difference into pathology.
- **None of this replaces professional help** — for yourself, if any of this course resonated uncomfortably with your own patterns, or for a relationship that seems to match Lesson 5's tactics. A therapist, not a self-administered field guide, is the right next step for either.
