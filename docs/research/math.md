# Basic Math: how numerate adults reason with everyday numbers (research for the build-subject skill, step 2)

Written 2026-10-22 for the subject `math` (field Numbers & Money). Finding ids M1 to M24. This note replaces, for the
plan, the older `learning-everyday-math.md` (2026-10-10); findings taken over from it are re-checked where the source
could be reached and say so, and are marked "not re-read" where it could not.

**Strength words.** *Strong*: many studies or a meta-analysis agreeing, or a panel rating of strong. *Moderate*: a few
good studies or a panel rating of moderate. *Limited*: one study, school children only, or a source read only as an
abstract or a secondary account. *Practice*: a professional framework or rule, which says what practitioners do, not
that teaching it works. **Read** says what was actually opened.

## Part 1. How skilled adults handle everyday numbers

**M1. Numeracy is managing a real situation, not doing a school exercise.** The OECD's adult-skills framework defines
numeracy as "the ability to access, use, interpret, and communicate mathematical information and ideas, in order to
engage in and manage the mathematical demands of a range of situations in adult life", and numerate behavior as
"managing a situation or solving a problem in a real context, by responding to mathematical content/information/ideas
represented in multiple ways". The responses it lists are: identify or locate; act upon ("order, count, estimate,
compute, measure, model"); interpret; evaluate; communicate (Table 1). The hard part for adults is the translation: the
top complexity level is where "numbers needed for calculations may need to be derived or estimated" and "considerable
translation is required" from the situation to the operation (Appendix, "Translation (Problem Transparency)").
*Source:* PIAAC Numeracy Expert Group (2009), *PIAAC Numeracy: A Conceptual Framework*, OECD Education Working Paper 35,
paras 55 and 57, Table 1 (pp. 21–22), Appendix complexity factors (p. 45). *Read:* full text of those sections.
*Strength:* practice (the framework behind the international adult survey). **Decides:** every problem starts from a
real document (receipt, tag, offer, room) and the learner has to find which numbers matter and what to do with them;
the method is never named in the problem.

**M2. Numerate adults judge how exact to be and which tool to use.** "Contextual judgment comes into play when
deciding how precise one should be or which tool (calculator, mental math, a computer) to use" (para on Quantity and
Number, p. 27). Workplace studies list "skills in both fast and accurate computations but also estimation, and knowing
when each skill is required and why" and "ability to deal with proportions and percents" as key (para 27, p. 13).
Conceptual understanding "can help learners produce reasonable estimates that can help them catch computational
errors, or realise that an exact product is not necessary" (para 73, p. 29). *Source, read, strength:* as M1.
**Decides:** two answers per problem, a rough one in the head and an exact one on the calculator, and the habit of
comparing them.

**M3. Adults use their own shortcuts and benchmark numbers.** "Adults are known to use a lot of invented strategies";
"complex numbers may be broken down in ways that ease mental load"; benchmark values "such as 1/2, 1/4, 50%, or 25%,
are familiar to many people" and are easier for adults than a school order of difficulty predicts (para 7A, p. 46).
*Source, read, strength:* as M1. **Decides:** percents and fractions are taught from the benchmarks (half, a quarter,
10%) out, and the estimate uses friendly numbers, not a fixed rounding rule.

**M4. Good estimators change the numbers, change the problem, then adjust.** Reys, Rybolt, Bestgen and Wyatt (1982)
tested over 1,200 students in grades 7 to 12 and some adults with a 55-item estimation test, then interviewed the 59 best
estimators. They found three processes: *reformulation*, "changing numerical data and leaving the problem's structure
intact" (rounding, front-end digits, compatible numbers); *translation*, changing the structure (83 + 74 + 96 + 82
becomes about 4 × 80); and *compensation*, "making final numerical adjustments to the results" (47 × 66: rounded up to
50 × 70 = 3,500, then lowered because both numbers went up). "The same strategy is not adequate for all problems, and
an important characteristic of a good estimator is knowing how to select and use the adequate strategies in each
problem" (Reys 1986; Flores, Reys & Reys 1990). In a replication with the best 5% of 248 Mexican secondary students, the
good estimators "always tried to replace the original data with numbers easier to handle" and most "made final
adjustments"; they said they had learned to estimate outside school. *Source:* Reys et al. (1982), *JRME* 13(3),
183–201, through Cortés Flores, Backhoff & Organista (2005), *Revista Mexicana de Investigación Educativa* 10(25),
pp. 2–4 and 9 (English edition). *Read:* full text of the 2005 paper; the 1982 original only through it (not reachable).
*Strength:* limited to moderate (interview studies of what good estimators do; widely replicated description, no
effect size). **Decides:** the estimate lesson teaches three moves by name in plain words: make the numbers friendly,
make the sum simpler, then nudge the answer up or down.

**M5. Skilled mental calculators use the numbers' properties; unskilled ones run the paper method in their head.**
Hope's study of 15 skilled and 15 unskilled senior secondary students (from 280) found most unskilled students "relied
on strategies better suited to written calculation", while the proficient ones used "strategies based upon properties
suggested by the factors" (Hope 1987, UBC dissertation; Hope & Sherrill 1987, *JRME* 18). *Read:* a search summary of
the dissertation record (the repository blocked access). *Strength:* limited. **Supports** M4: the estimate is taught as
picking easy numbers for this problem, not as column arithmetic done mentally.

**M6. Problem solvers first ask what is unknown and what is given, plan from a familiar problem, and look back.**
Pólya's four phases: "First, we have to understand the problem; we have to see clearly what is required. Second, we
have to see how the various items are connected, how the unknown is linked to the data ... to make a plan. Third, we
carry out our plan. Fourth, we look back at the completed solution" (Part I, sec. 6, pp. 5–6). "The worst may happen if
the student embarks upon computations ... without having understood the problem" (sec. 6). The questions not to skip:
"What is the unknown? What are the data? What is the condition?"; "draw a figure and point out on it the unknown and the
data" (sec. 8, p. 7). For a plan: "Look at the unknown! And try to think of a familiar problem having the same or a
similar unknown" (sec. 9, "Devising a plan", pp. 8–10). Looking back: students who skip it "miss an important and instructive phase"; "Can you check
the result?"; "Did you use all the data?" (secs. 13–14, pp. 14–16). *Source:* G. Pólya, *How to Solve It*, 2nd ed.
(Princeton, 1957). *Read:* full text (Internet Archive scan), Part I secs. 6–14. *Strength:* practice (the classic
account by a working mathematician; its teaching value is tested in M15). **Decides:** the way of thinking opens with
"what is asked, what is given" and closes with "does it fit the rough answer, the units and the direction"; each kind of
problem is introduced by its unknown ("a price per one", "the price before").

**M7. The deciding distinction is multiplicative against additive, and novices get it wrong both ways.** In 755
students from grade 4 to 10 solving missing-value word problems, using additive methods on proportional problems rose
through primary school and then fell, while using proportional methods on additive (non-proportional) problems grew
across both levels; whether the numbers made whole-number ratios had a large effect on which method was chosen
(Fernández, Llinares, Van Dooren, De Bock & Verschaffel, 2010, working paper of the study published in *Eur. J. Psychol.
Educ.* 2012). Secondary students strongly tend to apply the linear model where it does not hold, the "illusion of
linearity": many believe that doubling the lengths of a figure doubles its area, and the belief often survives teaching
(De Bock, Verschaffel & Janssens 1998, *Educ. Stud. Math.* 35, 65–85; De Bock, Van Dooren, Janssens & Verschaffel, *The
Illusion of Linearity*, 2008). Lamon describes the core of proportional reasoning as unitizing (chunking a quantity into
convenient pieces, as a "per one" or "per pack") and *relative* thinking, analyzing change "in both absolute and relative
terms" (Lamon 2005, *Teaching Fractions and Ratios for Understanding*, p. 30). *Read:* abstracts and secondary accounts
only (the papers and the book are behind logins). *Strength:* moderate for the two errors (a consistent research
program, children and teenagers); limited for Lamon's wording. **Decides:** rates and percents are taught as "per one"
and "times a multiplier"; a lesson sets proportional and non-proportional look-alikes side by side (a fixed fee plus a
rate, area when length doubles, cooking time for twice the food); the named slips include the additive one ("you added
the difference instead of scaling").

**M8. What adult numeracy contains.** PIAAC's content areas are quantity and number (including "fractions, decimals and
percents as expressions of greater precision, parts or comparisons"); dimension and shape (lengths, perimeters, areas,
"a sense of 'benchmarks' and estimation"); pattern, relationships and change (proportional reasoning, formulas; "more
demanding tasks ... using formulae such as for calculating compound interest"); data and chance (pp. 27–28). Its
examples: the cost of one can given 4 for $2.00; cheese at 12.95 per kg for 0.283 kg (p. 27); "work out discounts as
fractions and percentages", from the UK adult numeracy curriculum it cites (para 35, p. 15). Primes, common factors, irrational numbers, quadratic equations and trigonometry
are not named among adult numeracy content. *Source, read, strength:* as M1. **Decides:** the core of the subject is
rates, percents, measurement and growth; the school topics the owner asked for (primes, quadratics, trigonometry,
counting formulas) are kept but placed last and taught through the everyday problem each one solves, with the owner
asked whether to keep the parts that have none (see the plan).

**M9. Compound growth is underestimated by intuition; adults treat it as straight-line.** "Exponential-growth bias
(EGB) is the tendency for individuals to partially neglect compounding"; in a US-representative incentivized sample
"approximately one-third of subjects" were estimated as fully biased; the bias goes with lower savings and "does not
respond to a simple graphical intervention" (Levy & Tasoff 2016, *J. Eur. Econ. Assoc.*, abstract and introduction).
Stango and Zinman (2009, *J. Finance* 64, 2807–2849) define it as "the pervasive tendency to linearize exponential
functions" and show it explains underestimating a loan's rate from its other terms and a savings plan's future value.
*Read:* full abstract and introduction of Levy & Tasoff (author copy); Stango & Zinman as an abstract via a search
listing. *Strength:* moderate (two large-sample studies agreeing). **Decides:** money over time is worked by the
multiplier on the calculator (× 1.05 each year, or the power key), never by feel; the named slip is "simple where it
compounds"; the rule of 72 is taught only as a rough check, and a growth problem's estimate is checked against the
calculator, not the other way round.

**M10. Chances are reasoned best as counts of people, not as percents.** With the same problems in a frequency format
("of 1,000 people, 10 have ..."), naive adults reached the Bayesian answer by a correct route in 46% and 50% of answers,
against 16% and 28% with probabilities (Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995, *Psych. Rev.* 102, 684–704, p. 693, Study 1).
*Read:* full text of the results section. *Strength:* strong (widely replicated). **Decides:** the chance lesson the
owner asked for works every problem as a count out of 1,000 people on the app's `people` figure, never with the
probability formula.

**M11. Everyday experts keep the quantities meaningful.** Brazilian child street vendors who calculated correctly in
the market did worse on the same sums written as school problems; "context-embedded problems were much more easily
solved than ones without a context" (Carraher, Carraher & Schliemann 1985, *Br. J. Dev. Psychol.* 3, 21–29; followed
up in Nunes, Schliemann & Carraher 1993, *Street Mathematics and School Mathematics*). *Read:* abstract via secondary
sources. *Strength:* limited (children, one setting). **Supports** M1: the working shown in feedback keeps the units
and the things ("$4.20 per pound × 3 pounds") rather than bare numbers.

**M12. Trades use a fixed ratio rule for a ladder, not a triangle formula.** The US construction standard: a ladder
leaning on a wall is set "at an angle such that the horizontal distance from the top support to the foot of the
ladder is approximately one-quarter of the working length of the ladder" (29 CFR 1926.1053(b)(5)(i)), and its rails
reach "at least 3 feet (.9 m) above the upper landing surface" (1926.1053(b)(1)). *Read:* the regulation text (OSHA
site). *Strength:* practice. **Decides:** the right-triangle lesson the owner asked for ("the ladder against a wall")
starts from the 1-in-4 rule, then uses the square rule (a² + b² = c²) to find how high it reaches or how long a ladder
is needed; the triangle rule is the check, the trade rule is the habit.

## Part 2. What the learning research says about this kind of skill

The skill is mainly a procedure applied to new numbers, with one judging step inside it: what kind of problem this is,
so which set-up applies (`docs/learning-science.md` section 2, procedure units; P10, P12, P17, P22).

**M13. Worked examples first, alternated with problems, fewer as skill grows.** "Interleave worked example solutions and
problem-solving exercises", reducing examples as expertise grows; level of evidence moderate; in algebra, 8th and 9th
graders alternating four example–problem pairs took less time on eight problems and did better on the post-test than
those solving eight problems (Pashler et al. 2007, IES practice guide, Recommendation 2, pp. 9–10). *Read:* full text of
Recommendation 2 (re-read 2026-10-22). The 2023 meta-analysis (Barbieri et al., g about 0.48 across 55 maths studies) was
read as an abstract only by the older note. *Strength:* moderate to strong. **Decides:** each kind opens with one worked
example before any problem of that kind.

**M14. Fade the worked steps, and ask the reason of one.** Removing worked steps one at a time, with prompts to name the
principle of a step, gave medium to large near and far transfer gains (Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill 2003, *J. Educ.
Psych.* 95, 774–783). *Read:* abstract only (older note, not re-read). *Strength:* limited; a judgement call for the
reason prompt (the 2023 meta-analysis found added self-explanation prompts did not help on average). **Decides:** a set
with `leave` (last step, then last two) between the worked example and whole problems.

**M15. Teach students to monitor and look back, to draw the problem, and to compare methods.** An IES panel on problem
solving (grades 4–8) rated *strong* evidence for "assist students in monitoring and reflecting on the problem-solving
process" with a list of prompts, and for "teach students how to use visual representations": "Students who learn to
visually represent the mathematical information in problems prior to writing an equation are more effective at problem
solving"; representations named include "tables, graphs, number lines, and diagrams such as strip diagrams, percent
bars". It rated *moderate* evidence for teaching and comparing multiple strategies in worked examples, and for asking
students to explain each step of a worked example (Woodward et al. 2012, IES practice guide NCEE 2012-4055,
Recommendations 2–5, pp. 1, 17, 23, 32). *Read:* full text of the recommendation summaries and evidence levels.
*Strength:* strong (2, 3), moderate (4, 5); school children. **Decides:** one picture per kind (two-row rate table,
100% bar, labeled plan, year table) filled in with the problem's numbers in the worked example and the feedback; the
"look back" step (compare with the rough answer, units, bigger or smaller) is part of every worked example; where two
methods exist (a percent by benchmarks or by the multiplier) the worked example shows both side by side.

**M16. Teach the problem's structure, not key words.** Schema-based word-problem teaching (name the kind from its
structure, fill a fixed diagram, write the number sentence) beat ordinary teaching for 1,411 seventh graders on ratio,
percent, tax, discount, tip and interest problems (Jitendra et al. 2019; WWC meets standards without reservations), and
for at-risk first graders, g = 1.08, with reading the whole problem instead of hunting key words adding g = 0.47 (Fuchs et
al. 2020, *J. Educ. Psych.* 113, 86–103). *Read:* the older note read Fuchs et al. in full on 2026-10-12 and Jitendra
through the WWC page; not re-read here (PMC blocked the fetch). *Strength:* moderate; children only. **Decides:** as
M15, and problems are written so that key words mislead ("off" in a price that went up).

**M17. Mix the kinds once each has been taught alone.** Mixed practice beat blocked on a test a month later, 61% to 38%,
d = 0.83, 787 seventh graders (Rohrer et al. 2020); introduce a type alone first and give feedback. *Read:* the older
note read it in full; not re-read here; also R9 in `review-after-lessons.md`, P22. *Strength:* strong for maths method
choice. **Decides:** from the second lesson of a unit on, sets carry `mix` from earlier lessons, unlabeled.

**M18. The answer is typed, as in life.** In real life "adults have flexibility in how they choose to respond", "many
real life tasks call for approximate answers or estimates", and forced-choice items have "limited ability ... to
reflect reasoning" (PIAAC 2009, on response formats, p. 32). With answer options, students solved algebra items by
checking each option instead of setting the problem up (2016 study in *Educ. Stud. Math.*, read as a summary by the older
note). *Read:* the PIAAC passage in full. *Strength:* practice plus limited. **Decides:** the `number` ask, estimate
first; choices only for verdicts ("is the total right?", "proportional or not?").

**M19. Feedback shows the working and names the slip.** Elaborated feedback beats right/wrong (Wisniewski, Zierer &
Hattie 2020; Van der Kleij et al. 2015), via `learning-science.md` P19–P21. *Strength:* strong. **Decides:** every
problem has `steps` and `traps` with a named slip for each known wrong path.

**M20. A calculator does the exact arithmetic.** Across 54 studies calculator use kept or improved operational and
problem-solving skills (Ellington 2003, *JRME* 34, 433–463; abstract, read by the older note). *Strength:* moderate.
With M2: the exact answer is the calculator's, the estimate is the head's.

**M21. Judging whether an answer is sensible is a separate skill, often missing.** Most of 200 eighth graders could not
recognize reasonable answers and used procedural strategies (Alajmi & Reys 2010); adults did better than fifth graders,
mostly at judging whether an answer is practical (2022 study, 160 participants). A review finds poor estimation in
children and adults "is susceptible to interventions", particularly when they address the misconception that estimation
is for calculating exact answers (Andrews et al. 2021, *J. Math. Educ.* 14(1), 6–27, abstract). *Read:* abstracts and
summaries. *Strength:* limited. **Decides:** the first lesson names that wrong idea ("a rough answer has to be exact")
and the estimate is scored by a band, not exactly.

**M22. Space the kinds over days as new problems.** R1–R4 and R8 in `review-after-lessons.md`: recall on later days, with
the right answers of the review being those practice taught. *Strength:* strong for spacing in general; for procedures,
relearning gave a small benefit in the one set of experiments on probability problems (learning-science 4.3). **Decides:**
the review asks a fresh problem of each strand, made by its generator, estimate first.

**M23. Adult numeracy teaching research is thin.** About 30% of US adults have difficulty with whole-number and percent
tasks (PIAAC, NCES 2020); reviews of adult numeracy teaching find few rigorous studies, and favour explicit teaching in
meaningful everyday contexts (older note M9, summaries only). *Strength:* limited. **So** M13–M17 are borrowed from
school research; the owner's own try is the real test.

**M24. Algebra and the square: what the evidence says about the owner's school topics.** No source read places
quadratic equations, systems of equations, prime factorization, irrational numbers, logarithms or trigonometry among the
mathematics adults meet in daily life (M8 lists adult content; PIAAC puts "complex formulae, equations or functions" only
at its top complexity level, p. 45). Algebra is, however, where the worked-example evidence is strongest (M13: the IES
example is algebra). *Strength:* the absence is a judgement call from M8. **Decides:** these topics are kept as the
owner asked, taught through the everyday problem each one solves where one exists (two unknowns: when two price plans
cost the same; a square side from an area: completing the square drawn as a square with strips), and listed for the
owner to keep or drop where none exists.

## The way of thinking, in plain words (what the subject teaches)

1. **Say what is asked and what is given** (M6, M1): the number the situation needs, its unit, and the figures that
   matter; ignore the rest.
2. **Rough answer first, from friendly numbers** (M4, M3, M2, M21): make the numbers easy, make the sum simple, nudge.
3. **Find the kind by its structure, and draw it** (M7, M15, M16): per one (a rate), a part of 100 (a percent), a space
   (area, volume), a multiplier each year (growth); proportional or not.
4. **Work it exactly with the calculator** (M20, M2, M9): set the multiplier or the per-one value, then compute.
5. **Look back** (M6, M15): does the exact answer sit near the rough one, in the right unit, the right direction (bigger
   or smaller than what you started with), with all the data used?

## What the evidence does not settle

- Whether these school-age findings (M13–M17) hold for one adult on a phone (M23).
- Whether naming Reys's three estimation moves helps a beginner more than practice alone (M4 describes experts; no trial
  of teaching them was read).
- Whether the "illusion of linearity" lesson (M7) changes behavior beyond the lesson; the belief often survives teaching.
