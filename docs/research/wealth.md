# Wealth Preservation: how fiduciary planners and financial economists reason (research, 2026-10-22)

Step 2 of the build-subject skill for Wealth (`wealth`, field Numbers & Money). The question: when a careful planner or a
financial economist looks at a household's money, what do they look at first, what distinctions do they use, what do they
change and what do they leave, and what mistakes do non-experts make that they do not? The learner is a Canadian now living in
the US, so the accounts and rules are US ones (401(k), IRA, HSA, taxable brokerage, FDIC and SIPC) and the cross-border flags
are Canada to US.

**How this was read.** Sources were downloaded and read in their own text where the site allowed it (curl, then the text
extracted); several sites (Bogleheads, Advisor Perspectives, SSRN, JSTOR, Morningstar's research pages) refused every request,
and for those a search summary is all that was seen, which the "Read" line says. "Strength" rates how well the passage supports
the use made of it, not the source's general standing. A choice with no passage found is marked **judgement call**.
The old note `docs/research/acting-on-your-own-money.md` (F1 to F11, 2026-10-10) is reused below only where it is cited by its
id; its own "Read" levels stand, and W20 re-reads one of its sources (Choi, Laibson & Madrian) in full.

Sources live here only, never in a lesson.

## A. The expert's process: see everything, then compare the current course with the alternatives, then monitor

**W1. The fiduciary planner's process has seven steps, and step 1 gathers the whole picture, including "capacity for risk".**
CFP Board, *Code of Ethics and Standards of Conduct* (in force since 2019), Section 3, "Practice Standards for the Financial
Planning Process". *Read: full text of the web edition, cfp.net, 2026-10-22.* Strength: strong as a description of what the
profession requires of its own practitioners; it is a professional standard, not an outcome study.
The seven steps: "1. Understanding the Client's Personal and Financial Circumstances; 2. Identifying and Selecting Goals; 3.
Analyzing the Client's Current Course of Action and Potential Alternative Course(s) of Action; 4. Developing the Financial
Planning Recommendation(s); 5. Presenting ...; 6. Implementing ...; 7. Monitoring Progress and Updating." Step 1 lists the
quantitative information to gather: "the Client's age, dependents, ... income, expenses, cash flow, savings, assets,
liabilities, available resources, liquidity, taxes, employee benefits, government benefits, insurance coverage, estate plans,
education and retirement accounts and benefits, and capacity for risk"; the qualitative side includes "risk tolerance, goals,
needs, priorities, and current course of action". Section 2.1: financial planning "integrates relevant elements of the Client's
personal and financial circumstances".
*Use:* the subject's first move is the list of everything (accounts, liquidity, insurance, estate papers), before any change.
Liquidity, insurance and estate plans are on the profession's own list, so they belong in the subject, not outside it.

**W2. "The recommendation may be to continue the Client's current course of action."** Same source, Practice Standard 4
("Developing the Financial Planning Recommendation(s)") and 3.a: the professional "must analyze the Client's current course of
action, including the material advantages and disadvantages of the current course and whether the current course maximizes the
potential for meeting the Client's goals", and where appropriate "consider and analyze one or more potential alternative courses
of action". *Read: full text.* Strength: strong (as a professional norm).
*Use:* every decision in the subject is the current course compared with a named alternative, and "leave it" is a legitimate
result the expert reaches on purpose. This is the field's own reason for the leave-alone items (it agrees with P26, which is
a learning reason).

**W3. Monitoring is a step of its own, at set intervals, with fresh information.** Same source, Practice Standard 7: a
professional with monitoring duties "must analyze, at appropriate intervals, the progress toward achieving the Client's goals"
and "obtain current qualitative and quantitative information". *Read: full text.* Strength: strong as norm; the interval is not
fixed by the standard (the yearly cadence is a **judgement call**, common practice, and it matches W15's annual monitoring).
*Use:* the subject ends in a repeated check, not a one-time fix.

**W4. Professionals are not automatically experts: many advisors invest badly in their own accounts.** Linnainmaa, Melzer &
Previtero, "The Misguided Beliefs of Financial Advisors" (working paper, April 2016; later *Journal of Finance* 2021). Canadian
data, over 4,000 advisors. *Read: the April 2016 working paper's abstract and introduction, full text PDF.* Strength: moderate
(one large data set, one country, the learner's own). Abstract: "most advisors invest their personal portfolios just like they
advise their clients. They trade frequently, chase returns, and prefer expensive, actively managed funds over cheap index
funds"; "their own performance would actually improve if they held exact copies of their clients' portfolios"; "many advisors
offer well-meaning, but misguided, recommendations rather than self-serving ones".
*Use:* the "expert" this subject copies is the reasoning that the evidence supports (costs, diversification, process), not
whatever a person called an advisor does. A lesson may show a salesperson's or advisor's pitch as a case to judge.

**W5. No study of how expert planners think, step by step, was found.** Searches for expert-novice or think-aloud studies of
financial planners found none. The nearest: a verbal-protocol study of 90 people making a mortgage decision, in which more
knowledgeable people "made more problem framing statements and used more compensatory decision rules", and novices more often
misunderstood the information they were given (European Journal of Marketing 1998, DOI 10.1108/03090569810216181). *Read: search
summary only; the publisher page refused.* Strength: limited. **Gap:** the way of thinking below rests on the profession's
standard (W1 to W3) and on the economics of what the expert is weighing (W6 onward), not on observed expert reasoning.

## B. Costs are certain; returns are not

**W6. After costs, the average actively managed dollar must do worse than the average passive dollar.** Sharpe, "The
Arithmetic of Active Management", *Financial Analysts Journal*, Jan/Feb 1991. *Read: full text, Sharpe's Stanford page.*
Strength: strong (an identity, not an estimate). "A passive investor always holds every security from the market, with each
represented in the same manner as in the market." "(1) before costs, the return on the average actively managed dollar will
equal the return on the average passively managed dollar and (2) after costs, the return on the average actively managed dollar
will be less than the return on the average passively managed dollar."
*Use:* the cost is the one part of the return the learner controls with certainty.

**W7. Bogle's first rule: gross return minus costs equals net return.** Bogle, "The U.S. Financial Sector and the Relentless
Rules of Humble Arithmetic", remarks at New York University, 14 November 2007 (the same argument as his 2005 *FAJ* article,
which was not reachable). *Read: full text of the remarks, Bogle Center PDF.* Strength: strong as statement of the argument
(it restates W6). "Gross return in the financial markets, minus the costs of financial intermediation, equals the net return
that we investors share."

**W8. Most active funds trail their index over long periods, and survivorship hides how many.** S&P Dow Jones Indices, *SPIVA
U.S. Scorecard, Year-End 2024*. *Read: full text PDF (a copy hosted by an adviser; S&P's own site refused), Report 1a p. 10 and
Report 2.* Strength: strong (counts the whole starting set). All Large-Cap funds that trailed the S&P 500: 65.24% over 1 year,
84.34% over 10, 89.50% over 15, 91.99% over 20. Of the large-cap funds alive 20 years earlier, 32.95% survived. Method: "Many
funds might be liquidated or merged during a period of study. However, for someone making an investment decision at the
beginning of the period, these funds are part of the opportunity set."

**W9. The fee is the most reliable predictor of a fund's future success.** Kinnel, *The Predictive Power of Fees* (Morningstar,
2016). *Read: trade-press and Morningstar repost summaries only.* Strength: moderate. Reported: in US equity, the cheapest fee
quintile had a 62% success rate against 20% for the priciest; similar gaps for bond funds (59% vs 17%).

**W10. A 1% fee against a 0.25% fee costs about $29,000 on $100,000 over 20 years.** SEC Office of Investor Education,
"Understanding Fees" (investor.gov). *Read: full page text.* Strength: strong as arithmetic. "$100,000 investment that grows 4%
annually over 20 years": with a yearly fee of 0.25% "approximately $208,000"; 0.50% "approximately $198,000"; 1.00%
"approximately $179,000".
*Use:* yearly costs are turned into dollars (the pilot's rule), and the long-run picture shows why the expert cares about a
fraction of a percent.

**W11. Low-cost index funds are the efficient-markets economist's answer.** Malkiel, "The Efficient Market Hypothesis and Its
Critics", *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 17(1), 2003, pp. 59-82. *Read: full text.* Strength: moderate (a review by an
advocate). p. 76: "Perhaps the more important puzzle today is why so many investors buy high-expense, actively managed mutual
funds instead of low-cost index funds." His section on professional managers reports most large-cap funds outperformed by the
index and that survivorship bias in the data "was substantial".

**W12. Non-experts ignore fees even between identical funds.** Choi, Laibson & Madrian (2010), from the old note F5. *Read: as
F5 states (abstract).* Strength: moderate. *Use:* the novice error is choosing on past return or name; the expert compares costs
within the same kind of fund.

## C. One thing that could wipe it out: concentration

**W13. Most single stocks do worse than Treasury bills over their life; a few stocks carry the market.** Bessembinder, "Do
Stocks Outperform Treasury Bills?", *Journal of Financial Economics* 2018. *Read: search-returned abstract wording only (SSRN
refused); ASU's page confirms "just 86 stocks have accounted for $16 trillion in wealth creation, half of the stock market
total".* Strength: moderate (the finding is well known; I did not read the paper). Abstract as returned: "slightly more than
four out of every seven common stocks ... have lifetime buy-and-hold returns ... less than those on one-month Treasuries", and
"the best-performing four percent of listed companies" account for the net gain.
*Use:* one stock is a bet that the average stock loses; a fund of the whole market holds the few winners by construction.

**W14. Holding your employer's stock costs about 42% of its value in risk you are not paid for.** Meulbroek, "Company Stock
in Pension Plans: How Costly Is It?", Pensions Institute working paper PI-0205 (published *Journal of Law and Economics* 48(2),
2005). *Read: the working-paper abstract (quoted in parts by the fetch tool).* Strength: moderate (model-based). "holding company
stock is inefficient for _all_ employees, even risk tolerant ones"; employees give up about 42% of its market value by bearing
diversifiable risk.

**W14b. FINRA names employer stock and a holding that grew as the two common ways concentration happens.** FINRA, "Concentrate
on Concentration Risk" (investor insight). *Read: full page text.* Strength: moderate as guidance. "Company stock concentration.
Employees might be tempted to concentrate their retirement savings in the stock of their employer." and "Maybe one of your
investments has performed very well relative to the rest of your portfolio." FINRA gives **no numeric threshold**. The 10%
cut-off the subject uses is a **judgement call** (a common rule of thumb; no source found states it with evidence).

## D. Mix, drift and behavior

**W15. Rebalance by a rule: check once or twice a year, act when a share is 5 points off.** Jaconetti, Kinniry & Zilbering,
*Best Practices for Portfolio Rebalancing* (Vanguard, 2010). *Read: full text PDF.* Strength: moderate (one firm's simulation on
US history; it says no rule is optimal). "there is no optimal frequency or threshold"; "annual or semiannual monitoring, with
rebalancing at 5% thresholds, is likely to produce a reasonable balance between risk control and cost minimization for most
investors. Annual rebalancing is likely to be preferred when taxes or substantial time/costs are involved."
*Use:* the 5-point band and the yearly look are a convention the field itself calls reasonable, not optimal; the lesson says so.

**W16. Trading more earns less.** Barber & Odean, "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth", *Journal of Finance* 55(2), April
2000, pp. 773-806. *Read: full text, abstract and introduction.* Strength: strong (66,465 households). "those that trade most earn
an annual return of 11.4 percent, while the market returns 17.9 percent"; "Overconfidence can explain high trading levels and
the resulting poor performance". Epigraph (Graham): "The investor's chief problem ... is likely to be himself."

**W17. Investors earn less than their own funds, about 1.2 points a year, least in all-in-one funds.** Morningstar, *Mind the
Gap 2025* (US, decade to 31 December 2024). *Read: secondary summaries (Boldin; Morningstar Australia); Morningstar's own page
refused.* Strength: moderate. Reported: funds' time-weighted return 8.2% a year, investors' dollar-weighted 7.0%, a 1.2-point
gap; widest in sector equity funds; allocation funds (target-date, balanced) smallest, "sometimes just 0.2 percentage points".
**Caution on the better-known figure:** DALBAR's yearly QAIB study reports much larger gaps; Wade Pfau (2017) and the authors of
*The 3 Simple Rules of Investing* argued its method mixes dollar-weighted and time-weighted returns and overstates the gap.
*Read: search summaries of the critiques and of DALBAR's reply; the articles refused.* Strength of the critique: limited as read.
*Use:* teach the behavior gap with the Morningstar size and the Barber-Odean study, and never quote DALBAR's figure.

**W18. Separate the willingness to take risk from the ability to bear it, and let the tighter one decide.** Kitces, "Tolerisk:
Aligning Risk Tolerance and Risk Capacity on Two Dimensions" (kitces.com). *Read: full page text.* Strength: moderate
(practitioner reasoning; W1 lists both as separate items). "risk tolerance and risk capacity should be measured separately";
"by averaging together risk tolerance and risk capacity scores, the advisor can unwittingly end up in situations where clients
with extremely low risk tolerance (or risk capacity) end up with portfolios that are far too risky". The CFA curriculum's
"willingness" and "ability" split agrees (search summaries only).
*Use:* money needed soon has no capacity for a fall, whatever the learner's nerve. This is the expert's reason for cash set
aside for known bills (owner ask `cash-for-bills`) and for the emergency fund; and the reason the subject does not choose a mix
for the learner (willingness is theirs).

## E. Money needed soon, and ruinous losses

**W19. A small cash buffer goes with much less hardship.** AARP Public Policy Institute, "Short-Term Emergency Savings" series
(first paper). *Read: full page text.* Strength: moderate (observational; the page says "this analysis speaks to correlation and
not causation"). "Households that achieved liquid savings of just $2,452 at any point are significantly less likely to experience
extreme financial hardship up to three years later", about one month of income for the low-income sample. The Federal Reserve's
*Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households in 2024* (May 2025): "Sixty-three percent of adults said they would cover a hypothetical
$400 emergency expense exclusively using cash or its equivalent." *Read: executive summary, full text.*
*Use:* the emergency fund is a capacity tool. Its size in months (three to six is the common rule) is a **judgement call**;
the evidence supports that even one month matters.

**W20. Rare, severe losses are transferred; small, frequent ones are kept.** Baranoff, Brockett & Kahane, *Risk Management for
Enterprises and Individuals* (open textbook, Saylor), section 4.4 "The Risk Management Matrix". *Read: full section text.*
Strength: strong as the field's standard framework. Table 4.4: low frequency and low severity: "Retention—self-insurance"; high
frequency, low severity: "Retention with loss control"; low frequency, high severity: "Transfer—insurance". "risk transference
involves paying someone else to bear some or all of the risk of certain financial losses that cannot be avoided, assumed, or
reduced". A separate legal entity is a transfer too: "investors who wish to limit possible losses connected with a particular
venture may create a corporation and transfer such risks to it", so "the stockholders' personal assets beyond their stock in
this particular corporation are not exposed to loss."
*Use:* the reasoning for insurance, umbrella cover and an LLC (owner ask `insurance-and-structures`): what is the worst single
loss, could I pay it, and if not, who can carry it for a price.

**W21. Umbrella cover: the rule of thumb is liability limits at least equal to net worth.** Insurer and broker guidance (USAA,
Compare.com, a broker site). *Read: search summary only.* Strength: limited, and the brokers sell the product. **Judgement
call**, used only as the trigger to look ("is my net worth above my auto and home liability limits?"), never as a purchase
instruction.

**W22. Deposit insurance and brokerage protection cover the institution failing, not the market falling.** FDIC, "Understanding
Deposit Insurance": "at least $250,000 per depositor, per ownership category at each FDIC-insured bank". SIPC, "What SIPC
Protects": "The limit of SIPC protection is $500,000, which includes a $250,000 limit for cash"; "SIPC does not protect against
the decline in value of your securities." *Read: full page text, both.* Strength: strong (the agencies' own words).
*Use:* on the list, each account is marked by what protects it; a fall in a fund's value is not a covered loss.

## F. Free money first, and the order of saving

**W23. Not taking the full employer match is a dominated choice, and telling people does not fix it.** Choi, Laibson & Madrian,
"$100 Bills on the Sidewalk: Suboptimal Investment in 401(k) Plans", NBER w11554 (2005; *Review of Economics and Statistics*
2011). *Read: full text PDF, abstract and pp. 1-3.* Strength: strong for the setting (employees over 59½ who could withdraw
without penalty, so contributing up to the match was riskless gain). "between 20% and 60% contribute below the threshold, losing
as much as 6% of their annual pay. Providing employees with information about the free lunch they are foregoing fails to raise
contribution rates." Average loss 0.66% to 2.32% of salary by company.
*Use:* (1) the match comes before almost anything else; (2) knowing is not doing, so the lesson ends with the rate changed on the
plan site or dated (with old note F1, F3, F7).

**W24. The practitioners' order of saving: buffer, match, high-interest debt, tax-advantaged accounts, then taxable.**
Bogleheads wiki "Prioritizing investments" and the common "financial order of operations". *Read: search summaries of the wiki
and of mirrors only; the wiki refused.* Strength: limited (a consensus of practice, not a study; versions disagree on where the
HSA and IRA go). As summarized: emergency fund, then the full employer match, then high-interest debt, then HSA if eligible, IRA,
the rest of the 401(k), then taxable. The HSA's place rests on IRS Publication 969, which lists a deduction for contributions,
"The interest or other earnings on the assets in the account are tax free", and "Distributions may be tax free if you pay
qualified medical expenses" (*read: full text*). The order is a **judgement call** built on W23 (match), W19 (buffer), and the tax
facts; the lesson teaches why each rung sits where it does, so the learner can see when a rung does not apply.

## G. Taxes

**W25. Where a fund sits matters: heavily taxed bonds belong in the tax-deferred account.** Dammon, Spatt & Zhang, "Optimal
Asset Location and Allocation with Taxable and Tax-Deferred Investing", *Journal of Finance* 59(3), 2004. *Read: abstract via a
search result reproducing the journal record.* Strength: moderate. Investors "should hold taxable bonds in the tax-deferred
account and equities in the taxable account", a result the authors call in contrast with practice. Bergstresser & Poterba,
"Asset Allocation and Asset Location: Household Evidence from the Survey of Consumer Finances", NBER w9268 (2002). *Read: full
abstract.* "Many households hold equities in their tax-deferred accounts, but not in their taxable accounts, while also holding
taxable bonds in their taxable accounts. Most of these households could reduce their taxes by relocating heavily-taxed fixed
income assets to their tax-deferred account."
*Use:* owner ask `tax-placement`: the expert first fixes the mix across all accounts, then decides which account holds which part.

**W26. A loss can cut tax, but not if the same fund is bought back within 30 days.** IRS Publication 550. *Read: full text.*
Strength: strong (the rule itself). "A wash sale occurs when you sell or trade stock or securities at a loss and within 30 days
before or after the sale you" buy substantially identical stock or securities; a net capital loss offsets ordinary income up to
"$3,000 ($1,500 if you are married and file a separate return)". Deferring a gain (owner ask: "deferring gains") is the plain
consequence of the tax falling on a sale: no sale, no tax that year. **Judgement call** for the teaching form; no study read.

## H. Inflation and the spending rate

**W27. People judge money in nominal dollars rather than real ones.** Shafir, Diamond & Tversky, "Money Illusion", *Quarterly
Journal of Economics* 112(2), 1997, pp. 341-374. *Read: abstract via bibliographic records (search summary).* Strength: moderate
(replicated on 604 participants in 2021, per the replication's abstract). "a tendency to think in terms of nominal rather than real
monetary values". The CFP standard (W1, Practice Standard 2.a) requires "reasonable assumptions and estimates", including
"inflation rates". *Use:* every long-run number in the subject is in today's dollars, and cash held beyond the buffer is named as
losing to inflation.

**W28. The spending-rate research: about 4% of the starting pot, raised each year for inflation, lasted 30 years in US
history; today's estimates are a little lower, and flexible spending allows more.** Bengen, "Determining Withdrawal Rates Using
Historical Data", *Journal of Financial Planning*, October 1994 (FPA reprint). *Read: full text.* Strength: moderate (US history
only, one mix). "Assuming a minimum requirement of 30 years of portfolio longevity, a first-year withdrawal of 4 percent ...
followed by inflation-adjusted withdrawals in subsequent years, should be safe. In no past case has it caused a portfolio to be
exhausted before 33 years". Benz & Guo, "What's a Safe Retirement Spending Rate for 2025?" (Morningstar, 11 December 2024).
*Read: full text.* "the highest starting safe withdrawal percentage for a retiree seeking fixed real portfolio withdrawals and a
90% success rate over a 30-year horizon is just 3.7%"; flexible systems "help enlarge lifetime spending". Kitces, "Understanding
Sequence of Return Risk" (kitces.com, 2021). *Read: full text.* "the true driver of sequence of return risk and safe withdrawal
rates are the returns that the retiree earns over the first decade – and specifically, the real returns".
*Use:* owner ask `spending-rate`, kept and corrected: the old course's "spend a percentage of the pot" becomes the expert's
version, a starting rate in the 3.5-4% range with inflation raises, flexibility in bad decades, and the rate re-checked yearly; the
number is a dated reference (the yearly estimate moves), not a fact to memorize.

## I. Who inherits, and who can act

**W29. For an employer plan, the beneficiary form governs, even against a divorce decree or a state law.** *Egelhoff v.
Egelhoff*, 532 U.S. 141 (2001), syllabus: Mr. Egelhoff named his wife on an ERISA pension and life insurance, divorced, and died;
a Washington law revoking a spouse's designation on divorce was pre-empted, the Court objecting that under it "Administrators
must pay benefits to the beneficiaries chosen by state law, rather than to those identified in the plan documents"; the plan's
named beneficiary, the ex-wife, took the benefits. *Kennedy v.
Plan Administrator for DuPont*, 555 U.S. 285 (2009), syllabus: although the ex-wife had waived her rights in the divorce decree,
"the plan administrator did its ERISA duty by paying the SIP benefits to Liv in conformity with the plan documents." *Read: full
syllabi, Cornell LII.* Strength: strong (the Supreme Court).
*Use:* the expert checks the beneficiary page of each account, not the will, and checks it again after a marriage, divorce or
death.

**W30. Federal estate tax concerns very few estates.** IRS, "Estate Tax": filing threshold "$13,990,000" for 2025 and
"$15,000,000" for 2026; "Most relatively simple estates ... do not require the filing of an estate tax return." *Read: full page
text.* Strength: strong (the rule). *Use:* owner asks on gifting and moving assets out of the estate are kept as a check ("is my
estate anywhere near the threshold?") whose usual answer is "leave it"; a non-citizen spouse or assets in Canada is a flag for the
cross-border professional (a **judgement call**: the special rules for a non-citizen spouse were not read here).

## J. Canada to US

**W31. Foreign accounts: an FBAR when they total over $10,000 at any time; Form 8938 at higher thresholds.** IRS, "Report of
Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FBAR)": required if "the aggregate value of those foreign financial accounts exceeded
$10,000 at any time during the calendar year reported"; due April 15, automatic extension to October 15; "Filing an FBAR late or
not at all is a violation and may subject you to penalties." IRS, "Comparison of Form 8938 and FBAR Requirements": for a person
living in the US, unmarried, "more than $50,000 on the last day of the tax year, or more than $75,000 at any time during the
year"; married filing jointly $100,000 and $150,000. *Read: full page text, both.* Strength: strong (the rules). The thresholds
are a dated reference in the app (`subject.refs`).

**W32. A Canadian mutual fund or ETF held by a US person is usually a PFIC, taxed at the highest rate plus interest.** IRS,
Instructions for Form 8621: an excess distribution or any gain is spread over the holding period, and each earlier year's share is
taxed "by the highest rate of tax under section 1 ... in effect for that tax year", with "the separate tax and interest charge set
forth in section 1291(c)", unless a QEF or mark-to-market election was made. *Read: full text.* Strength: strong for the rule;
that Canadian-registered funds are PFICs is practitioner consensus (*read: search summaries*), limited as read.

**W33. RRSPs and RRIFs defer US tax automatically; a TFSA has no treaty status.** Rev. Proc. 2014-55 (IRB 2014-44): eligible US
citizens and residents "will be treated as making an election under paragraph 7 of Article XVIII of the U.S.-Canada Income Tax
Treaty to defer U.S. income tax on income accruing in their retirement plans until a distribution is made", and Form 8891 is no
longer required. *Read: full text.* Strength: strong. TFSA: cross-border practitioners say it "has no special status under the
Internal Revenue Code", its income is taxable in the US, and it is commonly treated as a foreign trust (Forms 3520/3520-A).
*Read: search summaries of practitioner pages only.* Strength: limited.
*Use:* owner ask "Foreign holdings under US rules" is kept: the learner learns to raise the flags (total over the threshold; a
Canadian fund; a TFSA) and the action is booking a cross-border preparer. The subject never gives the filing itself.

## K. Learning this kind of skill (from the old note and the project's learning science)

The skill is three things at once: a **procedure** on a document (find the line, compute dollars, a share, a drift, a shortfall),
a **judgment** (change or leave, flag or clear), and an **action** done once and repeated yearly.

- **W34 = old note F1 and F2.** Financial education taught ahead of time fades; it works best tied to a decision of immediate
  relevance (Fernandes, Lynch & Netemeyer 2014, read in full as F1 states; Kaiser & Menkhoff 2017, abstract). *Use:* every lesson
  ends on the learner's own accounts, and a lesson tied to a moment (a new US job, a new account, a yearly statement) waits for it.
- **W35 = W23 plus old note F3 and F7.** Information alone did not raise the match (W23, read in full); advice was mostly not
  followed (F3, abstract); writing a date and time raised follow-through for a one-off act (F7, Milkman et al., read in full).
  *Use:* the own-account step ends in a change made now or dated.
- **W36 = old note F6.** A rule-of-thumb course beat a full accounting course, most for weaker starting skills (Drexler et al.
  2014, abstract). *Use:* each lesson carries one rule with its reason and its threshold, not theory.
- **Procedures:** worked example, then completion, then alone, with an explanation after each answer (learning-science P10,
  P17, P19; old note F10). **Judgments:** look-alike pairs and mixed practice (P12, P22), the novice's wrong idea named and
  corrected (P29), legitimate cases among the bad ones (P26 requires 1). **Transfer:** practice in the real form, a real-looking
  US document (D6, R8). **Return:** spaced return with a later return at weeks to months (P24, P26 requires 2, R4); for this
  subject the yearly check is the natural return.

## What the evidence does not settle

- How expert planners actually reason, step by step (W5): no study found.
- The thresholds (10% in one company, a 5-point band, three to six months of expenses, liability limits at net worth, a
  3.5-4% starting spending rate) are conventions, each marked above; only the 5-point band and the spending rate rest on a
  read study, and both studies say their numbers are reasonable, not optimal.
- The order of saving (W24) is a practitioners' consensus read only through summaries.
- TFSA and Canadian-fund treatment (W32, W33) rest on practitioner summaries beyond the IRS's PFIC rules themselves.
- Whether the behavior gap teaching changes behavior is untested; the subject leans on set-once fixes (target-date funds, a
  written rebalancing rule, automatic contributions) because Morningstar's gap is smallest in all-in-one funds (W17).
