# US Civics & History: how experts reason, and what the test asks (2026-10-22)

Step 2 of the build-subject skill for `civics`. It replaces `civics-interview-and-news.md` as this subject's research note; that
note's findings are reused below only where re-checked today, or carried with the strength it gave them and marked so.
"Read" says what was actually read. A choice with no passage behind it is marked **judgement call**.

The subject has two real moments: the **citizenship interview** (an officer asks civics questions aloud; the owner answers
from memory) and **a news story about the US government** (a law, an order, a ruling, read on the phone). The experts are
three groups: constitutional scholars and judges (who may act, under what power, who can stop it), historians (reading a
past event in its time, by its source, against other accounts) and professional fact checkers (deciding whether a source
can be taken as it stands).

## A. The real moment: the 2025 civics test

**C1. The format.** "The 2025 naturalization civics test is an oral test consisting of 20 questions from the list of 128
civics test questions." "You must answer 12 questions correctly to pass"; "Officers will stop asking questions when an
alien answers 12 questions correctly, or 9 questions incorrectly." Filing on or after Oct. 20, 2025 means the 2025 test;
earlier filers take the 2008 test. Applicants 65 or older with 20 years as permanent residents take 10 of 20 starred
questions, in their own language. "You must answer the question with the name of the official serving at the time of your
naturalization interview." *Read: full: USCIS, "Study for the Test", fetched 2026-10-22.* Strength: primary, current.
**Design:** questions are heard and answered from memory, no options shown; the eight officeholder/where-you-live answers
are entered by the learner and checked again before a mock interview.

**C2. The list.** M-1778 (09/25): 128 questions in three sections, American Government (1-72: principles 1-15, system of
government 16-62, rights and responsibilities 63-72), American History (73-118: colonial period and independence 73-89,
1800s 90-99, recent American history 100-118) and Symbols and Holidays (119-128). Many accept several answers ("Name one
power of the president"); some ask for two or three; a run of them ask "why" with a short accepted reason (Q15 three
branches, Q26, Q28, Q49, Q56, Q71, Q77, Q84, Q101, Q106, Q110, Q111, Q114, Q121, Q122). *Read: full: the PDF from uscis.gov,
text extracted page by page, 2026-10-22.* Strength: primary. **Design:** the item bank is the 128 with their accepted
answers, in the list's own order of sections; each "why" answer is taught as the reason it is (C21).

**C3. Where 1877-1900 sits in the list.** The list has no section for 1877-1900 (its "1800s" questions stop at Reconstruction
and Q99; "Recent" starts at the 1900s), yet answers inside it come from those years: Q91 accepts "Spanish-American War"
(1898); Q99 names leaders of the women's rights movement whose work ran through the 1890s (Susan B. Anthony, Elizabeth Cady
Stanton, Lucy Stone); Q118 accepts the light bulb and skyscrapers; Q120 is the Statue of Liberty (dedicated 1886); Q63
accepts "You don't have to pay (a poll tax) to vote", a tax that only makes sense after 1877 (C17); Q97 (the 14th Amendment
makes everyone born here a citizen) was settled for the children of immigrants in 1898 (C16). *Read: full: M-1778 Q63, Q91,
Q97 to Q99, Q118, Q120.* **Design:** the years 1877-1900 get their own short lesson (see C16 to C18 for the rest of the
evidence).

**C1b. Where the changing answers are looked up.** USCIS's "Check for Test Updates" page names the current President, Vice
President, Speaker and Chief Justice, and for the rest sends the reader elsewhere: "Visit senate.gov to find your state's U.S.
Senators", "Visit house.gov to find your U.S. Representative", "Visit usa.gov/states-and-territories to find the Governor of your
state"; it does not name state capitals. *Read: the page, through a fetch tool, 2026-10-22 (quoted phrases checked in its output).*
**Design:** lesson 3 names these lookups before the learner enters the eight answers.

**C4. How the officer speaks** (carried from the old note, its C3, not re-read today): for the English part officers "repeat
and rephrase"; civics questions are asked orally. *Read: as the old note says, the Scoring Guidelines in full, 2026-10-12.*
**Design:** the question is heard as well as read (`spoken`); rephrasings are not practised.

## B. How constitutional scholars and courts decide who may act

**C5. Two divisions of power, both read at once.** Madison: "In the compound republic of America, the power surrendered by
the people is first divided between two distinct governments, and then the portion allotted to each subdivided among
distinct and separate departments. Hence a double security arises to the rights of the people." The branches check each
other because each is given "the necessary constitutional means and personal motives to resist encroachments of the
others... Ambition must be made to counteract ambition." *Read: full: Federalist No. 51, Avalon Project text.* Strength:
primary; it is the design the Constitution's own defenders gave, not a study. **Design:** every government action in a
story is placed on two axes: which **level** (federal, state, local) and which **branch** (writes, carries out, judges).

**C6. Which level holds which power.** A standard US government textbook: separation of powers "shares power among three
branches"; federalism "apportions power between two levels of government: national and subnational." Congress's enumerated
powers are in Article I, Section 8 (tax, borrow, regulate interstate and foreign commerce, raise armies, declare war, coin
money), stretched by the necessary and proper clause; powers not delegated are reserved to the states or the people (Tenth
Amendment), e.g. public health, safety, welfare and morals, intrastate commerce, running elections, setting up local
governments; some powers are concurrent (taxing, borrowing, making and enforcing laws, courts); under the supremacy clause,
if a state law conflicts with a valid federal law, "the federal law prevails." Local governments spend on police and fire,
housing, water and sewage, and schools. *Read: full: OpenStax, American Government 3e, section 3.1 "The Division of
Powers".* Strength: textbook consensus; matches M-1778 Q58 to Q60. **Design:** "theirs to make?" is answered from this
table first: is this on the level's list (federal: the listed powers; state: what is not given away; local: what the state
hands down)? And "who can stop it?" includes federal law over a state.

**C6a. Money with conditions, and no orders to the states.** "Incident to the spending power, Congress may attach conditions on the
receipt of federal funds" (*South Dakota v. Dole*, 483 U.S. 203 (1987), syllabus; the Court upheld withholding part of a state's
highway money unless it set a drinking age of 21, a rule Congress could not set directly). But "The Federal Government may not
compel the States to enact or administer a federal regulatory program" (*New York v. United States* (1992), as quoted in *Printz v.
United States*, 521 U.S. 898 (1997)), and "may neither issue directives requiring the States to address particular problems, nor
command the States' officers, or those of their political subdivisions, to administer or enforce a federal regulatory program"
(*Printz*, part V). *Read: LII texts of both, through a fetch tool, 2026-10-22 (quoted phrases checked in its output).* Strength:
primary law. **Design:** lesson 5 says a power of the states (schools) cannot be ordered from Washington, with or without Congress;
Congress can attach conditions to money it gives, and an agency enforcing them acts on a law that allows it (`l5-meals`, a pair).

**C7. How courts judge a president's power: three zones.** Justice Jackson: presidential powers "are not fixed but
fluctuate, depending upon their disjunction or conjunction with those of Congress." (1) Acting "pursuant to an express or
implied authorization of Congress, his authority is at its maximum"; (2) "in absence of either a congressional grant or
denial of authority... there is a zone of twilight"; (3) acting against "the expressed or implied will of Congress, his
power is at its lowest ebb." *Read: full: Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer, 343 U.S. 579, 635-638 (1952) (Jackson,
J., concurring), LII text.* That this is how the Court still reasons: "In considering claims of Presidential power this
Court refers to Justice Jackson's familiar tripartite framework" (*Zivotofsky v. Kerry*, 576 U.S. 1 (2015), part II, LII
text, read in that part). The majority in *Youngstown*: "the President's power to see that the laws are faithfully
executed refutes the idea that he is to be a lawmaker." Strength: primary law, the governing test. **Design:** for an
order by the president or an agency, the deciding question is "what did Congress say?": a law that allows it, silence, or
a law against it. This is the expert's first question, so it is taught as the question, not as a case name.

**C7a. The president's own branch and own powers.** "The executive Power shall be vested in a President of the United States"
(Art. II, §1); the president "shall be Commander in Chief", may "require the Opinion, in writing, of the principal Officer in each of
the executive Departments", makes treaties with two-thirds of the Senate and appoints judges (Art. II, §2), and "shall take Care that
the Laws be faithfully executed" (Art. II, §3). *Read: the Constitution's text as the National Archives transcribes it; quoted from
the text, not re-fetched today.* Strength: primary. With C7: Jackson's zones sort a president's reach into what Congress governs; the
Constitution's own grants, and running the executive branch's own offices, do not wait on a statute (the research review of
2026-10-22 made this point, citing Art. II's vesting and take-care clauses). **Design:** the president's own powers and the
branch's own offices are "theirs"; "what did Congress say?" is asked when an order reaches outside the branch (companies, people,
states); a power of the states is never the president's. "Not settled" (zone 2) is used only for the federal executive: the note
has no source carrying the zones to governors, and a state legislature holds every power not given away (C6).

**C8. Novice error: blaming the wrong level.** Voters "punish the president's party for tax increases enacted via direct
democracy" in local property-tax referendums, which presidents do not control (Sances 2017, *Journal of Politics* 79(4),
1286-1301). Citizens "do make distinctions among levels of government when evaluating issues" but "only link these
distinctions to their voting decisions if those issue attitudes are highly accessible" (Arceneaux 2006, *Political
Psychology* 27(5)). *Read: abstract: both, via OpenAlex.* Strength: observational, US adults; the error is the
president-for-everything default. **Design:** a named wrong idea (P29): "the president decided it" for a school, police,
property tax, a driving rule, an election rule; contrast pairs of a federal and a state or local story on the same topic.

**C9. Expert readers of legal texts use different strategies, and they can be taught.** Ten legal experts and ten novices
reading cases: "Experts used different strategies and distributed reading time differently than novices"; taught to 122
law students, "the strategies significantly improved comprehension, especially of beginning law students" (Lundeberg 1987,
*Reading Research Quarterly* 22(4)). *Read: abstract.* Strength: moderate, one study line. **Design:** supports teaching
the questions explicitly before practice.

**C10. How much adults know.** In the 2025 Annenberg Constitution Day survey 70% of US adults could name all three branches
(65% in 2024). *Read: summary: search summaries of the APPC release, not the release itself.* Strength: weak for detail.
**Design:** the three branches stay lesson 1; a newcomer is not behind most citizens here, so nothing is skipped.

## C. How historians reason

**C11. Sourcing, contextualization, corroboration.** Wineburg had working historians and high school seniors think aloud
over written and pictorial documents about the Battle of Lexington: "Differences were found in how each group reasoned
about historical evidence," due in part "to beliefs that frame the act of historical inquiry" (Wineburg 1991, *Journal of
Educational Psychology* 83(1), 73-87; *read: abstract via OpenAlex; the body is behind a paywall and the companion AERJ
paper's free copy is a scan that could not be read*). The heuristics as reported by those who read it: historians treat
texts "as human constructions, whose probity can and should be interrogated", applying "sourcing (considering the
[document's author and creation]), contextualization (placing the document in a temporal and spatial context), and
corroboration (comparing the accounts of multiple sources against each other)" (Reisman 2012, author's final draft p. 4;
the bracket fills a line the extraction lost); historians source by "interrogating a document's author and the
circumstances of its creation" (Wineburg & McGrew 2019, p. 9). Strength: expert-novice think-aloud, small samples (8 and
8), widely replicated in teaching; the original's numbers are not quoted here because they were not read. **Design:** the
history lessons teach each event in its time and its chain of cause (contextualization), and one news item asks where a
historical claim comes from (sourcing).

**C12. Taught explicitly, the heuristics raise both historical thinking and factual knowledge.** Reading Like a Historian:
236 eleventh-graders in five San Francisco schools, six months, a quasi-experiment; "MANCOVA analysis yielded significant
main effects for the treatment condition on all four outcome measures": historical thinking, transfer to contemporary
issues, "mastery of factual knowledge" and general reading comprehension. The mechanism was explicit strategy instruction:
"for novices... they must see the strategies named and explicitly modeled", first "explicit instruction, including naming
and modeling". Each lesson opened with background knowledge before the documents ("Students first reviewed relevant
historical background information"). *Read: full: Reisman 2012, Cognition and Instruction 30(1), 86-112, Stanford's
peer-reviewed final draft (abstract, pp. 2, 4, 8-10).* Strength: moderate (quasi-experiment, teachers varied in fidelity;
whole-class discussion was rare). **Design:** name and model the way of thinking first, give the background facts before
the case, then practise; history facts are not traded off against reasoning, since both rose.

**C13. Knowledge decides how far the reasoning gets.** Two historians read documents on Lincoln and slavery; the one
without detailed content knowledge, "after much cognitive flailing," reached by the end "where his more knowledgeable
colleague began" (Wineburg 1998, *Cognitive Science* 22(3)). *Read: abstract.* With the old note's C7 (Recht & Leslie 1988:
domain knowledge makes a text readable; *summary*, criticised for over-use). **Design:** the facts of each part come before
the news or history cases that use them.

**C14. The years are a period of their own in the field.** The AP US History framework makes 1865-1898 a period ("Period 6")
built on industrial capitalism, immigration and responses to it. *Read: summary: search summary of a 2019 Course and Exam
Description excerpt on a district site.* Strength: weak, but it shows historians do not skip the years.

## D. How fact checkers judge a source (civic online reasoning)

**C15. Lateral reading, taking bearings, click restraint.** 10 PhD historians, 10 professional fact checkers and 25
Stanford students evaluated live sites on social and political issues. "Historians and students often fell victim to
easily manipulated features of websites, such as official-looking logos and domain names. They read vertically, staying
within a website... fact checkers read laterally, leaving a site after a quick scan and opening up new browser tabs in
order to judge the credibility of the original site", reaching "more warranted conclusions in a fraction of the time."
Taking bearings: "before diving deeply into unfamiliar content, chart a plan." Click restraint: before clicking, "they
examined each URL, considered the source of the information, and scanned" the snippets. On one task, "80% of students had
devoted no time to investigating who was behind" the site; checkers left the starting page in 37 seconds on average.
*Read: full: Wineburg & McGrew 2019, Teachers College Record 121(11), the in-press version in Stanford's repository
(abstract, executive summary, pp. 20-21, 37-40, 48).* Strength: expert-novice study, strong for what experts do.
Note that even historians failed here: the history heuristic of reading a document closely is not the online one.
**Design:** "who is telling you this?" is answered off the page (what do others say about this source?), not from the
page's look, its .org, or its About page.

**C16 (history in today's news). An 1898 case decided a 2026 story.** On June 30, 2026 the Supreme Court held Executive
Order 14160 invalid: the 14th Amendment "guarantees citizenship to children born on U.S. soil," including "children born of
parents temporarily or unlawfully present"; the Court read *United States v. Wong Kim Ark* (1898) as confirming citizenship
for "nearly all children born in the United States" and saw "no reason to depart from that view today" (CRS Legal Sidebar
LSB11468, 2026-08-13; *read: summary through a fetch tool, quoted phrases checked in its output*). *Wong Kim Ark*'s
question was whether a child born here to Chinese parents domiciled here is a citizen under the 14th Amendment; the
government held him on a ship under the "Chinese Exclusion Acts" (169 U.S. 649, LII text, *read: the question presented
and the government's petition*). Strength: primary for 1898, secondary for 2026. **Design:** the 1877-1900 lesson ends on
this story, read the historian's way: what earlier law or ruling does it stand on, and from when?

**C17. What happened to the vote after 1877.** The Voting Rights Act (1965) "outlawed the discriminatory voting practices
adopted in many southern states after the Civil War, including literacy tests"; it came "95 years after the amendment was
ratified", years when Black Southerners "faced tremendous obstacles to voting, including poll taxes, literacy tests"
(National Archives, Milestone Documents, "Voting Rights Act"; *read: the page's summary*). *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896)
upheld a Louisiana law of 1890 requiring "equal but separate accommodations" on trains (163 U.S. 537, LII text, *read: the
statute as quoted and Harlan's dissent, "Our constitution is color-blind"*). Strength: primary. **Design:** Q98 ("all men
got the right to vote" in 1870) is taught with its truth in practice, so Q63's poll tax and Q112's civil rights movement
have a cause; without 1877-1900 the course would teach that the right, once written, was held.

**C18. Teaching lateral reading works, also for adults in a civics course.** 230 first-year college students in a general
education civics course: three class sessions on four moves ("look for trusted work," "find the original," "investigate
the source," "circle back"), three practice sessions and three online assignments; at posttest 61.0% of taught students
read laterally and judged trust correctly on at least one problem, against 0 to 1.2% per problem in control sections;
students "overestimate the extent to which they actually engaged in lateral reading"; no retention test (Brodsky et al.
2021, *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications* 6:23; *read: full text through a fetch tool, Methods, Results and
Discussion*). In a district field study, six one-hour lessons in a required high school government course raised
students' ability to judge digital content against matched classrooms (Wineburg et al. 2022, *Journal of Educational
Psychology* 114(5); *read: abstract summary*). Without teaching, 3,446 students mostly judged sites by "a website's 'look',
its top-level domain, the content on its About page" (Breakstone et al. 2021, *Educational Researcher*; *read: abstract*).
Strength: moderate; the gain is shown on the open web, with practice there. **Design:** one lesson teaches the move and
practises its first decisions in the app; the move itself is done on the open web, which the app cannot host (plan, gap 1).

## What the evidence settles, and what it does not
- **Settled**: the interview is facts recalled aloud from a published list (C1, C2). Experts place a government action on
  two axes and ask what power it rests on and who can check it (C5 to C7). Experts judge a source by leaving it (C15, C18).
  Historians read an event by its time and its source (C11, C12).
- **Not settled**: no study tests news-reading after civics facts directly (C13 is the nearest); no study tests lateral
  reading taught in an offline app; the Jackson zones simplified for a beginner are a **judgement call** (the Court's
  test is the three zones, the plain wording is ours); the timeline (`order`) item for contextualization is a **judgement
  call** drawn from C11's "placing the document in a temporal and spatial context".
- **Judgement calls added 2026-10-22**: *when to take a story as it stands* (a source already checked, or the original record reached
  yourself, and an ordinary claim; otherwise check). C15's click restraint is about choosing which result to open, not about
  skipping the check, so this rule has no passage of its own; it is ours, drawn from C15 and C18's "investigate the source" and
  "find the original". *State over local among the checks*: from C6 (local power is what the state hands down; a clash goes to the
  higher law), not from a separate source.
- **Not used**: Seixas's six historical thinking concepts (not read); the Historical Thinking Chart's question wording
  (seen only in third-party copies).

## F. Dates and causes for the history lessons (added while building lessons 10 to 14, 2026-10-22)

**C23. The dates the timelines and history stories rest on**, each read today on the page named (primary or the government's own
historians), quoted closely:
- Amendments (National Archives, "The Constitution: Amendments 11-27"): 13th ratified December 6, 1865; 14th ratified July 9, 1868
  ("All persons born or naturalized in the United States..."); 15th ratified February 3, 1870 (the vote "shall not be denied or abridged"
  by race); 19th ratified August 18, 1920; 24th ratified January 23, 1964 (no poll tax "or other tax" in a primary or other election for
  President, Vice President, electors, Senator or Representative); 26th ratified July 1, 1971 (citizens "eighteen years of age or older").
- Before independence (Office of the Historian, "Parliamentary Taxation of Colonies"): after the Seven Years' War Britain was "burdened by
  heavy debts"; in October 1765 delegates of 9 colonies petitioned "denying Parliament's authority to tax the colonies"; "renewed resistance
  to new taxes instituted in 1767 under the Townshend Acts"; "On December 16, 1773, American colonists disguised as Indians boarded East
  India Company ships"; Parliament "passed several pieces of legislation in 1774", in the colonies "referred to as the Intolerable Acts".
- The Revolution (Office of the Historian, "French Alliance"): the British surrender at Saratoga, news of which reached France "in December
  1777"; "the British surrender at Yorktown in 1781".
- The 1800s: "On May 13, 1846, the United States declared war on Mexico"; the war ended in 1848 (Office of the Historian, "Texas
  Annexation"). "Confederate forces fired the first shots of the Civil War upon Federal troops at Fort Sumter at 4:30 a.m. on April 12, 1861"
  (National Park Service, Fort Sumter). "President Abraham Lincoln issued the Emancipation Proclamation on January 1, 1863"; "It applied
  only to states that had seceded from the Union, leaving slavery untouched in the loyal border states" (National Archives, Milestone
  Documents). The fighting at Appomattox ended "that morning of April 9, 1865" (National Park Service, Appomattox).
- The 1900s: on April 2, 1917 Wilson asked Congress to declare war, citing "Germany's violation of its pledge to suspend unrestricted
  submarine warfare"; the Senate voted on April 4, the House two days later (Office of the Historian, "American Entry into World War I").
  "Japanese planes bombed the U.S. fleet at Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941. The following day, the United States declared war on Japan"
  (Office of the Historian, "Pearl Harbor"). North Korea's army "attacked the South in June 1950"; the United States led "a United
  Nations-authorized force" against it (Office of the Historian, "The Korean War, 1950-1953").
- The Voting Rights Act "was signed into law on August 6, 1965" (National Archives, as in C17).
*Read: the pages through a fetch tool, 2026-10-22, the quoted sentences checked in its output.* Strength: primary or official history.
**Design:** every date in a timeline (`order`) or a history story comes from M-1778 itself or from this list; a cause is given only where a
source above or C16, C17 names what came before and what it answered (taxes and the Declaration; the Civil War and the Emancipation
Proclamation; poll taxes and literacy tests and the 24th Amendment and the Voting Rights Act; the 1890 Louisiana law and *Plessy*; the
Chinese Exclusion Acts and *Wong Kim Ark*; the Tea Party and the 1774 laws). That the lesson's cause question names "what led to it" is a
**judgement call** drawn from C11's contextualization.
- Sources: [Amendments 11-27](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/amendments-11-27); [Parliamentary Taxation](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1750-1775/parliamentary-taxation); [French Alliance](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1776-1783/french-alliance); [Texas Annexation](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1830-1860/texas-annexation); [Fort Sumter](https://www.nps.gov/fosu/learn/historyculture/index.htm); [Emancipation Proclamation](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/emancipation-proclamation); [Appomattox](https://www.nps.gov/apco/learn/historyculture/index.htm); [WWI](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1914-1920/wwi); [Pearl Harbor](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1937-1945/pearl-harbor); [Korean War](https://history.state.gov/milestones/1945-1952/korean-war)

**C24. Causes and dates added after the research review of lessons 9 to 16 (2026-10-22)**, read today on the page named:
- The Civil War's causes (American Battlefield Trust, "Civil War Facts", quoting James McPherson): the war "started because of uncompromising
  differences between the free and slave states over the power of the national government to prohibit slavery in the territories that had not
  yet become states"; "When Abraham Lincoln won election in 1860 as the first Republican president", on a platform against letting slavery expand
  into the territories, "seven slave states in the deep South seceded and formed a new nation, the Confederate States of America." The page notes
  that historians still debate the deeper causes. After the war with Mexico, "Mexico ceded to the United States approximately 525,000 square miles"
  (Office of the Historian, "Texas Annexation").
- The Chinese Exclusion Act (National Archives, Milestone Documents): "approved on May 6, 1882"; it "provided an absolute 10-year ban on Chinese
  laborers immigrating to the United States" and barred courts from granting Chinese residents citizenship; its stated reason, that "the coming of
  Chinese laborers to this country endangers the good order of certain localities."
- *Plessy* (National Archives, Milestone Documents): the 1890 Louisiana law required railroads to "provide equal but separate accommodations for
  the white, and colored races"; the Court upheld it in 1896; "It was not until the Supreme Court's decision in Brown v. Board of Education and
  congressional civil rights acts of the 1950s and 1960s that systematic segregation under state law was ended."
- Poll taxes: the 24th Amendment (C23) ended them in federal elections in 1964; in *Harper v. Virginia State Board of Elections*, 383 U.S. 663
  (March 24, 1966), "a State violates the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment whenever it makes the affluence of the voter or
  payment of any fee an electoral standard" (LII text). The Voting Rights Act outlawed literacy tests (C17); it did not itself end poll taxes.
- The Spanish-American War (Office of the Historian): "The war that erupted in 1898 between the United States and Spain was preceded by three
  years of fighting" by Cubans against Spanish rule; "After the U.S. battleship Maine exploded and sank in Havana harbor under mysterious
  circumstances on February 15, 1898", U.S. intervention became likely; the war "ended Spain's colonial empire in the Western Hemisphere."
- The Statue of Liberty (National Park Service): dedicated in 1886 ("Opening Day", October 28, 1886); proposed by Edouard de Laboulaye as "a
  great monument as a gift from France to the United States", to honor the Union's victory and the end of slavery; the park's address is in
  Jersey City, New Jersey.
- Vietnam (Office of the Historian, "Gulf of Tonkin"): Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution on August 7, 1964; in 1965 Johnson
  "authorized the first of many deployments of regular ground combat troops"; the United States "was dedicated to containing the spread of
  communist regimes." The Gulf War (Office of the Historian): in August 1990 Iraqi troops "invaded Kuwait"; "a U.S.-led coalition air and ground
  war ... began on January 16, 1991", ending with Iraq's retreat from Kuwait on February 28, 1991. Afghanistan (Council on Foreign Relations
  timeline): on October 7, 2001 "The U.S. military, with British support, begins a bombing campaign against Taliban forces." No source read today
  gives the reason for the war in Iraq, so the lessons give none.
- What led to the Chinese Exclusion Act (Office of the Historian, "Chinese Immigration and the Chinese Exclusion Acts"): "many of the non-Chinese
  workers in the United States came to resent the Chinese laborers", who might squeeze them out of their jobs; as their numbers grew, "so did the
  strength of anti-Chinese sentiment among other workers"; "Democrats, led by supporters in the West, advocated for all-out exclusion"; a vetoed
  1879 bill to limit Chinese arrivals "to fifteen per ship or vessel"; then the Angell Treaty, which "permitted the United States to restrict, but
  not completely prohibit, Chinese immigration." The law's own preamble (above) is its account of itself, not taken as its cause.
- Lucy Stone died in 1893, so of C3's three names only Anthony and Stanton are said to have worked "through the 1890s".
