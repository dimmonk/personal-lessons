# Acting on your own money: what the evidence says (2026-10-10)

Gate 1 of the `build-subject` skill for Wealth Preservation. The end result is a procedure done on the learner's own
accounts, a judgment about what to change and what to leave, and an action actually taken. So the evidence here is about
whether financial education changes behavior, when it does, and what makes people act; the procedure evidence (worked
examples, fading, feedback) is in `docs/learning-science.md` (P10, P17, P19) and the review evidence in
`docs/research/review-after-lessons.md`. Each finding says what was read; most are abstracts and secondary summaries.

## Findings

**F1. Financial education taught ahead of time fades; teach it at the decision.** Meta-analysis of 168 papers (201
studies): interventions explained about 0.1% of the variance in financial behavior, less in low-income samples; even
long courses had negligible effects 20 months or more later. The authors propose "just in time" education tied to the
specific behavior it is meant to help (Fernandes, Lynch & Netemeyer, 2014, *Management Science* 60(8), 1861–1883).
*Read: abstract (several repository copies agree).* A later meta-analysis of 76 randomized trials (over 160,000 people)
finds effects on behavior about three times larger, comparable to education in other fields (Kaiser, Lusardi, Menkhoff &
Urban, 2022, *Journal of Financial Economics* 145(2), 255–272). *Read: abstract and secondary summaries.* Both agree
effects are modest and shrink with time. **Design:** every lesson ends on the learner's own accounts, now; a lesson whose
own-account step cannot be done yet (no US account) comes back when the moment arrives.

**F2. The "teachable moment" and intensity drive results.** 126 impact evaluations: education works best when it is
intense and offered when "directly linked to decisions of immediate relevance"; debt behavior is hardest to move;
mandatory courses look less effective (Kaiser & Menkhoff, 2017, *World Bank Economic Review* 31(3), 611–630). *Read:
abstract and working-paper summary.* **Design:** the triggers (a new job's 401(k) enrollment, opening an account, the
yearly statement) are when the matching lesson is offered.

**F3. Knowing what to do is not doing it.** About 8,000 retail clients of a large broker were offered free, unbiased,
personalized portfolio advice: 5% accepted, those who most needed it were least likely to, and those who accepted hardly
followed it (Bhattacharya, Hackethal, Kaesler, Loos & Meyer, 2012, *Review of Financial Studies* 25(4), 975–1032).
*Read: abstract and summaries.* **Design:** a lesson is not done until the change is made or dated; the subject counts
actions done, not answers given.

**F4. Set it once beats doing it often.** Switching a 401(k) to automatic enrollment raised participation of new hires
by about 48 points, and most kept the default rate and fund (inertia, and the default read as advice) (Madrian & Shea,
2001, *QJE* 116(4), 1149–1187). Committing in advance to raise saving at future raises took savers in one plan from 3.5%
to 13.6% of pay by the fourth raise (Thaler & Benartzi, 2004, *JPE*); the comparison group was self-selected, so the
causal rating is low (DOL CLEAR). *Read: abstracts and reviews.* **Design:** prefer fixes that then run by themselves
(automatic contributions, a target-date fund, automatic rebalancing, a beneficiary named once) over repeated effort; the
habit taught is one yearly check, not daily vigilance.

**F5. People ignore fees, even when the products are identical; showing them in money helps a little.** MBA and Harvard
students allocating $10,000 among four S&P 500 index funds overwhelmingly failed to pick the cheapest; making fees
salient shifted them toward cheaper funds but still did not minimize fees; they weighted past returns (Choi, Laibson &
Madrian, 2010, *Review of Financial Studies* 23(4), 1405–1432). *Read: abstract and working-paper summaries.* Showing
fees in pesos rather than as yearly percentages made less literate workers more fee-sensitive in hypothetical choices
(Hastings & Tejeda-Ashton, 2008, NBER w14538, not peer reviewed). *Read: abstract.* **Design:** the learner turns every
percentage into dollars a year, and the change rule is a comparison with a same-kind cheaper option, not a general
"fees are bad".

**F6. Simple rules of thumb beat full theory, most for weaker starting points.** Randomized trial with micro-
entrepreneurs: a rule-of-thumb course improved financial practices and reporting more than a standard accounting course,
with the largest gain for those with lower skills (Drexler, Fischer & Schoar, 2014, *AEJ: Applied* 6(2), 1–31). *Read:
abstract and summaries.* **Design:** each part is one rule with one threshold and one action, not finance theory.

**F7. Plans: a dated plan for a one-off act helps; a discretionary amount goal can backfire.** Writing down the date and
time of a planned flu shot raised vaccination (Milkman, Beshears, Choi, Laibson & Madrian, 2011, *PNAS*). *Read:
secondary summary.* In a savings field experiment, writing a plan with a self-chosen amount, date, method and income
source led to lower deposits than a simple intention (Loibl & Scharff, 2010, *Journal of Consumer Affairs* 44(1),
127–154); a gym-visit planning trial found a tightly estimated null (Carrera et al., 2018, NBER w24959). *Read:
summaries.* The 2025 meta-analysis of 642 tests (see `docs/learning-science.md` P26) finds plans work on average,
larger with an if-then format and a motivating goal. **Design:** the plan names when and where a one-off change is made
("Saturday morning, on the plan website, switch fund X to Y"); it never asks for a savings target.

**F8. Reminders bring it back.** Monthly reminders raised the reaching of savings commitments in three banks; extra late
reminders added nothing (Karlan, McConnell, Mullainathan & Zinman, 2016, *Management Science*). The fraud-education
effect was gone at six months without a reminder and held with one at three months (Burke et al., 2022; see
`docs/learning-science.md` P26). *Read: abstracts.* **Design:** the yearly check is scheduled by the app with a reminder;
dated actions get one reminder on their date.

**F9. Practice on the learner's own context helps.** In a randomized study with high-school algebra students, problems
rewritten around each student's interests improved the hardest skill (writing the expression) and the benefit held after
personalization was removed (Walkington, 2013, *J. Educ. Psych.*). *Read: secondary summaries; school algebra, not money.*
**Design:** practice on sample statements first (known answers, so feedback is exact), then the same step on the
learner's own statement.

## What the evidence does not settle

- No study found tests a self-study phone course whose outcome is a person checking and fixing their own accounts. The
  pilot (gate 6) and the learner's logged actions are the test.
- The thresholds used (a 5-point drift band, 10% in one company) are common rules of thumb, not findings.
- Whether typing one's own numbers into an app raises follow-through, or only feels like it, is untested.
