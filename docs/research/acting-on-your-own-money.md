# Acting on your own money: what the evidence says (2026-10-10)

Gate 1 of the `build-subject` skill for Wealth Preservation. The end result is a procedure done on the learner's own
accounts, a judgment about what to change and what to leave, and an action actually taken. So the evidence here is about
whether financial education changes behavior, when it does, and what makes people act; the procedure evidence (worked
examples, fading, feedback) is in `docs/learning-science.md` (P10, P17, P19) and the review evidence in
`docs/research/review-after-lessons.md`. Each finding says what was read; most are abstracts and secondary summaries, and each ends with whether it decides a choice of this subject's design or supports it (2026-10-12).

## Findings

**F1. Financial education taught ahead of time fades; teach it at the decision.** Meta-analysis of 168 papers (201
studies): interventions explained about 0.1% of the variance in financial behavior, less in low-income samples; even
long courses had negligible effects 20 months or more later. The authors propose "just in time" education tied to the
specific behavior it is meant to help (Fernandes, Lynch & Netemeyer, 2014, *Management Science* 60(8), 1861–1883).
*Read: full: full text (the June 2013 working-paper version, 155 papers and 188 studies; the 168 and 201 and the low-income
remark are the published abstract's and were not found in that text), "Explanation 1" pp. 13–14 and the discussion pp.
21–22, 2026-10-12.* What the text says: the decay comes from a meta-regression across studies (33 papers gave hours of
instruction, 29 gave the delay), not from an experiment that varied the delay; at delays of 20 months or more not even 24
hours of instruction had a significant effect, and a brief intervention with no delay matched a long one 10 months on.
"Just in time" is the authors' argued implication ("may have promise"), not something they tested. A later meta-analysis of 76 randomized trials (over 160,000 people)
finds effects on behavior about three times larger, comparable to education in other fields (Kaiser, Lusardi, Menkhoff &
Urban, 2022, *Journal of Financial Economics* 145(2), 255–272). *Read: abstract: abstract and secondary summaries.* Both agree
effects are modest and shrink with time. **Design:** every lesson ends on the learner's own accounts, now; a lesson whose
own-account step cannot be done yet (no US account) comes back when the moment arrives. **Decides:** every lesson ends on the
learner's own accounts, and a lesson whose own-account step cannot be done yet is held until its trigger; if teaching
decayed little with time, the course could teach on sample statements ahead of the moment and need no own-account step.

**F2. The "teachable moment" and intensity drive results.** 126 impact evaluations: education works best when it is
intense and offered when "directly linked to decisions of immediate relevance"; debt behavior is hardest to move;
mandatory courses look less effective (Kaiser & Menkhoff, 2017, *World Bank Economic Review* 31(3), 611–630). *Read: abstract: abstract and working-paper summary.* **Design:** the triggers (a new job's 401(k) enrollment, opening an account, the
yearly statement) are when the matching lesson is offered. **Supports.** F1 already decides that lessons end on the
learner's own accounts and wait for their trigger; this finding reaches the same design by a second route.

**F3. Knowing what to do is not doing it.** About 8,000 retail clients of a large broker were offered free, unbiased,
personalized portfolio advice: 5% accepted, those who most needed it were least likely to, and those who accepted hardly
followed it (Bhattacharya, Hackethal, Kaesler, Loos & Meyer, 2012, *Review of Financial Studies* 25(4), 975–1032).
*Read: abstract: abstract and summaries.* **Design:** a lesson is not done until the change is made or dated; the subject counts
actions done, not answers given. **Supports** (would decide that a lesson is not finished until the change is made or
dated, and the test's "done within 30 days"; full text not reachable 2026-10-12, the working-paper PDF sits behind a bot
check and the SSRN page refuses: a judgement call).

**F4. Set it once beats doing it often.** Switching a 401(k) to automatic enrollment raised participation of new hires
by about 48 points, and most kept the default rate and fund (inertia, and the default read as advice) (Madrian & Shea,
2001, *QJE* 116(4), 1149–1187). Committing in advance to raise saving at future raises took savers in one plan from 3.5%
to 13.6% of pay by the fourth raise (Thaler & Benartzi, 2004, *JPE*); the comparison group was self-selected, so the
causal rating is low (DOL CLEAR). *Read: abstract: abstracts and reviews.* **Design:** prefer fixes that then run by themselves
(automatic contributions, a target-date fund, automatic rebalancing, a beneficiary named once) over repeated effort; the
habit taught is one yearly check, not daily vigilance. **Supports.**

**F5. People ignore fees, even when the products are identical; showing them in money helps a little.** MBA and Harvard
students allocating $10,000 among four S&P 500 index funds overwhelmingly failed to pick the cheapest; making fees
salient shifted them toward cheaper funds but still did not minimize fees; they weighted past returns (Choi, Laibson &
Madrian, 2010, *Review of Financial Studies* 23(4), 1405–1432). *Read: abstract: abstract and working-paper summaries.* Showing
fees in pesos rather than as yearly percentages made less literate workers more fee-sensitive in hypothetical choices
(Hastings & Tejeda-Ashton, 2008, NBER w14538, not peer reviewed). *Read: abstract: abstract.* **Design:** the learner turns every
percentage into dollars a year, and the change rule is a comparison with a same-kind cheaper option, not a general
"fees are bad". **Supports.**

**F6. Simple rules of thumb beat full theory, most for weaker starting points.** Randomized trial with micro-
entrepreneurs: a rule-of-thumb course improved financial practices and reporting more than a standard accounting course,
with the largest gain for those with lower skills (Drexler, Fischer & Schoar, 2014, *AEJ: Applied* 6(2), 1–31). *Read: abstract: abstract and summaries.* **Design:** each part is one rule with one threshold and one action, not finance theory. **Supports.**

**F7. Plans: a dated plan for a one-off act helps; a discretionary amount goal can backfire.** Writing down the date and
time of a planned flu shot raised vaccination (Milkman, Beshears, Choi, Laibson & Madrian, 2011, *PNAS*). *Read: full: full text of Milkman et al. (PubMed Central, PMC3127912), Results with Table 2, and Discussion, 2026-10-12; the rest
are summaries.* What Milkman et al. found (3,272 employees of one utility, randomised, a reminder mailing with the clinic
times in every arm): asking for a date and a time raised vaccination from 33.1% to 37.1% (adjusted +4.2 points, P = 0.03);
asking for a date alone raised it 1.5 points adjusted, not significant (P = 0.51); the effect was larger where the clinic
ran a single day. It is a flu shot at one mostly male firm, not money. In a savings field experiment, writing a plan
with a self-chosen amount, date, method and income source led to lower deposits than a simple intention (Loibl & Scharff, 2010, *Journal of Consumer Affairs* 44(1),
127–154); a gym-visit planning trial found a tightly estimated null (Carrera et al., 2018, NBER w24959). *Read: summary: summaries.* The 2025 meta-analysis of 642 tests (see `docs/learning-science.md` P26) finds plans work on average,
larger with an if-then format and a motivating goal. **Design:** the plan names when and where a one-off change is made
("Saturday morning, on the plan website, switch fund X to Y"), with a time and not a date alone (a date alone did not
significantly help in the one trial read in full); it never asks for a savings target (that half rests on the
summaries). **Decides:** every own-account step ends in a change made now or dated with a day, a time and a place, never
in a savings target; if writing down a dated plan did not raise follow-through, the step would end at the answer and the
dated-action list the app builds would not be needed.

**F8. Reminders bring it back.** Monthly reminders raised the reaching of savings commitments in three banks; extra late
reminders added nothing (Karlan, McConnell, Mullainathan & Zinman, 2016, *Management Science*). The fraud-education
effect was gone at six months without a reminder and held with one at three months (Burke et al., 2022; see
`docs/learning-science.md` P26). *Read: abstract: abstracts.* **Design:** the yearly check is scheduled by the app with a reminder;
dated actions get one reminder on their date. **Supports.**

**F9. Practice on the learner's own context helps.** In a randomized study with high-school algebra students, problems
rewritten around each student's interests improved the hardest skill (writing the expression) and the benefit held after
personalization was removed (Walkington, 2013, *J. Educ. Psych.*). *Read: summary: secondary summaries; school algebra, not money.*
**Design:** practice on sample statements first (known answers, so feedback is exact), then the same step on the
learner's own statement. **Supports.**

**F10. Practice a procedure as worked example, then completion, then alone, with an explanation after every answer.** From
`docs/learning-science.md` P10, P17 and P19, added here on 2026-10-12 because the lesson blocks of this subject rely on
them. A meta-analysis of 55 studies found worked examples beat unaided problem solving for maths (g about 0.48, Barbieri
et al., 2023, numbers from two secondary reports); worked-out and completion problems beat conventional ones for transfer
(Paas, 1992, abstract); fading the example into the problem step by step helps (Pashler et al., 2007 practice guide, read
in full); feedback with an explanation beats a bare verdict (Rowland, 2014, read in full; Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie,
2020, abstract, effect sizes in the open-access full text). *Read: abstract: the levels learning-science.md states for each, from
abstracts and secondary reports up to full text; strength strong for novices (P10), moderate for fading (P17), strong that
explanation beats a bare verdict (P19).* The evidence is mostly school mathematics and science, not reading a financial
document. **Design:** each step is one worked account, one with the last step left to the learner, then two alone, with the
app's own calculation and the line to read shown after each. **Supports.**

**F11. Teach telling the sound from the unsound, so the learner does not come to suspect everything.** From
`docs/learning-science.md` P26, added here on 2026-10-12 because the lesson blocks rely on it. A preregistered randomised
experiment with a US panel: a few minutes of fraud education made people less willing to invest in adapted fraudulent
offers, with a much smaller effect on legitimate offers (Burke, Kieffer, Mottola & Perez-Arce, 2022, *Journal of Economic
Behavior & Organization*). *Read: full: full text (the February 2021 working-paper version), as learning-science.md states.*
Investment fraud only; the effect was gone at six months with no reminder; the benefit was larger for the more financially
sophisticated, and a text-only version went with a worse outcome on one measure for the least. **Design:** every practice
set holds an account that is sound and must be left alone, so the learner learns to discriminate and not to change
everything. **Supports.**

## What the evidence does not settle

- No study found tests a self-study phone course whose outcome is a person checking and fixing their own accounts. The
  pilot (gate 6) and the learner's logged actions are the test.
- The thresholds used (a 5-point drift band, 10% in one company) are common rules of thumb, not findings.
- Whether typing one's own numbers into an app raises follow-through, or only feels like it, is untested.
