# Learning everyday math: what the evidence says (2026-10-10)

Gate 1 of the `build-subject` skill for Basic Math. The kind of learning is mainly a **procedure** (setting up and
working out a calculation on new numbers), with a **judging** step inside it (seeing which kind of problem this is, so
which procedure applies). The evidence is worked-example, word-problem and mixed-practice research in mathematics, not
the classification research `docs/learning-science.md` was written around. Each finding says what was read, so its
strength is visible. Findings already in `docs/learning-science.md` or `docs/research/review-after-lessons.md` are
pointed to, not repeated.

## Findings

**M1. Worked examples first, for novices.** Studying worked examples beat solving problems unaided for maths
performance, g about 0.48 across 55 studies, school to adult, mostly algebra and geometry; correct examples beat
incorrect ones (Barbieri, Miller-Cotto, Clerjuste & Chawla, 2023). Alternating worked examples with problems beat
problems alone (Pashler et al., 2007, IES practice guide). The advantage shrinks as the learner becomes competent
(expertise reversal). *Read: via `docs/learning-science.md` P10 (secondary reports of the meta-analysis; the IES guide
in full).*

**M2. Fade the example into a problem, and ask for the reason of each step.** Worked-out steps removed one at a time
(worked example, then a problem with the last step left to do, then more steps left, then the whole problem), combined
with prompts asking the learner to name the principle behind a worked step, gave medium to large gains on near and far
transfer without extra study time (Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill, 2003, *J. Educ. Psych.* 95(4), 774–783; two
experiments). Fading alone helped near transfer but not reliably far transfer. *Read: the abstract via a search summary;
more in `docs/learning-science.md` P17.*

**M3. Teach the structure of the problem, not key words.** Schema-based instruction teaches the learner to name the kind
of problem from its structure, draw it in a fixed diagram, then solve. In a cluster-randomized trial with 1,411
seventh graders on ratio, proportion and percent (sales tax, discounts, tips, simple interest, percent of change),
it beat business-as-usual: improvement index 13 at posttest and 8 nine weeks later on a proportional problem-solving
test (Jitendra, Harwell, Im, Karl & Slater, 2019, *J. Educ. Psych.*; the WWC rates it as meeting standards without
reservations). In first graders, schema-based word-problem teaching beat control by g = 1.08, and adding teaching to
read the whole problem rather than hunt for key words added g = 0.47 (Fuchs et al., 2020, *J. Educ. Psych.* 113(1),
86–103, 391 children). A meta-analysis of 31 word-problem interventions for students with maths difficulties found
g = 0.56, larger when the teaching focused on the problem's underlying structure and explicitly taught transfer.
*Read: the WWC study page (Jitendra); the Fuchs article (first two-thirds); the meta-analysis only as a search summary.
All are school students; none is an adult studying alone.*

**M4. Mix the problem types once each is introduced, so the learner must choose the method.** Mixed practice beat
blocked practice a month later, 61% to 38% (Rohrer et al., 2020, 54 classes); the errors blocked practice leaves are
choosing the wrong method. Introduce a new type on its own first, then mix it with the earlier ones. *Read: R9 in
`review-after-lessons.md`, P22 in `learning-science.md`.*

**M5. Answer in the form of the real moment: a typed number.** Practice transfers much more when its response matches
the response the real use needs (Pan & Rickard, 2018: d = 0.58 matching against 0.28 not matching; R8). The IES panel
prefers recall formats to multiple choice (Pashler et al., 2007). With answer options, students solved algebra items by
checking the options forward instead of doing the calculation asked, which inflated multiple-choice scores most on
exactly those items (*Educational Studies in Mathematics*, 2016, doi 10.1007/s10649-016-9725-4). *Read: the 2016 study
only as a search summary (the publisher page needs a login); strength limited. The direction is consistent with M3:
picking from four numbers lets the learner skip the set-up, which is the part that is being learned.*

**M6. Feedback that shows the working and names the slip.** High-information feedback had the largest effect
(d = 0.99, against 0.46 for corrective feedback; Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie, 2020); in computer-based feedback,
elaborated feedback 0.49 against 0.05 for right/wrong only (Van der Kleij et al., 2015). What makes errors useful is
correction that analyses the reasoning behind the mistake (Metcalfe, 2017). *Read: via `learning-science.md` P19–P21.*

**M7. A calculator does the arithmetic.** Across 54 studies, students who used calculators kept or improved their
operational and problem-solving skills, and held more positive attitudes to mathematics (Ellington, 2003, *JRME* 34(5),
433–463). Retention was not settled for lack of data. *Read: abstract and secondary summaries.* **So** the subject
teaches the set-up and the check; the multiplying is done on a calculator, as in real life.

**M8. Checking whether an answer is sensible is a separate skill, and often missing.** Over 200 eighth graders were
generally unable to recognize reasonable answers, and over 60% of their strategies were procedural (Alajmi & Reys,
2010, *Int. J. Sci. Math. Educ.*). Adults did better than fifth graders, especially at judging whether an answer is
practical in its setting (*Int. J. Sci. Math. Educ.*, 2022, doi 10.1007/s10763-022-10250-0; 160 participants).
Estimation rests on flexible rounding and mental computation. No trial was found showing that teaching an
estimate-first habit improves accuracy. *Read: search summaries.* **So** an estimate before the calculation is a
judgement call: it is the cheapest check against the errors that cost most in real life (a slipped decimal point, a
ratio turned upside down), and the app can score it.

**M9. Adult numeracy: real need, little teaching research.** About 30% of US adults have difficulty with whole-number
and percent tasks (PIAAC, NCES 2020); adults at the lowest level manage one-step problems and struggle when the
maths is not stated explicitly. Reviews of adult numeracy teaching find little high-quality research and small samples;
they favour explicit teaching, and meaningful everyday contexts helped anxious adults' attitudes, while teaching that
looks only at right answers with little support was linked to anxiety (New Zealand Ministry of Education, 2005; Thomas
& Ward, 2010). A 2023 UK systematic review for the Multiply programme exists (Alma Economics for the DfE); its
conclusions were not read. *Read: search summaries.* **So** most of M1–M8 is borrowed from school research; the pilot
(gate 6) is the real test for an adult on a phone.

**M10. Spaced return of new problems** (R1–R6 in `review-after-lessons.md`): a problem type comes back in later
sessions, as a new problem to solve (R8), until it is solved right in several sessions.

## What the evidence does not settle

- Whether typing the answer beats choosing it *for learning* in maths: the format evidence is about matching the real
  response and about shortcuts with options, not a head-to-head trial in a phone app.
- Whether an estimate-first habit can be taught, and whether it lowers errors (M8).
- Any of this for adults studying alone on a phone (M9).
