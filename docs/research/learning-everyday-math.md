# Learning everyday math: what the evidence says (2026-10-10)

Gate 1 of the `build-subject` skill for Basic Math. The kind of learning is mainly a **procedure** (setting up and
working out a calculation on new numbers), with a **judging** step inside it (seeing which kind of problem this is, so
which procedure applies). The evidence is worked-example, word-problem and mixed-practice research in mathematics, not
the classification research `docs/learning-science.md` was written around. Each finding says what was read, so its
strength is visible. Findings already in `docs/learning-science.md` or `docs/research/review-after-lessons.md` are
pointed to, not repeated.

## Findings

**M1. Worked examples first, for novices.** Studying worked examples beat solving problems unaided for maths
performance, g about 0.48 across 55 studies, school to adult, mostly algebra and geometry; correct examples beat
incorrect ones (Barbieri, Miller-Cotto, Clerjuste & Chawla, 2023). Alternating worked examples with problems beat
problems alone: the IES panel rates this "moderate" evidence, from many laboratory experiments (for instance 8th and 9th
graders in algebra, four example-and-problem pairs against eight problems: less time, a better post-test) and some
classroom studies, and says it applies to mathematical problem solving (Pashler et al., 2007, IES practice guide,
Recommendation 2). The advantage shrinks as the learner becomes competent (expertise reversal). *Read: full text, IES
practice guide (Pashler et al., 2007), Recommendation 2, 2026-10-12; the Barbieri et al. meta-analysis only as its
abstract and secondary summaries (the publisher page needs a login).* **Decides:** every lesson opens a new kind with
one worked example before the learner solves anything of that kind, instead of giving problems with feedback only.

**M2. Fade the example into a problem, and ask for the reason of each step.** Worked-out steps removed one at a time
(worked example, then a problem with the last step left to do, then more steps left, then the whole problem), combined
with prompts asking the learner to name the principle behind a worked step, gave medium to large gains on near and far
transfer without extra study time (Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill, 2003, *J. Educ. Psych.* 95(4), 774–783; two
experiments). Fading alone helped near transfer but not reliably far transfer. A caution from the abstract of the 2023
meta-analysis in M1 (read as a search summary only): worked-example conditions that added self-explanation prompts did
worse than those without, on average, so the "why this step" prompt rests on this one pair of experiments. *Read: the
abstract via a search summary; more in `docs/learning-science.md` P17.* **Supports.** (Would decide the completion
problems and the one "why this step" prompt per kind; full text not reachable 2026-10-12: a judgement call.)

**M3. Teach the structure of the problem, not key words.** Schema-based instruction teaches the learner to name the kind
of problem from its structure, draw it in a fixed diagram, then solve. In a cluster-randomized trial with 1,411
seventh graders on ratio, proportion and percent (sales tax, discounts, tips, simple interest, percent of change),
it beat business-as-usual: improvement index 13 at posttest and 8 nine weeks later on a proportional problem-solving
test (Jitendra, Harwell, Im, Karl & Slater, 2019, *J. Educ. Psych.*; the WWC rates it as meeting standards without
reservations). In at-risk first graders taught one to one (45 sessions of 30 minutes), schema-based word-problem
teaching beat control by g = 1.08, and adding language teaching (to read the whole problem, not to hunt for key words
or grab numbers) added g = 0.47 (Fuchs et al., 2020, *J. Educ. Psych.* 113(1), 86–103, 391 children). In that study
the structure taught for each problem type is a diagram or an equation, the relevant numbers are placed into it, it is
turned into a number sentence with the missing quantity, and the learner computes; the whole-problem reading was taught
partly by having children find the error in other children's key-word solutions. The authors list as limits that it
was an efficacy study and that whether the effect lasts was not tested. A meta-analysis of 31 word-problem
interventions for students with maths difficulties found g = 0.56, larger when the teaching focused on the problem's
underlying structure and explicitly taught transfer. *Read: full text, Fuchs et al. (2020), Introduction, Method
(Intervention) and Table 3, 2026-10-12; Jitendra et al. only through the WWC study page (the article is behind a
publisher login); the meta-analysis only as a search summary. All are school students; none is an adult studying
alone.* **Decides:** each kind of problem is taught as one fixed picture (a two-row table, a 100% bar, a sketch, a
year table) filled in from the problem, and the learner reads the whole problem, instead of teaching key words or
leaving each learner to find a method.

**M4. Mix the problem types once each is introduced, so the learner must choose the method.** Mixed practice beat
blocked practice on an unannounced test a month later, 61% to 38%, d = 0.83, in 54 seventh-grade classes over four
months (787 students), with a positive effect for each of the 15 teachers (Rohrer et al., 2020). The authors say
interleaving combines three things at once (choosing the strategy from the problem, spacing, and retrieval) and the
trial did not separate them. Their own caveats: it probably takes more time per problem; the benefit may be smaller at
short delays; students had probably had some blocked practice with a new skill first, so it should not replace that
entirely; and feedback was given and may be necessary. So: introduce a new type on its own first, then mix it with the
earlier ones, with feedback. *Read: full text, Rohrer et al. (2020), Abstract, Results and Discussion with its
caveats (author-posted copy), 2026-10-12; R9 in `review-after-lessons.md`, P22 in `learning-science.md`.*
**Decides:** from lesson 3 on, part of every lesson is mixed, unlabeled problems of all kinds so far, after the kind has
been taught on its own, instead of a block of one kind per lesson.

**M5. Answer in the form of the real moment: a typed number.** Practice transfers more when the correct answers of the
practice test and the final test are the same or largely overlap ("response congruency": Pan & Rickard, 2018, a
meta-analysis of 192 effect sizes: d = 0.28 without it, 0.58 with it; R8). That moderator is about the same answers
recurring, not about the format of the response, so it does not by itself show that typing a number transfers better
than choosing one; and in that review the problem-solving skills category (which holds the worked-example studies)
transferred weakly (d = 0.29, not significant). The IES panel rates short-answer and fill-in quizzes above
multiple-choice for remembering facts, with strong evidence (Pashler et al., 2007, Recommendation 5b); that is about
recalling facts, not about solving. With answer options, students solved algebra items by checking the options forward
instead of doing the calculation asked, which inflated multiple-choice scores most on exactly those items
(*Educational Studies in Mathematics*, 2016, doi 10.1007/s10649-016-9725-4). *Read: full text, Pan & Rickard (2018),
Results and the response-congruency definition, and Pashler et al. (2007), Recommendation 5b, 2026-10-12; the 2016
study only as a search summary (the publisher page needs a login); strength limited. The direction is consistent with
M3: picking from four numbers lets the learner skip the set-up, which is the part that is being learned.*
**Supports.** (Would decide the typed numeric answer, with its tolerance, built before any lesson, instead of choosing
among given numbers; full text of the 2016 study not reachable 2026-10-12: a judgement call. The typed answer also
follows from gate 2's test, which asks for a typed number.)

**M6. Feedback that shows the working and names the slip.** High-information feedback had the largest effect
(d = 0.99, against 0.46 for corrective feedback; Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie, 2020); in computer-based feedback,
elaborated feedback 0.49 against 0.05 for right/wrong only (Van der Kleij et al., 2015). What makes errors useful is
correction that analyses the reasoning behind the mistake (Metcalfe, 2017). *Read: via `learning-science.md` P19–P21.* **Supports.**

**M7. A calculator does the arithmetic.** Across 54 studies, students who used calculators kept or improved their
operational and problem-solving skills, and held more positive attitudes to mathematics (Ellington, 2003, *JRME* 34(5),
433–463). Retention was not settled for lack of data. *Read: abstract and secondary summaries.* **So** the subject
teaches the set-up and the check; the multiplying is done on a calculator, as in real life. **Supports.**

**M8. Checking whether an answer is sensible is a separate skill, and often missing.** Over 200 eighth graders were
generally unable to recognize reasonable answers, and over 60% of their strategies were procedural (Alajmi & Reys,
2010, *Int. J. Sci. Math. Educ.*). Adults did better than fifth graders, especially at judging whether an answer is
practical in its setting (*Int. J. Sci. Math. Educ.*, 2022, doi 10.1007/s10763-022-10250-0; 160 participants).
Estimation rests on flexible rounding and mental computation. No trial was found showing that teaching an
estimate-first habit improves accuracy. *Read: search summaries.* **So** an estimate before the calculation is a
judgement call: it is the cheapest check against the errors that cost most in real life (a slipped decimal point, a
ratio turned upside down), and the app can score it. **Supports.** (Would decide the typed estimate before every
answer and the "your answer and your estimate disagree" prompt; full text not reachable 2026-10-12: a judgement call.)

**M9. Adult numeracy: real need, little teaching research.** About 30% of US adults have difficulty with whole-number
and percent tasks (PIAAC, NCES 2020); adults at the lowest level manage one-step problems and struggle when the
maths is not stated explicitly. Reviews of adult numeracy teaching find little high-quality research and small samples;
they favour explicit teaching, and meaningful everyday contexts helped anxious adults' attitudes, while teaching that
looks only at right answers with little support was linked to anxiety (New Zealand Ministry of Education, 2005; Thomas
& Ward, 2010). A 2023 UK systematic review for the Multiply programme exists (Alma Economics for the DfE); its
conclusions were not read. *Read: search summaries.* **So** most of M1–M8 is borrowed from school research; the pilot
(gate 6) is the real test for an adult on a phone. **Supports.**

**M10. Spaced return of new problems** (R1–R6 in `review-after-lessons.md`): a problem type comes back in later
sessions, as a new problem to solve (R8), until it is solved right in several sessions. *Read: the notes cited; no source of its own.* **Supports.** (Would decide that wrong kinds come back in later
sessions as new problems; it rests on R2 and R4, read only as abstracts and summaries: a judgement call.)

## What the evidence does not settle

- Whether typing the answer beats choosing it *for learning* in maths: the format evidence is about matching the real
  response and about shortcuts with options, not a head-to-head trial in a phone app.
- Whether an estimate-first habit can be taught, and whether it lowers errors (M8).
- Any of this for adults studying alone on a phone (M9).
