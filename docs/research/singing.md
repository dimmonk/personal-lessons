# Singing: how voice teachers and voice scientists reason (research note, 2026-10-22)

Step 2 of the `build-subject` skill for Singing (`singing`, field Skills). The learner is an adult beginner who sings for fun
(car, karaoke, friends). Singing is a body skill: the ear and the voice together. This note asks two questions: how do
teachers of singing and voice scientists decide what is wrong and what to do about it, and what does motor-learning research
say about learning a voice skill? Findings are numbered V1 to V20. Each gives the source, what was read, a strength
(**strong**, **moderate**, **limited**, as in `docs/learning-science.md` section 1), and the passage, quoted or closely
paraphrased with its page or section.

This note reuses the earlier note `docs/research/learning-to-sing.md` (S1-S6) only where the source was checked again here
or the earlier check was a full-text read recorded with page numbers. Each reuse says so.

## A. What is behind "I can't sing": the ear, the link, the voice

**V1. True tone deafness is rare; most people who think they cannot sing can.** Peretz & Vuvan (2017), *European Journal of
Human Genetics* 25, 625-630. Read: full text (PMC5437896). **Strong** (16,625 adults analysed, recruited without
self-referral, three objective tests). Abstract: congenital amusia, "commonly known as tone deafness", affects "only 1.5%";
Discussion: a looser criterion (the Scale test alone) gives 4.2%. Results, "Musical training": asked whether they lack a sense
of music, 66.7% of amusics said yes, and so did 20.3% of the controls, a high rate of false alarms. Wise & Sloboda (2008),
*Musicae Scientiae* 12(1), 3-26, read as abstract only (**limited**): about 17% of Western adults call themselves tone deaf;
13 such adults scored lower than 17 others on every measure but did not show the severe, pervasive deficits of amusia, and
both groups sang better with accompaniment. **Use:** the subject opens by retiring the idea "I'm tone deaf": the check is
the measured note, not the feeling.

**V2. Most occasional singers sing in tune, especially when they slow down.** Dalla Bella, Berkowska & Sowiński (2011),
"Disorders of pitch production in tone deafness", *Frontiers in Psychology* 2:164. Read: full text (PMC3140645), sections
"Singing in the General Population" and "Measuring Singing Accuracy and Precision". **Moderate** (a review by the field's
main group, summarising their own studies). Dalla Bella, Giguère & Peretz (2007) tested 62 occasional singers in Montreal
(42 recruited in a park, 20 students) against 4 professionals; "slowing down tempo typically enhances accuracy in producing
pitch intervals"; in a follow-up of 15, two poor-pitch singers did not improve when slower. Around 85-90% "can sing in tune";
10-15% "may be particularly inaccurate in producing pitch"; the figure rises to about 55% when precision (consistency over
repeats) is counted rather than accuracy (the average error). The PubMed abstract of the 2007 paper (*JASA* 121, 1182-1189)
says the same: most occasional singers sang about as accurately as professionals when asked to sing slowly. **Use:** a new
tune is learned slowly first; accuracy and consistency are both checked (more than one try per check).

**V3. Poor singing is mostly the link from hearing to voice, not the ear.** Pfordresher & Brown (2007), *Music Perception*;
reused from S1 (full text read 2026-10-12, Exp. 2 and discussion pp. 14-17): 10-15% of untrained students sang clearly off
(a semitone or more) imitating four-note tunes, while they discriminated pitch as well as accurate singers; the whole tune
shifted and intervals were compressed; errors grew with the target's distance from the singer's comfortable pitch. Hutchins
& Peretz (2012), *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 141, 76-97, read through the BRAMS lab's summary only
(**limited** for the numbers): non-musicians matched pitch 97% of the time with a slider and 59% with their voice; of 31
non-musicians in a second experiment, 2 (6%) failed even with the slider, 6 (19%) matched with the slider but not the voice
(vocal control), and 11 (35%) matched their own recorded voice but not the synthesized tone (translating timbre).
Pfordresher, Demorest, Dalla Bella, Hutchins, Loui, Rutkowski & Welch (2015), "Theoretical perspectives on singing accuracy",
*Music Perception* 32, 227-231, read in full: the field's consensus model (p. 229) puts most of the recent evidence on "an
inability to appropriately translate perceptual representations into motor plans ... accompanied in some cases by difficulty
in controlling the vocal motor system", and says perceptual deficits are not a sine qua non of poor singing. **Moderate.**
**Use:** practice is copying heard targets with the voice (the link is what is trained); targets sit in the learner's range;
a target that sounds like a voice is easier to copy than a pure tone (see the engine gap in the plan).

**V4. Range and accuracy grow together.** Pfordresher et al. (2015), p. 230 (read in full): "on average vocal range
increases with age, along with vocal accuracy"; at an early phase, with a constrained range, "singing takes on a
monotone-like quality"; accuracy grows "as vocal range widens and various vocal registers become comfortable". Rutkowski's
paper in that issue proposes register use (moving from the speaking range into a higher singing range) as a catalyst.
**Limited for adults** (the developmental evidence is about children). **Use:** finding the range comes first (with V3);
the light upper voice is practised, not avoided.

**V5. Hearing it in your head first.** Pfordresher & Halpern (2013), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*; reused from S1 (read in
full 2026-10-12): accurate singing went with vivid self-reported auditory imagery, not with pitch discrimination;
correlational. **Limited.** **Use:** "hear the note before you sing it" is taught as a step (the old course's "guessing the
note" is the failure of this step).

## B. How listeners and teachers judge pitch

**V6. Pitch is what teachers rank first, and what lay listeners hear: interval size.** Michael & Gilman (2021), "The myth of
intonation as an objective measure of singing quality", *Journal of Singing* 77(5), 591-604, read p. 591: "pitch accuracy is
generally considered by singing teachers, coaches, among others, to be not only the most important factor in judging singing
ability and talent, but also the most objective"; yet "singing teachers disagree how good the intonation is" about as much
as on more subjective qualities. Larrouy-Maestri, Magis, Grabenhorst & Morsomme (2015), *PLOS ONE*, read in full
(PMC4550346): 166 untrained singers' "Happy Birthday"; mean interval deviation 55.97 cents (SD 48.21, range 10 to 383);
for lay judges "only the pitch interval deviation variable has an impact" on their scores, while experts also counted
tonality modulations (an interval error larger than a semitone, so the tune drifts to a new key). **Moderate.** **Use:** the
subject puts the note first among the checks; the score is measured, not judged by ear; melodies are scored note by note so a
drift counts.

**V7. Listeners forgive a voice more than an instrument.** Hutchins, Roquet & Peretz (2012), "The vocal generosity effect",
*Music Perception* 30, abstract only: musicians and non-musicians needed a larger mistuning before they judged a sung tone out
of tune than a violin tone. **Limited** (no cents read). **Use with V6 and V15:** the pass mark need not be stricter than what a
listener hears; it is a judgement call (plan).

## C. How teachers diagnose

**V8. Teachers diagnose like clinicians: symptom, cause, correction, in a fixed set of areas.** McKinney, *The Diagnosis and
Correction of Vocal Faults* (rev. ed. 1994; Waveland 2005). Read: the table of contents (library record) and a ChoralNet
summary of its method, not the book. Chapters in order: diagnosing vocal faults, a basic approach to vocal sound, posture,
breathing and support, phonation, registration, voice classification, resonation, articulation, the speaking voice,
coordination. The summary: diagnosis needs "comprehensive knowledge of the vocal mechanism", starts with listening to the
student talk about goals, and its "most structured phase" is hearing the student sing. Van Vuuren (2017, MMus, Pretoria),
abstract: faults are traced to interdependent foundations (alignment, breath management, phonation, resonance, articulation,
registration, vibrato). **Limited** (the method is a textbook tradition; no expert-novice study of voice teachers'
diagnosis was found, see "Gaps"). **Use:** the subject's way of thinking is a diagnosis before a fix, with named areas; which
area comes first for this learner is decided below by what changes the result most and what the app can check.

**V9. Feedback goes to the most important error only, and to large errors before small ones.** Crocco & Meyer (2021), "Motor
learning and teaching singing: an overview", *Journal of Singing* 77(5), 693-702, read in full. P. 697: teachers should
"offer feedback only on the most important ingredient that the student performed incorrectly" (the recipe analogy) and "only
on larger rather than smaller errors". Maxfield (2013), "Improve your students' learning by improving your feedback",
*Journal of Singing* 69(4), 471-478, read in full, p. 474: Schmidt "recommends focusing on one feature of the task that is
most fundamental to its successful completion such as breathing or articulation, but not both". **Moderate** (motor-learning
principles applied to voice by voice scientists; the voice-specific trials are few). **Use:** one thing at a time; the order of
the checks is the order of importance.

**V10. The goal is an internal sense of right, so the student can be their own teacher.** Crocco & Meyer (2021), p. 694,
"Internal reference-of-correctness": students who rely on the teacher are "borrowing their teacher's brain"; teachers build
the student's own reference by "1) ensuring that students have an understanding of the target and goal; 2) asking students
what they hear/feel; 3) slowly withdrawing feedback and asking students to assess their own performance". They also report
(p. 693) that teachers in their study gave "copious amounts of verbal instruction, modeling, and feedback, but spent
surprisingly little time having them sing". **Moderate.** **Use:** most of every lesson is singing, not reading; the learner
says what they heard before the app shows the result (engine gap, plan); the display and the result fade.

## D. Voice science behind the checks

**V11. Push harder and the note goes sharp, most of all high and loud.** Sundberg (1993), "Breathing behavior during
singing", *The NATS Journal* 49(3) (reprint of STL-QPSR 1992), read in full. P. 4 of the reprint: in singing, pressure "has
to be tailored also to pitch; higher pitches need higher pressures than lower pitches"; "a raised subglottal pressure raises
the pitch", so a pressure error is "an error in pitch, which, of course, may be a disaster for a singer"; a failure to reach
the target pressure "becomes quite substantial in loud singing, particularly at high pitches". P. 8: "exaggerated glottal
adduction implies pressed phonation, the type of phonation that speakers generally resort to under conditions of high pitch
and loudness: the voice sounds strained". **Moderate** (a review of the author's own measurements in a few singers). **Use:**
the top notes and the pitch are not separate problems: a note that goes off only where the line climbs loud is first treated
as effort (sing it lighter), not as the ear. This keeps the old course's rule that the top notes come before the note when
both show, and gives it its reason.

**V12. Straw, hum and "oo" make the voice easier, especially through the high range.** Titze (2018), "Major benefits of
semi-occluded vocal tract exercises", *Journal of Singing* 74(3), 311-312, read in full: the exercises' "efficacy is no longer
challenged"; collision forces fall, "allowing lung pressure and fundamental frequency to be taken high in a pitch glide"; the
even reinforcement of harmonics "helps to avoid register instabilities and supports mixed registration"; "most of these
effects occur simultaneously without much voluntary adjustment. The system self-regulates." Titze (2006), *JSLHR* 49,
448-459, abstract: a computer simulation; such exercises had been praised by clinicians and teachers but had little
scientific underpinning. The earlier note's reviews (S5, summaries) say the evidence is mostly in people with voice disorders.
**Moderate** for the physics, **limited** for singers' outcomes. **Use:** each sung lesson opens with a hum or straw warm-up;
high notes are first tried on "oo" or through a straw, then on an open vowel.

**V13. There is no one right way to breathe for singing.** Collyer, "In search of good breathing for singing" (NATS ICVT 7
handout, Sally Collyer, after Collyer et al. 2008, *JSLHR* 51, 612-628, and Thomasson & Sundberg 1997, 1999), read in full.
P. 1: singers on average start phrases at about 70-80% of vital capacity and end at 30-50%; above 80% "recoil exhalatory force
increases rapidly", so "avoiding VC extremes in singing makes sense because it means less effort"; each trained singer is
"very consistent" but "different singers use very different lung volumes even on the same task". P. 2: the best singers in
the sample used different rib-cage and belly patterns; "at least part of a singer's breathing behaviour does not seem to be
under conscious control". Crocco & Meyer (2021, p. 696) call "Use more support" "infamous" as an instruction. **Moderate**
(physiology in trained classical singers). **Use:** the breath lesson teaches outcomes (a breath that lasts the phrase, no
gasp, no squeezing out the last air, breaths planned at the ends of phrases), not one body pattern; "belly out, shoulders
still" is not taught as the only right way, which corrects the old course's "A breath that works" definition.

## E. How a voice skill is learned

**V14. Less frequent, slightly delayed feedback builds the learner's own judgement; constant live feedback can make them
depend on it.** Maxfield (2013), read in full. P. 474: decreasing the relative frequency of feedback increases learning, so
"the ideal situation for learning would be large numbers of attempts made by the learner, with feedback being provided ...
only after every few attempts"; concurrent feedback improves performance during practice but "is less effective when
relatively long-term changes in performance ability are desired" (citing Schmidt & Wulf 1997; Swinnen et al. 1990); a delay of
about three seconds lets the student "hone his or her own error detection skills". P. 475: beginners do not benefit from
highly precise feedback; direction (sharp or flat) matters more than size; "bandwidth feedback" (comment only outside an
acceptable range) suits a clear target. P. 476 on biofeedback: a real-time display during the attempt may not serve long-term
learning; and best practice 5 is to have students assess themselves. Austermann Hula (2008), dissertation, UC San Diego,
abstract p. xxviii: "delaying or reducing the frequency of feedback promotes retention and transfer of skills. In contrast,
immediate or frequent feedback may temporarily enhance performance during acquisition, yet interfere with learning";
learner-controlled feedback seemed to work through "promotion of self-evaluation skills". Maxfield's own dissertation (Iowa
2011, snippet only): lower feedback frequency may suit novice singers. Crocco & Meyer (2021, p. 697) add the qualification:
for a beginner on a task complex for them, frequent, easy feedback may be better at first. **Moderate** (a large general
literature; few voice trials, apraxia patients and healthy speakers in Austermann Hula). The earlier note's S2 (Berglin et al.
2022, read in full) found a live pitch line helped poor-pitch adults in one session, more on four-note tunes than single notes;
S3 found the singing studies on removing a display mixed. **Use:** the live line is used early and briefly; the result words
give direction ("under", "over"); the line is gone in later sets and every check; the learner says first what they heard.

**V15. Direction first, then size, with a band of "good enough".** Maxfield (2013), p. 475, citing Magill & Wood (1986) and
Schmidt: "information regarding the direction of the error is more important than information regarding the magnitude"; a
bandwidth method withholds correction inside an acceptable range. **Moderate.** **Use:** the app's words (on, a shade under,
well over) already give direction and a rough size; the band is the task's tolerance.

**V16. Attend to the sound and its effect, not the muscles; the evidence is weaker than it looked.** Chua et al. (2021),
*Psychological Bulletin*, and McKay et al. (bias re-analysis): reused from S4 (full text read 2026-10-12): external focus beat
internal focus for learning (g about 0.58), but a model correcting for publication bias puts it near zero (g about 0.15).
Voice: Atkins & Duke (2013), *International Journal of Research in Choral Singing* 4(2), 28-36 (citation only): untrained
singers' tone rated better under external than internal instructions; Atkins (2015 conference slides, read): trained singers'
"ring" and "overall" ratings changed with focus condition (soft palate and vibrato versus a near, middle or far point).
Crocco & Meyer (p. 696): "Modify your vowel toward a schwa" (external) rather than "Raise your soft palate". **Limited.**
**Use:** instructions name the sound or the result ("lighter", "softer", "land on the bar"); it costs nothing if the effect is
small.

**V17. Slow first, at the real movement.** Dalla Bella et al. (V2) for tempo; Maxfield (2018), "Slow practice: how to do more
than just practice slowly", *Journal of Singing* 75(1), 69-73, read pp. 69-71: slow practice gives "thinking space" and
attention, with the caveat that "the muscular processes must be the same in slow practice as those used when the piece is
played up to tempo"; singers are limited by breath when they slow down. **Limited** (opinion plus V2's data). **Use:** a new
tune is sung slowly, then at its speed (the app's melody speed is fixed: a gap, plan).

**V18. Short, spread, regular practice; review in the real form.** General findings in `docs/research/review-after-lessons.md`
(R1, R2, R4, R8) and `docs/research/designing-a-subject.md` (D6, D12): review by doing again, spaced over days, in the form of
use. Crocco & Meyer (p. 694): learning is inferred from retention and transfer, not from how a lesson went; "true performance
of a skill is when the student can sing accurately in various performance environments". **Moderate** (general). **Use:**
the review of Singing is sung checks without the line, on later days; real songs away from the phone are part of the plan.

## F. Looking after the voice

**V19. Hoarseness: rest it; see a doctor if it is not better within a few weeks, sooner with warning signs.** American
Academy of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery, Clinical Practice Guideline: Hoarseness (Dysphonia) (Update), Stachler et
al. 2018; the Plain Language Summary (Krouse et al. 2018, *Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery* 158(3), 427-431) read in
full. P. 427: most hoarseness follows a cold "and goes away on its own in 7 to 10 days"; "See your health care provider if
your hoarseness does not go away or get better in 4 weeks". P. 428, see a provider when hoarseness does not get better in 7
to 10 days "especially if you smoke", when "you do not have a cold or flu", with pain when speaking or swallowing, coughing
blood, a lump in the neck, trouble breathing, or a severe change lasting more than a few days. P. 428: "Most hoarseness can
be treated by simply resting the voice or modifying how the voice is used." Prevention (p. 429): no smoking or second-hand
smoke, drink plenty of water, humidify, avoid excessive throat clearing, do not use the voice too long or too loudly. Key
action statement 4B (p. 430): laryngoscopy "when dysphonia fails to resolve or improve within 4 weeks or, irrespective of
duration, if a serious underlying cause is suspected". NCVS, "Tips to keep you talkin'" (ncvs.org, read): hoarseness that
does not resolve in about two weeks of gentle voice use warrants an otolaryngologist; an extra glass of water for each
caffeinated or alcoholic drink. NIDCD, "Taking care of your voice" (read): avoid "screaming or whispering", rest the voice
when sick, 30% humidity at home. **Strong** for the doctor rule (a clinical guideline), **moderate** for the habits. **Use:**
the voice-care lesson corrects the old course's fact "after more than two or three weeks": rest a hoarse voice; no singing
through pain; see a doctor if it is not better in about two weeks without a cold to explain it, at the latest at four weeks,
and at once with the warning signs.

**V20. Water helps a little; the evidence is mixed.** Leydon, Wroblewski, Eichorn & Sivasankar (2010), "A meta-analysis of
outcomes of hydration intervention on phonation threshold pressure", *Journal of Voice* 24(6), 637-643, abstract read: of 34
hydration studies, 9 entered the analysis; hydration "demonstrated a tendency to reduce" the pressure needed to start the
voice (average effect about 0.33), not significant at 95%, with effects from -0.19 to 3.96. A singer-hygiene page hosted by
NCVS (Koziara, adapted, read) says to start with eight 8-ounce glasses a day, adjusted for heat and activity, and lists throat
clearing and singing without breaks as harmful. **Limited** for an effect on singing. **Use:** water is taught as a cheap
daily habit, not as a fix; steam or humid air for a dry throat now.

## The way of thinking this research finds

A teacher of singing hears a singer and asks, in order of what most changes the result: is the song in this voice's range
(V3, V4), is the note heard before it is sung (V5), is the note on (measured, not felt: V1, V6), and if it is off where the
line climbs or gets loud, is it effort rather than ear (V11, V12)? Then whether the air lasts the phrase without gasping or
squeezing (V13), then the sound of the words (V8 places resonance and articulation after breath and phonation). They fix one
thing at a time, the largest first (V9), and teach the singer to judge their own sound (V10), with feedback that fades and
gives direction (V14, V15), talking about the sound rather than muscles (V16). Rest and a doctor are the rule for a hurt voice
(V19).

## Where the evidence is thin

- **No expert-novice study of voice teachers' diagnosis was found.** The order above is built from the textbook tradition
  (V8), from what changes the measured result (V6, V11) and from motor-learning principles (V9); it is partly a judgement
  call, named as such in the plan.
- **Tone qualities (pushing, airy, nasal, muffled) cannot be measured by the app** and no recorded clips exist
  (`docs/singing-clips.md`). Self-checks done away from the phone (holding the nose shut on "ah", recording yourself) stand
  in; their accuracy is untested.
- **No controlled study of a months-long programme for adult hobby singers** with pitch accuracy as the outcome was found;
  Berglin et al. (2022) is one session with no delayed test (S2).
- **The pass mark in cents is ours.** The field's line for a poor-pitch singer is about a semitone (V3); untrained singers'
  interval error averages about 56 cents (V6); listeners are generous to voices (V7). No source gives a cents threshold for
  "in tune enough for karaoke".
- **Not read in full:** McKinney's book, Hutchins & Peretz 2012 (lab summary), Wise & Sloboda 2008 (abstract), Hutchins et
  al. 2012 (abstract), Atkins & Duke 2013 (citation), Maas et al. 2008 (not reached; the companion dissertation's abstract was
  read instead), Titze 2006 (abstract), Leydon et al. 2010 (abstract). Findings resting on them are labelled limited.
- **Posture and onset** (a hard glottal start versus a breathy one) are standard teaching areas (V8; Maxfield's example of a
  "hard, glottal onset", p. 475) but no study tied them to a hobby singer's results; the plan treats them in words only.
