# Research: Debating (disagreeing with someone and trying to change their mind)

Step 2 of build-subject. Written 2026-10-20 for this subject alone. Sources live here only, never in a lesson.
Strength: **strong** = replicated or large and preregistered; **moderate** = one solid study or a field consensus;
**weak** = theory, essay, abstract-only read, or contested. "Read" says what I actually had in hand.
Quotes are under 15 words; page/section as available (some PDFs were text-extracted without page numbers).

## 0. What the field is (and is not)

There is no single "debating" method. Three separate literatures speak to it, and they answer different questions:
1. **Argumentation theory** (Walton, van Eemeren): what kind of exchange is this, and what is fair play in it.
2. **Psychology of reasoning and persuasion** (Mercier & Sperber, Kahan, Wood & Porter, Nyhan, Kalla & Broockman,
   Kubin et al.): what actually happens in the other person's head, and which moves have measured effects.
3. **Rhetoric** (Aristotle): the oldest list of what persuades.

Competitive/academic debate (a judged contest between assigned sides, aimed at a third-party judge) is a different
activity from changing a neighbour's mind. Walton's longer table lists "Debate" as its own row and his later work treats
political debate as a mixed dialogue: see F1. The subject therefore teaches the first three literatures, and uses competitive
debate only as the contrast case.

## 1. Findings

### F1. Name the kind of conversation before arguing in it (Walton and Krabbe)
- **Read (rewritten 2026-10-21 after two failed reviews; the earlier version rested on one secondary table and taught five
  kinds, two of them the lesson's own):**
  1. Walton 2009, "Dialectical Shifts Underlying Arguments from Consequences", *Informal Logic* 29(1):54-83, section 2 (pp. 59-62),
     full text (informallogic.ca). Walton himself, on the Walton and Krabbe typology and on shifts.
  2. McBurney & Parsons 2002, "Dialogue Games in Multi-Agent Systems", *Informal Logic* 22(3):257-274, section 3 (pp. 260-261), full
     text. A survey that restates each type in one sentence and says real talk is mixed.
  3. Macagno 2008, "Dialectical Relevance and Dialogical Context in Walton's Pragmatic Theory", *Informal Logic* 28(2):102-128, Figure 3
     (p. 110), the section on mixed dialogues (pp. 110-111) and the shift to a quarrel (pp. 121-122), full text.
  4. Aberdein 2020, arXiv 2011.13845 (the earlier source): Table 2 reproduces Walton's longer table (Walton 1997, p. 605), which adds
     Debate and Pedagogical rows. I did **not** read Walton & Krabbe's book (1995) or Walton's 1998 *New Dialectic*; the page numbers
     the papers give for them (W&K pp. 80, 100-116) are second-hand.
- **Strength:** moderate. It is the standard classification in the field and Walton's own papers state it, but it is a conceptual scheme
  (Walton calls the types "normative models", not descriptions of real talk), not an experiment.
- **The six primary types** (Walton 2009, p. 59: "six primary types of dialog: information-seeking dialog, inquiry dialog, persuasion
  dialog, negotiation dialog, deliberation dialog and eristic dialog"; "Each model of dialog is defined by its initial situation, the
  participants' individual goals, and the collective goal of the dialog"). What each is, from the passages:
  - **Persuasion** (Walton 2009, p. 60): "one participant puts forward a thesis to be proved, and the other puts forward an opposed thesis,
    or else expresses doubt". Initial situation "difference of opinion"; collective goal "Resolve difference of opinion" (Aberdein Table 2;
    Macagno Fig. 3: "Conflict of opinions").
  - **Deliberation** (Walton 2009, pp. 61-62): the opening stage raises "a governing question ... about what is to be done", e.g. "Where
    shall we go for dinner this evening?"; the "ultimate goal ... is for the participants to collectively decide on what is the best
    available course of action". "Deliberation is not centrally an attempt by one participant to persuade another to become committed to
    a particular proposal, although it is quite common for there to be a shift to persuasion dialog" as reasons are criticized. It
    "is different from negotiation dialog, which deals with competing interests". Walton says both persuasion and deliberation "can be
    about actions" and that a "bright line" between them must be drawn carefully (p. 62).
  - **Negotiation:** conflict of interests; participants "bargain over the division of some scarce resource" (McBurney & Parsons p. 260);
    collective goal a "Reasonable settlement that both can live with" (Macagno Fig. 3), "Settlement (without undue inequity)" (Aberdein).
  - **Inquiry:** "participants collaborate to answer some question or questions whose answers are not known to anyone participant"
    (McBurney & Parsons p. 260); Aberdein: "a collaborative exchange ... with the shared goal of settling an open question, which neither
    of them has prejudged".
  - **Information-seeking:** "one participant seeks the answer to some question(s) from another participant, who is believed by the first
    to know the answer(s)" (McBurney & Parsons p. 260).
  - **Eristic (quarrel):** initial situation "Personal conflict"; individual goal "Verbally hit out at and humiliate opponent"; collective
    goal "Reveal deeper conflict"; benefit "Vent emotions" (Aberdein Table 2); "participants quarrel verbally as a substitute for physical
    fighting, aiming to vent perceived grievances" (McBurney & Parsons p. 261). "Almost anything goes in a quarrel" (Aberdein section 1).
- **Debate and "a show for an audience."** Walton's longer table (Aberdein Table 2) has a seventh row, Debate: initial situation
  "Adversarial", individual goal "Persuade third party", collective goal "Air strongest arguments for both sides". It is not one of the
  six primary types. Macagno (pp. 110-111) says "political discourse, debate, or, at the extremes, propaganda, can be modelled as mixed
  dialogues ... in which the participants have several different purposes. For instance, in a political debate, the participants want to
  persuade others that they are right, but at the same time they want their opponent to look bad." **Decision:** the lessons teach the six
  primary types. "A show" is **dropped as a kind**: it was the lesson's own category (the earlier research check found it was not
  Walton's), and the one place Walton has an audience-directed type is a formal contest, which is not a talk with a neighbor. A public
  jibe that only hits out at someone is a quarrel by its goal; a talk with several purposes is a mixed talk (below), and the lesson asks
  about the cue (what a line is for) instead of forcing one label.
- **Mixed talks and shifts.** Walton 2009, p. 59: the types "do not necessarily correspond exactly to real instances ... in a real
  conversational exchange". McBurney & Parsons p. 261: "most actual dialogue occurrences ... involve mixtures of these dialogue types";
  "The two parties may or may not be aware of the different nature of their discussions at each phase, or of the transitions". Walton
  2009, pp. 59-60 (citing W&K pp. 100-116): a shift can be an **embedding**, as when a deliberation about a dam turns to
  information-seeking about its costs: "the goal of the first dialogue, the deliberation, is supported by the advent of the second dialog".
  Other shifts are **illicit**, "meaning that the advent of the second dialogue blocks or interferes with the progress of the first" (p. 60;
  W&K p. 107). Macagno (pp. 121-122): ad hominem moves in a negotiation "reveal a shift to a quarrel, which is irrelevant to the goal of
  the original dialogue. This shift, instead of helping to achieve the purpose of a negotiation, blocks it." Aberdein: shifts "can also be
  troublesome, especially if they go unnoticed by one or more of the participants".
- **Use:** the first skill is diagnosis. Ask the three things Walton defines a type by: what the talk started from, what each person is
  trying to get, and what would count as done. Then notice a shift, and whether it helps the first goal or blocks it. The owner's "chess with a
  pigeon" is a quip, not in the literature; it lines up with the quarrel row: the other party's goal is not to be persuaded.
- **Not in Walton, said in the lessons:** that the six kinds have plain names ("persuading", "deciding together", "bargaining", "finding
  out together", "asking", "quarrel"); that the cue for each is the one the lesson names (a claim the other doubts; a choice the people share;
  wants that compete; a question nobody can answer yet; one person asking another who is thought to know; lines that hit at the person). The
  bright line between persuasion and deliberation is Walton's own concern (p. 62); the lesson's cue (is there a claim in dispute, or a choice the
  speakers must make together?) is a reading of his passage, a judgement call. "Early in the talk" replaces the plan's "in the first minute"
  (the first minute is the owner's goal, not a finding).

### F2. Most people do update on facts; "backfire" is rare, and the real problem is that updates fade (Wood & Porter; Nyhan)
- **Read:** Wood & Porter 2019, *Political Behavior* 41:135-163 (full text; abstract, Discussion). Nyhan 2021,
  PNAS 2021 "Why the backfire effect does not explain the durability of political misperceptions" (full text of
  the author PDF: abstract and sections on decay and elite cues).
- **Strength:** moderate to strong (five experiments, 10,100+ subjects, 52 issues; Nyhan is a review by the original
  author of the backfire claim). Limit: these are survey experiments where people read a correction, not live
  arguments, and they measure beliefs about facts, not attitudes or identity.
- **Passages:** Wood & Porter abstract: "we found no corrections capable of triggering backfire"; "By and large,
  citizens heed factual information". Discussion: "backfire is stubbornly difficult to induce". Nyhan abstract:
  corrective information "is typically at least somewhat effective", but the gains "often do not last or accumulate";
  "the best approach is to disrupt the formation of linkages between group identities and false claims".
- **Use:** corrects the common story that facts make people dig in. The accurate story is smaller: a fact usually moves
  a belief a little, then the gains "often do not last or accumulate" and Nyhan ties that to group identity. The note holds no
  time scale for the fade, so the lessons give none (an earlier draft said "weeks"; it is removed). The goal of one conversation is therefore a
  small move, and what blocks it is identity, not stupidity.

### F3. People with a side treat the other's arguments as things to rebut; people with no axe to grind judge arguments well (Mercier & Sperber)
- **Read:** Mercier & Sperber 2011, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 34(2), full text (abstract; sect. 2.1-2.3; the passage on debate
  at pp. 71-72; open peer commentary also present, including a commentary that disputes the claim that people evaluate arguments well).
- **Strength:** moderate. A major, much-cited theory with experiments behind it, but a theory with published dissent in the commentaries.
- **Passages:** abstract: "Skilled arguers, however, are not after the truth". Sect. 2.1 (end): "people are good at evaluating arguments both
  at the level of individual inferences and at the level of whole discussions". pp. 71-72: "People who have an opinion to defend don't really
  evaluate the arguments of their interlocutors in a search for genuine information but rather consider them from the start as
  counterarguments to be rebutted. Still ... people are good at assessing arguments and are quite able to do so in an unbiased way, provided they
  have no particular axe to grind." Sect. 2.3: group debate is "especially conducive to good reasoning performance".
- **What the lessons say (2026-10-21):** only these passages, with the counterweight in section 2.3 and pp. 71-72 stated too (group arguing improves reasoning when people share the goal of getting the answer right): a person with an opinion to defend (so the person you are disagreeing with)
  treats your argument as something to rebut; fair judging is for someone with no axe to grind. **Not said:** that "people judge arguments
  fairly" without that condition, and that the person you are about to argue with is a fair check on your reasons (that was the round-2
  finding). "Ask someone with no stake to look at your reasons" is an **inference** from the passage (it is about judging others' arguments);
  it is stated as the lesson's inference and is not graded.
- **Use:** lower the stakes of the position and give a clear argument to judge; keep in mind that your own arguments are produced the same
  way (the abstract's "not after the truth").
- **Read in full on 2026-10-21 (SSRN copy of BBS 34(2):57-74 with the open peer commentary; page numbers are the journal's, from the running heads of that copy).** Passages now held
  verbatim: sect. 2.2 (p. 62): looking for counterarguments against one's own claims is hard, but "the difficulty ... should be easily overcome by having them challenge someone else's
  claims rather than defending their own"; "all participants were able to find counterarguments against a claim (which was not theirs) and to do so very quickly" (Shaw 1996). Sect. 3.4
  (pp. 64-65): when "arguments among people who disagree but have a common interest in the truth", the confirmation bias "contributes to an efficient form of division of cognitive labor";
  alone or "with people who hold similar views, one's arguments will not be critically evaluated. This is when the confirmation bias is most likely to lead to poor outcomes." Sect. 4.2.1
  (pp. 66-67), Lord et al. 1979 as the authors report it: two studies of the death penalty with "almost identical" methodologies; "the studies that yielded a conclusion not in line with the
  participants' opinions were consistently rated as having been much more poorly conducted"; "When people disagree with the conclusion of an argument, they often spend more time evaluating
  it (Edwards & Smith 1996) ... mostly devoted to finding counterarguments." Concluding section (p. 72): "Individuals thinking on their own ... can assess only their own hypotheses, ... they are
  both judge and party, or rather judge and advocate, and this is not an optimal stance for pursuing the truth"; looking for flaws in one's own arguments is "a painstakingly acquired ability to
  exert some limited control over one's own biases", and "In most discussions, rather than looking for flaws in our own arguments, it is easier to let the other person find them and only then
  adjust our arguments, if necessary." **Counterweight, Wolfe's commentary (BBS 34(2):92-93, same copy):** "reasoning biases are common even in the context of argumentation" and people "are
  subject to fallacies and suboptimal performance in generating, comprehending, and evaluating arguments" (against M&S's "people are good at evaluating arguments"); in Wolfe's lab experiments "the
  myside bias was pervasive. However, it was not linked to participant's personal opinions. People exhibited the myside bias when arguing for the side with which they personally disagreed just as
  often as for the side with which they agreed"; "presenting and rebutting other-side information consistently leads to better ratings of agreement, argument quality, and impressions of authors
  than does excluding other-side information" (Wolfe's own experiments as he reports them; the experiments themselves were not read). **Lesson 9 says only these.** It says "ask someone with
  nothing at stake" is the lesson's inference (M&S p. 72 supports "easier to let the other person find them", not a tested method). It says nothing about numeracy or ability.

### F4. On a topic tied to a side, the same numbers get read the side's way (Kahan; the Persson replication)
- **Read:** the abstract of Kahan, Peters, Dawson & Slovic 2017, "Motivated numeracy and enlightened self-government", *Behavioural Public
  Policy* 1(1):54-86 (Cambridge abstract page; full text not fetched). The abstract of the preregistered replication, Persson, Andersson,
  Koppel, Västfjäll & Tinghög 2021, *Cognition* 214, 104768, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2021.104768 (existence checked against
  Crossref and Semantic Scholar; abstract only, no sample size, full text not read).
- **Strength:** weak to moderate. The replication says "We did not find good evidence for motivated numeracy", and finds "ideologically
  congruent responding that is not moderated by numeracy".
- **Passage (Kahan abstract):** on a rash-treatment problem, highly numerate people did much better, but on the same data framed as gun
  control, responses "became politically polarized - and even less accurate".
- **What the lessons say (2026-10-21):** only that, when the same numbers were presented as gun control, answers followed people's side
  in the first study and a preregistered repeat also found answers leaning toward the person's side ("ideologically congruent responding").
  **No claim about numeracy at all** (whether skill helps, hurts, or does not matter): the two studies disagree on it (Kahan: polarization
  increased with numeracy; Persson: no moderation, "not moderated by numeracy"), and the abstract-only reading cannot settle it. "He is not
  smart enough" is therefore not argued against with these studies; the lesson only says the studies show a topic effect, not a fact about
  the person's ability.
- **Use:** with a topic tied to someone's side, expect their reading of the same numbers to lean toward it.

### F5. Arguments and facts alone did not move attitudes; a story did, a little (Kalla & Broockman; Broockman & Kalla 2016)
- **Read:** Kalla & Broockman 2020, *APSR* 114(2):410-425 (full text through char. 100,000: abstract; Experiment 1;
  "The Non-Judgmental Exchange of Narratives"; Discussion; footnote 14). Broockman & Kalla 2016 (*Science*): news
  and Retraction Watch coverage only, not the paper.
- **Strength:** moderate to strong (three preregistered field experiments, 230 canvassers, 6,869 voters; effects
  checked at four months). Effects are small and the authors say so.
- **Passages:** abstract: "deploying arguments alone had no effects"; with narrative exchange, durable reduction for
  at least four months (d = 0.08; later experiments 0.08, 0.04). Experiment 1: the arguments-only condition's effects
  were "indistinguishable from zero". Method: canvassers told voters they were "not there to judge" and asked for a
  story about "a time when someone showed [them] compassion"; the authors suggest it "encouraged analogic
  perspective-taking". Discussion: "we do not wish to overstate the substantive size of the effects". Mechanism:
  "we were unable to test all the specific mechanisms".
- **Scope the lessons keep to:** the arguments-only null is Experiment 1, on immigration; Experiments 2 and 3 (transgender rights)
  are reported as replicating the narrative-exchange effect (d = 0.08, 0.04), and the note does not hold an arguments-only result
  for them. The people who talked to voters were canvassers (230), trained, not volunteers in the lessons' sense.
- **Santoro, Broockman, Kalla & Porat 2025** (the group's later test), "Listen for a change? A longitudinal field experiment on listening's
  potential to enhance persuasion", *PNAS* 122(8), e2421982122, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2421982122. **Read in full on 2026-10-21** (Europe PMC copy, PMC11873821, CC BY-NC-ND; before that only the abstract was read). Passages: preregistered; "trained professional canvassers, acting as
  confederates, had ~10 min video conversations with U.S. participants (N = 1,485) about unauthorized immigration"; the team randomized
  whether the confederate "shared a persuasive narrative about an undocumented immigrant" and whether they "practiced high-quality
  nonjudgmental listening"; outcomes right after and five weeks later. "Sharing a persuasive narrative meaningfully and durably reduced
  prejudice and changed policy attitudes." The listening "successfully improved perceptions of the persuader and increased processing.
  Surprisingly, however, the listening manipulation did not enhance persuasion: Sharing a persuasive narrative was just as effective in the
  absence of high-quality listening." **Effect on the lessons:** the narrative is the part with evidence; "without judging" must not be taught
  as the active ingredient, and listening is taught as good for how the person sees you, not as a way to persuade more.
- **Santoro 2025 full-text passages (2026-10-21), used by lesson 6.** Conditions: a 2 x 2 (listen or not, narrative or not); 1,485 completed ~10-minute Zoom chats with people who opposed in-state college
  tuition for undocumented immigrants (recruited from social media ads, told they would talk with a "fellow participant"); outcomes immediately and five weeks later. Listening was
  "multiple probing follow-up questions, nodded their head, made eye contact, and avoided expressing judgment". Narrative alone vs placebo: policy and prejudice about 0.25 SD
  immediately and about 0.20 SD at five weeks. Listening + narrative vs narrative alone: d = 0.06 and 0.07 immediately (not significant), -0.02 at five weeks; "we do not find evidence that
  adding listening to a persuasive intervention meaningfully enhances persuasion"; listening alone: about 0.09 SD, adjusted p = .062, "decayed to zero in the long term". Mechanisms (Table 3):
  listening raised liking, respect, felt understood (0.79 SD), lowered defensiveness (-0.23 SD) and did not raise elaboration or learning. Authors: "adding listening to persuasive appeals may
  not be worth the added costs if persuasion is the goal" and "listening can make others feel respected, less defensive, and increasing their liking". Limits they state: one persuasive method
  (a detailed narrative; listening "may enhance the persuasiveness of facts or other persuasive methods"), people willing to talk to a stranger on video, video not text or in person, one
  topic, only opponents of the policy; the confederates were told to avoid the kind of listening that tailors the message to the person's concerns. They also review three text-chat studies
  (Chen et al.: an elaboration question raised perceived receptiveness and did not change views; Minson et al.; Argyle et al.) that found no added persuasion either.
- **Use:** the best direct field evidence for what to do when arguments alone will not do: share a relevant story (and, in the first
  test, ask for theirs). Honest framing: small, from trained canvassers on strangers, topics were
  immigration and transgender policy, in the US. It is not shown to work on a family argument, or a close relative.

### F6. Personal experience earns respect better than facts, but respect is not persuasion (Kubin et al. 2021)
- **Read:** Kubin, Puryear, Schein & Gray 2021, *PNAS* 2021 (full text: abstract and the passage on persuasion);
  plus the Van Bavel et al. 2021 commentary on it (PMC7923648; the warning about false stories and the respect-versus-rationality distinction checked against the full text on 2026-10-21).
- **Strength:** moderate (15 studies, but outcome is respect and perceived rationality, mostly US online samples).
- **Passages:** abstract: opponents "respect moral beliefs more when they are supported by personal experiences".
  Experiences "issue-relevant and involve harm" work best, via perceived rationality. The authors "leave aside the
  question of persuasion". Commentary: "a false personal story will have more power to create respect than facts"
  (a warning that stories are exploitable).
- **Read in full on 2026-10-21** (Europe PMC copy, PMC8017692, PNAS 118(6):e2008389118; before that only the abstract and the commentary). Held now: the opponent in the vignettes and in Study 5
  (a real face-to-face talk about guns with 153 people stopped in public in North Carolina, a team member taking the opposing side) **based their own stance** on the experience or on facts.
  Study 7 (n = 273, guns): relevant personal experience beat facts and beat "nonrelevant personal experiences (i.e., had their business go into bankruptcy)"; "no significant difference between
  facts and nonrelevant personal experiences." Study 8 (n = 255): harm-based (used a gun to protect my family) beat nonharm personal experiences (took a firearm safety course) on rationality and
  respect, and "nonharm (but still relevant) experiences were significantly more effective ... compared with facts". Study 9 (n = 408): a "personalness gradient": they themselves suffered
  (respect M = 5.08), a friend or relative suffered (4.66), they read about someone who suffered (4.21). Study 13: doubt of the truth was highest "when opponents based their stance on facts in
  matters of morality/politics"; "personal experiences only have an advantage in moral disagreement - for agreement (whether moral or nonmoral) and for nonmoral disagreement, facts foster
  respect just as well (Study 13)" (the nonmoral topic was blender preferences). Study 15 (immigration, n = 1,412): a layperson's experience beat a scientist's research on respect. Authors:
  "we leave aside the question of persuasion"; "We speculate that personal experiences might be deployed early in conversations to first build a foundation of mutual respect, and then facts
  could be introduced"; "It is an open question of whether these results apply cross-culturally." Samples are mostly MTurk and a few representative samples; Study 12 (CNN/Fox transcripts)
  had a significant total effect on rationality but not on respect. Nothing in the paper tests length, a story told about someone else, or a non-judging manner.
- **Use:** explains why the clip's "pathos beats logos" has something to it: in a moral disagreement a story gets you a
  hearing that a statistic does not. It does not show the other person changed their mind. Teach honesty: share a true
  experience; do not invent one.

### F7. Receptive language and restating the other side first (Yeomans et al. 2020; Rapoport via Dennett)
- **Read:** the **full text** of Yeomans, Minson, Collins, Chen & Gino 2020, *OBHDP* 160:131-148 (author manuscript, Imperial College repository, read 2026-10-21; before that only the abstract);
  Dennett's four steps from Rapoport as quoted by The Marginalian (2014 article on *Intuition Pumps*; the book itself not read). **Correction found on reading the full text:** the "receptiveness
  recipe" (Study 4, 771 writers and 1,548 raters on MTurk) is four wording markers, "positive statements, rather than negations; explicit acknowledgement of understanding; finding points of
  agreement; and hedging to soften claims", and it is **not** a restatement of the other side's view. Writers given the recipe were rated by disagreeing readers as more receptive (beta .57),
  better future collaborators (.28) and their replies as more persuasive (.24, a -3 to +3 slider rating of a written reply, not a change of attitude); the recipe made the task feel harder.
  The unreceptive replies had more negations ("no", "wrong") and explanatory reasoning ("because", "why I"). "You" was a sign of understanding where speakers restated their partner's position
  (a post-hoc observation, with the authors' own caution about actions versus beliefs). The review section says "conflict resolution practitioners advise engaging partisans in an exercise of
  restating each other's positions" (Coltri 2010, second-hand) and that Chen, Minson & Tormala 2010 found an elaboration question made people more willing to talk with the counterpart again.
- **Strength:** weak to moderate. Yeomans: the abstract says a "receptiveness recipe" made writers rated better
  collaborators and messages "more convincing" (Study 4), and receptive language early predicts less later escalation.
  Rapoport's Rules are an expert's rule of thumb (Dennett: engaging first makes the other "a more receptive audience"),
  with no direct test found. **Judgement call:** I keep restating-before-disagreeing as a taught move because it is
  consistent with F5 (non-judgment) and Yeomans, and say in the lesson it is practice-based advice, not proven.
- **Passage (Dennett on Rapoport):** re-express the target's view "so clearly, vividly, and fairly" that they accept it;
  list agreements; say what you learned; only then rebut.

### F8. Asking someone to explain how it works can reduce certainty, in some cases (Fernbach et al. 2013; Sloman & Vives 2022; Meyer et al. 2024)
- **Read:** Sloman & Vives 2022, *Cognition* 225:105146 (full text, abstract + introduction); Meyer et al. 2024 *HKS
  Misinformation Review* (via the fetched article page: findings 1-3 and implications). Fernbach et al. 2013 original:
  not read (APS press summary seen only).
- **Strength:** weak to moderate. The original effect is contested.
- **Passages:** Sloman & Vives abstract: "Some attempts to replicate this reduction of polarization have been
  unsuccessful"; understanding and extremity fall after explanation "only for consequentialist issues, not those
  based on protected values". Meyer et al.: street epistemology has "only anecdotal support"; reflecting on reasons
  reduced belief on average in an online survey, yet "we did not observe significant effects among individuals with
  higher levels of conspiracy thinking".
- **Use:** "ask how that would work" and "what's your reason, how sure are you?" are worth teaching as questions
  that sometimes loosen a view, with the limits stated: they do not work on values the person treats as sacred or on
  deeply committed believers. Street epistemology as a brand is dropped (anecdote only); its core question is kept.

### F9. Aristotle's three means: character, emotion, argument (Rhetoric I.1-2, II.1-3)
- **Read (rewritten 2026-10-21 for lessons 4 and 5; the earlier version read only I.1-2):** *Rhetoric*, W. Rhys Roberts translation
  (MIT Internet Classics Archive, classics.mit.edu), full text of **Book I, parts 1 and 2, and Book II, parts 1, 2 and 3** (II.1: the
  speaker's character and the hearers' frame of mind, with the definition of the emotions; II.2: anger; II.3: growing calm). **Not read:**
  II.4-II.26 (the other emotions, the characters of hearers by age and fortune, the common lines of argument) and Book III (style and
  arrangement). Greek not read; one translation.
- **Strength:** the best-known account in the field, a philosopher's analysis of speeches to law courts, assemblies and ceremonies, built from
  observation and thought, not experiment. Moderate as a description of persuading; **not a tested method**, and applying it to a talk
  with a neighbor is our step, not his.
- **The three means (I.2):** persuasion by "the personal character of the speaker", by "putting the audience into a certain frame of mind", or
  by "the proof, or apparent proof, provided by the words of the speech itself". Character: "We believe good men more fully and more readily
  than others". It "should be achieved by what the speaker says, not by what people think of his character before he begins to speak"; his
  character "may almost be called the most effective means of persuasion". Frame of mind: judgements "are not the same" when "pleased and
  friendly" as when "pained and hostile".
- **What builds trust (II.1):** three things "inspire confidence in the orator's own character": "good sense, good moral character, and
  goodwill". The reasoning: false statements and bad advice come from want of good sense, from not saying what one thinks (moral badness), or
  from not being well disposed to the hearers. The lesson's sample lines for each (checking the numbers; saying what cuts against one's side;
  wanting it to work for them too) are **ours**, not Aristotle's.
- **Feelings (II.1-II.3):** the emotions "so change men as to affect their judgements" and come with pain or pleasure; friendly hearers regard the
  accused as having done little wrong, hostile hearers the opposite (II.1). Anger is a reaction to a slight, and people are angered by "those who laugh,
  mock, or jeer at us" (II.2). Anger settles toward "those who are serious when we are serious", toward "those who admit their fault and are
  sorry", and toward those who "do not insult or mock" (II.3). **Limit:** this is Aristotle's account of how to stir or settle a hearer; the
  lessons use only the two directions (slights anger, seriousness and an honest sorry calm) and call them an untested account.
- **Slight (II.2, reread 2026-10-21 for lesson 8):** "There are three kinds of slighting - contempt, spite, and insolence"; "The persons with whom we get angry are those who laugh, mock, or jeer at us,
  for such conduct is insolent"; also "those who speak ill of us, and show contempt for us, in connexion with the things we ourselves most care about"; and those who "oppose us though they are our
  inferiors" because "they seem to think us inferior to themselves". Lesson 8 uses only this: a line that mocks, jeers or shows contempt for the person is a slight. Calling someone "stupid" is
  contempt by this account; no claim is made about anyone's ability.
- **Argument (I.1-I.2):** the enthymeme (the short argument) "is, in general, the most effective of the modes of persuasion" (I.1) and is
  "the substance of rhetorical persuasion". It "must consist of few propositions"; a familiar fact need not be mentioned, "the hearer adds it
  himself" (I.2). Rhetoric addresses hearers who "cannot take in at a glance a complicated argument, or follow a long chain of reasoning";
  long chains "will necessarily be hard to follow owing to their length", and reasoning from premises "not generally admitted or believed" will
  "fail to win assent" (I.2). In I.1, before some audiences even the exactest knowledge does not make it easy to convince, because "there are people
  whom one cannot instruct", so use notions "possessed by everybody". The example (a like case used as proof) is "as persuasive" as the
  enthymeme, though enthymemes "excite the louder applause" (I.2). His own limit (I.1): "we must not make people believe what is wrong".
- **Correction of the earlier note (2026-10-21):** it said Aristotle "calls argument the core and character the strongest". He says both: the
  enthymeme is "in general, the most effective of the modes of persuasion" (I.1) and character "may almost be called the most effective" (I.2). He
  does not rank them, and the lessons do not. It also said stirring prejudice, pity or anger is "a mere appeal to the judge" without context: that
  is I.1 on law-court handbooks, and it adds that in a political debate the listener decides his own vital interests, so there is "no need to prove
  anything except that the facts are what the supporter of a measure maintains". II.1 then says the orator must also make his character look right
  and put his hearers in the right frame of mind.
- **Not claimed:** that a person's reply can be sorted into the three by Aristotle's method (that reading, and the rule "answer the one the reply
  is about", is ours and nobody has tested it; the lesson grades only the reading of the reply, not that the answer works); any tested way
  to build credibility; that the "example" of I.2 (a like case from the past, such as earlier tyrants who asked for a bodyguard) is the same thing as a
  personal story.
- **Use:** confirms that who is speaking, the hearer's state and the argument each shape the result, none above the others, and that the argument
  should be short and begin from what the hearer already grants.

### F10. Bonhoeffer on stupidity (letter "After Ten Years", in *Letters and Papers from Prison*)
- **Read:** a published English reprint (Substack and Open Culture copies; translator not named). Not the critical
  edition (DBWE 8). Quotes are therefore unverified against the edition.
- **Strength:** weak as evidence (an essay by a pastor in a dictatorship, not a study). Kept because the owner asked.
- **Passages:** "Against stupidity we are defenseless." The stupid person is "under a spell" and one deals with
  "slogans, catch-words" that "have taken possession of him". It is "perhaps less a psychological than a sociological
  problem" and "an act of liberation, not instruction" is the cure. A translation reads: facts that contradict a
  prejudgment "simply need not be believed".
- **Judgement call / correction:** Bonhoeffer's own claim is that this is a feature of people under group pressure, not of
  low intelligence, which F4 supports. But his "never again will we persuade with reasons" is an observation under
  1940s conditions; F2 and F5 show reasons and stories do move many people a little. Teach his idea as one real type
  (a person fused with a group position), not as a label for anyone who disagrees.

## 2. What did not make the cut (and why)
- **Pragma-dialectics (van Eemeren & Grootendorst's ten rules for a critical discussion):** only secondary
  descriptions read (rule 1: parties "must not prevent each other from presenting standpoints"). The model assumes both
  sides want to resolve the difference; it is a standard for a cooperative discussion, so it is used only as the
  F1 "persuasion/inquiry" ideal, not taught as ten rules.
- **Competitive debate pedagogy (Allen et al. 1999 meta-analysis on forensics and critical thinking):** only a
  secondary summary seen; and its skills (flowing, rebuttal order, speaking to a judge) belong to the "persuade a third
  party" type in F1, not to the owner's need. Not a lesson. **Judgement call.**
- **Deep canvassing by name:** the term is kept out of lessons; the move taught is the one with evidence (F5).
- **Cross-cultural (Canada vs US) norms:** nothing in the sources read speaks to it; all studies are US samples.
  Cases are set in everyday US situations (work, neighbours, relatives), no claim about cultural difference is made.
- **Ethos as a taught skill:** no tested "how to build credibility" was found. Lesson 4 teaches what Aristotle lists (II.1: good sense, good moral
  character, goodwill, shown in what is said), with sample lines of our own. **Judgement call.**

## 3. What learning research says for this kind of skill
The skill is judging a live situation and choosing a next move from memory: a case-judgment skill with a habit
in it. docs/learning-science.md: guidance and worked cases before solo practice; recall with an explanation after
every answer (P16, P19); contrast (P12), naming the wrong idea (P29), mixing then spacing (P22, P24); practice in the
form of real use (D6); feeling is not evidence (D4). Here that means short realistic exchanges where the learner picks the
type and the next reply, not definitions of terms.
