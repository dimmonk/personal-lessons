# Judging statistical claims: what the evidence says (2026-10-10)

Gate 1 of the `build-subject` skill for Statistical Claims. The kinds of learning are **judging situations** (does this
number show what it is used to claim) and a small **procedure** (turning a number into a plain size). The general
evidence in `docs/learning-science.md` assumed the task was classification with a key; this file collects evidence on
the task itself: reasoning about statistics in everyday claims, understanding risk numbers, reading charts, telling sound
claims from unsound ones. Each finding says what was read. Most are abstracts or search-returned summaries of them.

## Findings

**C1. Statistical rules can be taught so that people use them on everyday problems, near, and they fade.** Brief training
in the law of large numbers, by rule or by examples, improved reasoning about everyday problems, including untrained
areas (Fong, Krantz & Nisbett, 1986); the untrained-area benefit declined over two weeks but stayed above controls (Fong
& Nisbett, 1991; both via `docs/learning-science.md` P25). Graduate training in psychology and medicine produced large
gains in statistical and methodological reasoning about everyday events, including confounded variables (Lehman, Lempert
& Nisbett, 1988, *American Psychologist* 43, 431–443). *Read: abstract via search summary.* **Design:** each question is
taught as an explicit rule with varied everyday examples, tested in an area of life not used to teach it, and returned
after a delay.

**C2. Explicit instruction plus practice is needed; practice alone does not teach unbiased reasoning; worked examples
beat unaided problems for novices.** 141 economics students: only explicit instruction combined with practice improved
reasoning; prompts added nothing (Heijltjes, van Gog & Paas, 2014, *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 28, 518–530). Novices
learning to avoid biased reasoning learned more from worked examples than from low-assistance problems; interleaving
gave no advantage there (van Peppen et al., 2021, *Journal of Cognitive Psychology*). Far transfer to new kinds of
reasoning problem consistently failed, even with methods that transfer in other domains (van Peppen et al., 2021,
*Instructional Science*, gains held at 3 weeks and 9 months). *Read: abstracts via search summaries.* **Design:** every
question the learner will use is taught explicitly and shown worked before practice; the subject teaches the specific
problems real claims have, not "critical thinking".

**C3. A short list of concepts for judging claims is learnable and lasts.** Informed Health Choices: a cluster-randomised
trial in Ugandan primary schools taught 12 concepts for judging claims about treatments; after one year, 80% of children
in intervention schools passed versus 52% in control schools (mean 68.7% versus 53.0%) (Nsangi et al., 2020, *Trials*;
the end-of-term results were published in 2017). *Read: abstract via search summary; children, classroom, a teacher.*
**Design:** a small fixed set of questions, each with its own practice, is the right size for the subject.

**C4. Where real claims go wrong.** Over a third of 462 university press releases on health research contained one of
three exaggerations: a cause claimed from a correlation, people inferred from animal studies, or advice beyond the
evidence; exaggeration in the news was strongly associated with exaggeration in the release (Sumner et al., 2014, *BMJ*
349, g7015). Caveats did not reduce coverage. *Read: abstract via search summary.* **Design:** "cause or just a link",
"who was studied", and "the headline claims more than the study" are core parts, not extras.

**C5. Relative risk persuades without being understood better; natural frequencies are understood better.** Cochrane
review, 35 studies: relative risk reductions were understood no better than absolute ones (SMD 0.02) but were perceived
as larger and more persuasive (SMD 0.66); natural frequencies were understood better than percentages (SMD 0.69, for
diagnostic and screening tests); moderate-quality evidence, no behaviour outcomes (Akl et al., 2011, CD006776). *Read:
abstract via search summary.* **Design:** turning a number into "so many in 1,000 before, so many after" is a part of its
own, practised by doing it, and asked of every risk claim.

**C6. Teaching people to build the frequency picture beats teaching the formula.** A computer tutorial that trained people
to construct natural-frequency representations gave a higher immediate effect and more stable long-term learning than
training the rule with probabilities; transfer was similar (Sedlmeier & Gigerenzer, 2001, *JEP: General* 130, 380–400).
*Read: abstract via search summary.* **Design:** the size procedure is "picture 1,000 people", worked on real numbers,
not a formula to remember.

**C7. Knowing a chart trick does not stop it working on you.** Across five studies, a truncated y-axis made differences
look larger for about 83.5% of people; the effect persisted after people were warned and was not predicted by graph
literacy (Yang, Vargas Restrepo, Stanley & Marsh, 2021, *JARMAC* 10(2), 298–311). *Read: abstract via search summary.*
**Design:** chart practice is reading the axis numbers and working out the real difference, not naming the trick.

**C8. Practice that teaches "doubt" can make people doubt everything.** Reanalysed with signal detection, two
misinformation games raised "false" answers to all news, true and fake, without improving discrimination
(Modirrousta-Galian & Higham, 2023, *JEP: General* 152(9), 2411–2437); later work with feedback exercises, and a 2026
meta-analysis of 33 experiments, report improved discrimination without bias, so this is contested. *Read: abstract and
search summaries.* With the fraud result in `docs/learning-science.md` P26 (Burke et al., 2022). **Design:** every
practice set has claims that hold up; the learner's rate of rejecting sound claims is scored and shown, not only hits.

**C9. Whole tasks, simple to complex, with part practice for the recurring steps.** The four-component instructional
design model (whole-task practice from the start, supportive information, procedural information just in time,
part-task practice for recurrent skills): meta-analysis, d = 0.79 on performance, larger in higher education (Costa,
Miranda & Melo, 2022, *Learning Environments Research* 25(2), 445–463). *Read: abstract via search summary.*
**Design:** from the first lesson the learner judges whole real-format items; each lesson adds one question; the size
procedure gets its own drill.

**C10. General evidence that applies unchanged** (`docs/research/review-after-lessons.md` and `docs/learning-science.md`):
practice in the form of real use transfers best (R8, Pan & Rickard 2018); mixed practice for telling related problems
apart (R9, P22, and Sana, Yan & Kim 2017 on statistical concepts); feedback after each answer (R7, P19, P20); commit
before seeing the answer (P15); spaced returns with new cases, scheduled by the app (R1–R6, P24).

## What the evidence does not settle

- No study found tests adults alone on a phone judging real news items with numbers. The pilot (gate 6) and the
  learner's own end checks are the test.
- Whether the skill is used unprompted in daily life (the habit) is almost unmeasured; the evidence is for ability when
  asked.
- Lateral reading (checking a source on the web) was effective in a classroom trial (Wineburg et al., *Journal of
  Educational Psychology*, read only in press coverage) but is a different skill from judging the number itself.
