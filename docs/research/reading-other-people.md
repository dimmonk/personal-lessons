# Reading other people's behavior: what the evidence says (2026-10-10)

Gate 1 of the `build-subject` skill for Psychology. The kind of learning is judging situations (is this one bad moment
or a pattern, and does the word someone reached for fit what was done), with a habit attached (stopping to check before
the label in real life). The general evidence for judging and for review is in `docs/learning-science.md` (P12, P14,
P22, P25, P26, P29) and `docs/research/review-after-lessons.md` (R1 to R9); this file adds what those do not cover: the
errors people make reading others, and what is known to reduce them. Each finding says what was read; most are abstracts
and secondary summaries, and the strength is stated.

## Findings

**O1. The main error is reading a lasting trait from one act.** The correspondence bias: inferring someone's enduring
disposition from behavior that the situation could fully explain. Four causes: not seeing the situation's pressures,
unrealistic expectations of how people behave, "inflated" readings of the act itself, and correcting too little after a
first trait reading (Gilbert & Malone, 1995, *Psychological Bulletin* 117, 21–38). *Read: abstract and secondary
summaries.* **Design:** the core of every item is the step from "what they did" to "what it shows"; "one act, ordinary
reason" must be a frequent right answer, and the learner must name the situation that would explain it.

**O2. Words for harm and disorder have stretched to cover milder and different things.** "Concept creep": abuse,
bullying, trauma, mental disorder, addiction and prejudice have expanded outward (new kinds of thing) and downward (less
severe cases); the author judges the effects mixed, with a risk of pathologizing everyday experience (Haslam, 2016,
*Psychological Inquiry* 27, 1–17). *Read: summaries; the paper is open access and the argument is a review, not an
experiment.* **Design:** each popular label is taught with what it requires; the everyday look-alike it gets stretched
over is its contrast case.

**O3. "Be fair" does not reduce bias; a concrete step that makes the other possibility visible does.** Told to be
objective, people judged as biasedly as before; asked "would I judge this the same if it had come out the other way?",
the bias disappeared, in judging evidence and in testing an impression of a person (Lord, Lepper & Preston, 1984,
*JPSP* 47, 1231–1243). Consistent with medical-diagnosis trials where "be careful" changed nothing (learning-science P14).
*Read: abstract and secondary summaries.* **Design:** every judgment item has a fixed step "what else would explain
this, and what would you need to see?", answered, not a warning card.

**O4. Imagining the other person's view makes you more confident, not more accurate; asking them does.** Across 25
experiments, instructions to take the other's perspective did not improve accuracy about their thoughts and preferences
and raised confidence; getting the perspective (asking) did (Eyal, Steffel & Epley, 2018, *JPSP* 114(4), 547–571).
*Read: abstract and secondary coverage; the size of the asking benefit is from one summary.* **Design:** "I can't tell
yet; ask them" is a right answer, and "ask" is one of the next steps practiced.

**O5. Most people called a narcissist do not have the disorder.** DSM-5 gives 0 to 6.2% for narcissistic personality
disorder in community samples; the 6.2% lifetime figure is the high end, from one US survey (NESARC Wave 2, Stinson et
al., 2008, *J Clin Psychiatry*), and DSM-IV said under 1%. A personality disorder is by definition a lasting pattern
across many situations. *Read: secondary summaries.* **Design:** a "who someone is" label needs many situations over
time; one incident, or one relationship told by one side, never earns it.

**O6. The costly miss is a pattern of control, and it is told apart by pattern, not by one fight.** Johnson's typology
separates violence that grows out of a specific conflict from violence embedded in a general pattern of coercive
control (monitoring, isolation, money control, intimidation, threats), which escalates and is more dangerous; fear
levels differ between the two (summaries of Johnson, 2008, and later studies; critics note controlling relationships
without violence fall outside it). *Read: summaries.* **Design:** control cases are in the mix, and are told the way
they usually arrive: minimized ("he just likes to know where I am"); the decisive feature is several kinds of control
together, repeated.

**O7. Knowing a tactic changes how observers judge it.** Students who read a vignette where the accused used DARVO
(deny, attack, reverse victim and offender) judged the victim less believable; a short explanation of DARVO beforehand
reduced that effect (Harsey & Freyd, 2020, *J. Aggression, Maltreatment & Trauma*, two experiments, 316 and 360
students). *Read: abstract via summary; fictional vignettes, students.* **Design:** a few named tactics are worth
teaching where the name protects the learner from being misled; not a long catalogue.

**O8. Bias-reduction training works best as practice on instances with personal feedback, and holds for months.** One
session of a game with practice and feedback reduced bias blind spot, confirmation bias and the fundamental attribution
error (the same thing as O1) immediately and two months later, more than a video, including in untaught formats
(Morewedge et al., 2015, *Policy Insights from the Behavioral and Brain Sciences*). *Read: abstract and summaries;
one research group, lab tasks.* Transfer to real life is the main open problem (Ludolph & Schulz, 2018, in
learning-science P25). **Design:** the subject is practice on many accounts with feedback, not cards about biases.

**O9. Accuracy about other people can be trained in ordinary adults; how is trained matters more than how long.**
Meta-analysis of training studies in nonclinical adults: training raised person-perception accuracy across domains, and
training approach mattered more than length (Blanch-Hartigan, Andrzejewski & Hill, 2012, *Basic and Applied Social
Psychology* 34(6), 483–498). *Read: abstract snippet only; effect size and the practice and feedback comparison not
seen.* **Design:** limited support that the skill is trainable at all; the design leans on O8 and the review evidence.

## What the evidence does not settle
- No study was found that trains adults to check popular labels ("narcissist", "gaslighting") against described
  behavior. The pilot and the end checks are the test.
- Whether practice on written accounts carries over to the learner's own heated moments. The own-case step and the
  optional if-then plan (learning-science P26) are inferences, not tested aids.
- What each popular label "really" requires is partly a matter of definition. Where a clinical or research definition
  exists (personality disorder, coercive control, DARVO) the lesson uses it and names the source; where none does
  ("toxic"), the lesson says so and asks for the behavior instead.
