# Research: how strategists reason (subject `strategy`, field `business`)

Written 2026-10-22 for build-subject step 2. Every finding has an id (ST), its source, what was read, its strength, and
the passage it rests on (quoted or closely paraphrased, with page or section). "Read" means the text itself; "via
quotations" means the book was not open to me and the line was taken from two or more independent quotations of it,
which is weaker and is said each time. A choice with no passage is marked **judgement call**.

Strength words: **strong** (the field's canonical, widely taught position, read in the original, or a replicated
empirical result); **moderate** (one good empirical study, or a canonical text read only via quotations); **limited**
(one practitioner's argument, or a single small study).

## A. What strategy is, and what it is not

**ST1. Strategy is being different on purpose; doing the same things better is operational effectiveness, which is
necessary but is not strategy.** Porter, M. E. (1996), "What Is Strategy?", *Harvard Business Review* 74(6). Read in
full (HBR reprint copy; page labels below are the reprint's). Strong (one of the field's canonical articles; its distinction is the line the IESE core draws
between strategic and operational problems, ST16).
- p. 38: "Operational effectiveness (OE) means performing similar activities better than rivals perform them ... In
  contrast, strategic positioning means performing different activities from rivals' or performing similar activities
  in different ways."
- p. 38: "A company can outperform rivals only if it can establish a difference that it can preserve. It must deliver
  greater value to customers or create comparable value at a lower cost, or do both."
- p. 39: "Competitive strategy is about being different. It means deliberately choosing a different set of activities
  to deliver a unique mix of value."
- p. 39 (section I): managers "have let operational effectiveness supplant strategy. The result is zero-sum
  competition, static or declining prices, and pressures on costs."
- Novice error it names: treating best practice, benchmarking, TQM, outsourcing as the strategy (p. 38-39). Best
  practice is copied, so it does not last ("The productivity frontier is constantly shifting outward", p. 38).

**ST2. A position lasts only if it carries trade-offs: choosing what not to do.** Porter (1996), section III "A
Sustainable Strategic Position Requires Trade-offs". Read. Strong.
- p. 44: "a trade-off means that more of one thing necessitates less of another. An airline can choose to serve
  meals ... or it can choose not to, but it cannot do both without bearing major inefficiencies."
- p. 45: "Strategy is making trade-offs in competing. The essence of strategy is choosing what not to do. Without
  trade-offs, there would be no need for choice and thus no need for strategy. Any good idea could and would be quickly
  imitated."
- The look-alike error, **straddling**, p. 44: "The straddler seeks to match the benefits of a successful position while
  maintaining its existing position." Worked case: Continental Lite copied Southwest on some routes while staying
  full-service, kept travel agents and a mixed fleet, and lost (p. 44-45). Added 2026-10-23 (research review, defect 1):
  on the Lite routes Continental did cut meals and first class, raised departures, lowered fares and shortened turns at the
  gate, but kept the travel agents, the mixed fleet, baggage checking and seat assignments the rest of the airline needed,
  so it paid for both ways; its losses ran to hundreds of millions of dollars (Porter p. 44-45, as the reviewer read it;
  the 1995 press confirms the meals were cut). Porter's test is that the straddler's activities **conflict** with each
  other (p. 44: "more of one thing necessitates less of another"), not that it gives nothing up; lesson 6 asks whether the
  new work pulls against the old (judgement call on the plain wording).
- p. 45: companies that try "to be all things to all customers ... risk confusion in the trenches as employees attempt
  to make day-to-day operating decisions without a clear framework."
- Added 2026-10-23 (round-2 research review, D17). p. 43-44, where trade-offs come from: "Trade-offs arise for three
  reasons." First, inconsistencies in image or reputation (a company known for one kind of value loses credibility when it
  offers another); second, the activities themselves (different positions need different product configurations,
  equipment, employee behavior, skills and management systems); third, limits on internal coordination and control.
  Lesson 6's pull covers all three: the same people, hours or room (the second), or customers who would stop believing the
  promise (the first); the third is left to the strategist and not taught (judgement call on scope).
- Added 2026-10-23 (round-2 research review, D4): the Southwest of these pages is the 1990s airline. Southwest began
  charging for checked bags on 28 May 2025 and assigning seats on flights from 27 January 2026 (Southwest's own
  announcements, as the reviewer reported them; not re-read in this pass). Lessons 2 and 6 date the story "in the 1990s";
  Vanguard (ST3) is likewise told in the past tense, as Porter described it then, since today most of its funds are bought
  through other brokerages and it advertises (the reviewer's D5).

**ST3. The test of a position is fit: activities that are consistent with it, reinforce each other, and optimize
together, so a rival must copy the whole system, not one piece.** Porter (1996), section IV "Fit Drives Both Competitive
Advantage and Sustainability". Read. Strong.
- p. 46: "First-order fit is simple consistency between each activity (function) and the overall strategy." Vanguard:
  low turnover, no star managers, direct distribution, little advertising, bonuses tied to cost savings.
- p. 46-47: "Second-order fit occurs when activities are reinforcing" (Neutrogena); "Third-order fit goes beyond
  activity reinforcement to ... optimization of effort" (The Gap restocking daily from three warehouses).
- Activity-system maps of IKEA, Vanguard and Southwest (exhibits, p. 42-49) are the field's standard picture of a
  strategy.

**ST4. Growth for its own sake erodes a position; the fix is to find the core of uniqueness and trim around it.**
Porter (1996), section V "Rediscovering Strategy". Read. Strong (as the field's canonical warning; the Maytag numbers
are Porter's).
- p. 51, "The Growth Trap": "Among all other influences, the desire to grow has perhaps the most perverse effect on
  strategy. Trade-offs and limits appear to constrain growth."
- p. 51-52: Maytag grew from $684 million (1985) to $3.4 billion (1994) by extending its line and buying brands, while
  return on sales fell from 8%-12% in the 1970s and 1980s to an average of under 1% for 1989-1995 (corrected 2026-10-23
  after the research review: under 1% is the 1989-1995 average, not the 1995 figure). "Profits fall, but more revenue is
  seen as the answer."
- p. 51, box "Reconnecting with Strategy", the questions an established firm asks first: "Which of our product or
  service varieties are the most distinctive? ... the most profitable? Which of our customers are the most satisfied?
  Which customers, channels, or purchase occasions are the most profitable? Which of the activities in our value chain
  are the most different and effective?" And: "A small percentage of varieties or customers may well account for most of
  a company's sales and especially its profits."
- This box is the most directly usable passage for an owner-operator: it is a diagnosis an owner can run on their own
  books.

**ST5. A strategy has a kernel: a diagnosis of the challenge, a guiding policy, and coherent actions. A goal is not a
strategy.** Rumelt, R. P. (2011), *Good Strategy/Bad Strategy*, Crown; and Rumelt (2011), "The perils of bad strategy",
*McKinsey Quarterly*. **Via quotations** (the book was not open to me; the McKinsey page timed out on three tries); the
lines below agree across several independent quotation sources. Moderate (canonical text, secondhand reading).
- Ch. 5, "The Kernel of Good Strategy": "A good strategy has an essential logical structure that I call the kernel."
  "A diagnosis defines or explains the nature of the challenge." "The guiding policy is an overall approach chosen to
  cope with or overcome the obstacles identified in the diagnosis." Coherent actions "are not just 'implementation' ...
  they are the substance of the strategy." "The kernel does not spell out a long list of things to do. It is not a plan."
- "Good strategy is not just what you are trying to do. It is a cohesive response to a challenge."
- Ch. 3, "Bad Strategy", four hallmarks: fluff ("a form of gibberish masquerading as strategic concepts or arguments"),
  failure to face the challenge ("If you fail to identify and analyze the obstacles, you don't have a strategy. Instead,
  you have a stretch goal or a budget or a list of things you wish would happen"), mistaking goals for strategy ("Many
  bad strategies are just statements of desire rather than plans for overcoming obstacles"), and bad strategic
  objectives ("a 'dog's dinner' of everything everyone wants"). p. 36 (as cited in a review): "Bad strategy is long on
  goals and short on policy or action."
- "Template-style strategic planning" (fill in vision, mission, values) "is the enemy of strategy."
- Focus: "Good strategy works by harnessing power and applying it where it will have the greatest effect." From *The
  Crux* (2022): the crux is "that one pivotal issue you can actually do something about".
- Novice errors this names, which Steven's ask ("move a business to the next level") invites: a revenue target as the
  plan; a list of initiatives with no shared logic; skipping the diagnosis.

## B. Where the money comes from (the diagnosis experts run)

**ST6. An industry's profit is set by five forces, not by how fast it grows or how new it is; dig into the strongest
force, do not list all five.** Porter, M. E. (2008), "The Five Competitive Forces That Shape Strategy", *HBR* 86(1),
reprint R0801E. Read in full (reprint copy; page labels are the reprint's). Strong.
- p. 2: "the job of the strategist is to understand and cope with competition. Often, however, managers define
  competition too narrowly, as if it occurred only among today's direct competitors. Yet competition for profits goes
  beyond established industry rivals to include four other competitive forces as well: customers, suppliers, potential
  entrants, and substitute products."
- p. 3: "Industry structure drives competition and profitability, not whether an industry produces a product or service,
  is emerging or mature, high tech or low tech, regulated or unregulated."
- p. 10, the novice error: "A common mistake is to assume that fast-growing industries are always attractive ... high
  growth with low entry barriers will draw in entrants ... A narrow focus on growth is one of the major causes of bad
  strategy decisions."
- p. 16, box "Common Pitfalls": "Making lists instead of engaging in rigorous analysis"; "Paying equal attention to all of
  the forces rather than digging deeply into the most important ones"; "Confusing effect (price sensitivity) with cause
  (buyer economics)"; "Confusing cyclical or transient changes with true structural changes"; "Using the framework to
  declare an industry attractive or unattractive rather than using it to guide strategic choices."
- p. 12-13, positioning against the forces: "Strategy can be viewed as building defenses against the competitive forces
  or finding a position in the industry where the forces are weakest." Worked case: Paccar chose owner-operator truck
  drivers, who have little clout as buyers and are less price sensitive, and earns a 10% price premium.
- Added 2026-10-23 (lessons 1-5 review): the same passage describes heavy trucks as hard on several forces at once:
  powerful buyers (large fleets and leasing companies) with leverage on price, stiff price competition and fierce rivalry
  in downturns, and unions with "considerable supplier power". **Via the research reviewer's search on the passage**
  (docs/subjects/strategy/review-research-l1-5.md, defect 12); my own search found only secondary write-ups, so lesson 4
  uses only the buyers, the rivalry and Paccar's answer, not the unions. Moderate.
- Added 2026-10-23 (round-2 research review, D16). The same article treats a middleman as a buyer: "Intermediate customers,
  or customers who purchase but are not the end user (such as assemblers or distribution channels), can be analyzed the same
  way as other buyers, with one important addition": they gain bargaining power when they can sway the choices of the
  customers downstream (passage as the reviewer quoted it; I did not re-open the reprint in this pass). Lesson 4 counts a
  delivery app or a chain store that brings the customers as a buyer.
- Added 2026-10-23 (round-2 research review, D3). US drug wholesaling is concentrated: three wholesalers (McKesson, Cencora,
  Cardinal Health) hold over 90% of drug distribution (Morningstar and IBISWorld, as the reviewer cited them; not re-read).
  Lesson 4's pharmacy report says so, and keeps suppliers weak only because the three compete for each pharmacy's account
  and a pharmacy can move between them within a week (judgement call on the made town).
- Teaching consequence (judgement call on scope): the learner needs the five forces as a *question to ask* ("who is
  squeezing the profit out of this business, and why?"), not the full industry analysis an MBA writes.

**ST7. Value is created between what customers would pay and what suppliers (including staff) would accept; a firm keeps
at most what it adds that others cannot.** Brandenburger, A. M. & Stuart, H. W. (1996), "Value-based business strategy",
*Journal of Economics & Management Strategy* 5(1), 5-24. **Abstract only** (via search records of Columbia's and
EconBiz's pages; the full text was not open). Strong as the field's standard model (it is the base of the "value
creation, value capture" frame in MBA cores, ST18), moderate for my reading of it.
- Abstract, paraphrased from two records: the paper defines the value created by a firm with its suppliers and buyers,
  defines the firm's "added value", and argues that under some conditions added value is an upper bound on what the firm
  can capture; positive added value needs asymmetries between the firm and others, and it names four "value-based"
  strategies for creating them.
- The teachable form is the "value stick" of Oberholzer-Gee (2021), *Better, Simpler Strategy* (HBR Press), which an HBS
  professor built on this model for the HBS strategy course. **Via publisher text and a review**: willingness to pay
  (WTP) at the top, willingness to sell (WTS, what suppliers and employees need to take part) at the bottom; the length
  between them is the value created; winning firms raise customers' WTP or lower suppliers' WTS, not only cut price.
  Limited (secondhand), but the arithmetic is simple and checkable: value created = WTP - WTS; the customer's share =
  WTP - price; the firm's margin = price - cost; the supplier's share = cost - WTS.
- Teaching consequence: this is the one place in the subject where numbers decide, and the app's `number` asks and
  generators fit it.

**ST8. An established business finds its strategy in its own numbers first: which products, customers and channels are
most distinctive and most profitable.** Porter (1996), p. 51, quoted at ST4. Strong for being the field's advice; the
"small share of varieties makes most of the profit" line is Porter's observation, not a measured law (do not teach a
number such as 80/20 as fact; judgement call).

**ST19. Most of a firm's profit comes from a small share of its customers, and the losing ones take a large part of it
back; the field shows this as cumulative profit against the share of customers (the "whale curve"), where the top lines
reach well over 100% of total profit before the losers bring it down.** Kaplan, R. S. & Narayanan, V. G. (2001),
"Measuring and Managing Customer Profitability", *Journal of Cost Management* 15(5), 5-15. **Via secondary sources only**
(Wikipedia's "Customer profitability analysis", which states the most profitable 20% of customers "generate between 150
percent and 300 percent of total profits", and practitioner pages citing the same paper; the article itself was not open).
Limited to moderate. Teaching consequence for lesson 5 (judgement call on the denominator, now sourced): a line's share is
counted of the total profit of all the lines, losses included, so the share can pass 100% and shows what the losing lines
cost; the same share is shown in the learner's own work. Porter's box (ST4) gives the questions; this gives the arithmetic.
- Added 2026-10-23 (reviews of all fourteen lessons, what to do with a losing line): Kaplan, R. S. (2005), "A Balanced Scorecard
  Approach To Measure Customer Profitability", *HBS Working Knowledge*, 8 August 2005. **Read** (the page). The order is: first
  "improve its internal processes to lower the cost-to-serve", since "we can't expect customers to pay for our inefficiencies";
  then reprice ("Customized pricing policies should be at the heart of any strategy to manage customer profitability"); the
  insurer in his example "was prepared to drop the accounts" only if those failed. Moderate (the author's own practitioner
  summary of the same work). A practitioner list (Cokins, G. (2015), "Measuring and Managing Customer Profitability",
  CustomerThink, 11 February 2015, read) gives the same options: lower each customer's costs to serve, reprice or surcharge
  costly activities, and abandon unprofitable customers. Teaching consequence for lesson 5: a losing line is first asked
  whether a price or cost-to-serve change could make it pay; only if not is it judged by its tie to the core.

**ST9. An advantage lasts only if the thing behind it is valuable, rare, hard to copy and has no ready substitute.**
Barney, J. (1991), "Firm Resources and Sustained Competitive Advantage", *Journal of Management* 17(1), 99-120.
**Abstract via search records only.** Strong as canonical (the founding paper of the resource-based view); moderate for my
reading. The abstract names "four empirical indicators of the potential of firm resources to generate sustained
competitive advantage: value, rareness, imitability, and substitutability."
- Helmer, H. (2016), *7 Powers*, **via quotations** (limited): "Power" is "the set of conditions creating the potential
  for persistent differential returns", and each power needs both a **benefit** (higher price, lower cost or less
  capital) and a **barrier** that stops rivals from copying it. The benefit-and-barrier pair is the same test as Barney's
  and Porter's fit (ST3) in plainer words, which is why the plan uses it: "what do you get, and what stops a rival from
  getting it too?"
- Added 2026-10-23 (research review, defect 9). Helmer names seven powers: scale economies, network economies,
  counter-positioning, switching costs, branding, cornered resource and process power; switching costs are the loss a
  customer takes by moving to a rival, and branding's barrier is the long time it takes to build a reputation (**via
  summaries**, limited). Barney (1991) gives as reasons a resource is hard to copy unique history (path dependence),
  causal ambiguity and social complexity, such as reputation and culture (**via summaries of the paper's body**, moderate).
  Lesson 8 keeps four common answers for an owner-run business (only we have it, which is a cornered resource; fit,
  ST3; what customers would lose by leaving, their setup and records or trust built over years, which joins switching
  costs and a reputation; or nothing) and says the list is not complete (scale and networks named as others). Joining
  switching costs and reputation into one answer is a **judgement call** to keep the choice short.

## C. Choosing

**ST10. Strategy is an integrated set of choices: where to play and how to win are one choice, and the common failures
are failures to choose.** Lafley, A. G. & Martin, R. L. (2013), *Playing to Win*, HBR Press. **Via an excerpt** (Farnam
Street's excerpt of the six traps) and several summaries. Moderate (canonical practitioner book; secondhand).
- "Remember, strategy is choice." "Strategy is the answer to all five questions in the choice cascade" (winning
  aspiration, where to play, how to win, capabilities, management systems). "Where to play is your choice. Pick
  somewhere you can have a chance to win."
- The six traps, quoted from the excerpt: do-it-all ("failing to make choices, and making everything a priority"), Don
  Quixote ("taking on the strongest competitor first, head-to-head"), Waterloo ("starting wars on multiple fronts with
  multiple competitors at the same time"), something-for-everyone ("attempting to capture all consumer or channel or
  geographic or category segments at once"), dreams-that-never-come-true (aspirations and mission statements that
  "never get translated into concrete where-to-play and how-to-win choices"), program-of-the-month ("settling for generic
  industry strategies").
- The traps restate ST2 (trade-offs), ST5 (goals are not strategy) and ST6 (generic best practice) from a practitioner;
  three independent sources agreeing is why the plan treats "failure to choose" as the subject's central novice error.

**ST11. Before analysing, state each option and ask what would have to be true for it to work; test the least likely
condition first.** Lafley, Martin, Rivkin & Siggelkow (2012), "Bringing Science to the Art of Strategy", *HBR* 90(9).
**Via summaries only** (HBR and Rotman pages were refused). Limited to moderate. Summarized method: frame the choice;
generate possibilities; specify what would have to be true for each; identify the barriers (the conditions the team
most doubts); design tests; run them; choose the possibility with the fewest serious barriers. "Only then does it
unleash analysis." Teaching consequence: an `order` ask on the steps and a `choose` ask on which condition to test first.

## D. Judging the bet

**ST12. Executives' forecasts are optimistic because they take the inside view; the fix is the outside view (how did
similar ventures turn out?) and planning for rivals' replies.** Kahneman, D. & Lovallo, D. (1993), "Timid choices and bold
forecasts", *Management Science* 39(1), 17-31, **abstract read**; Lovallo, D. & Kahneman, D. (2003), "Delusions of
Success", *HBR* 81(7), **opening and summary read** (body paywalled), and Flyvbjerg's (2003) published reply. Strong for the
bias (the planning fallacy is among the best-replicated findings in judgement research), moderate for the remedy in
business settings.
- 1993 abstract: "Decision makers have a strong tendency to consider problems as unique"; "Overly optimistic forecasts
  result from the adoption of an inside view of the problem", which anchors on the plan and its scenarios and neglects
  past statistics.
- 2003 opening: Oxford Health's systems project ran late, the stock fell 63%, "destroying more than $3 billion in
  shareholder value in a single day"; the summary: most major initiatives (mergers, capital investments, market entries)
  fail to meet expectations. Flyvbjerg's reply summarises the article's four topics as "overoptimism, anchoring,
  competitor neglect, and the outside view".
- Lovallo, Clarke & Camerer (2012), "Robust analogizing and the outside view", *SMJ* 33(5), 496-512, **abstract read**:
  with private-equity investors, forming a reference class of many analogies beat relying on the few the decision maker
  already knew; with film revenues, similarity-based forecasting beat regression. Moderate (two studies).
- Novice error: judging a plan by how convincing its story is, and assuming rivals will not answer.

**ST20. The base rate for a new US business: about six in ten new establishments are still open three years after they
open, and about half after five.** US Bureau of Labor Statistics, Business Employment Dynamics, "Establishment age and
survival data", Table 7, "Survival of private sector establishments by opening year", Total Private
(https://www.bls.gov/bdm/us_age_naics_00_table7.txt, read 2026-10-23). Strong (official count of every private
establishment in the unemployment-insurance records).
- Opening year ended March 2013: 628,840 opened; survival rate since birth 79.6% after one year, 68.9% after two, 61.4% after
  three, 55.3% after four, 50.6% after five (318,339 still open in March 2018).
- The same table for other years: opened 1995, 59.8% after three years and 48.8% after five; opened 2005, 60.2% and 46.8%;
  opened 2018, 62.1% and 51.9%.
- The research reviewer's BLS papers (2004, 2006: about two-thirds after two years, 44% after four) agree
  (docs/subjects/strategy/review-research-l1-14.md, defect 6).
- Teaching consequence: lesson 12's made tables of new businesses use three years and keep the share still open between about
  52% and 70% (invented counts set near this record), so the lesson never repeats the myth that most new businesses close
  within a few years; lesson 13 uses "about half are still open after five years" as the wider record a new idea starts
  from. An establishment is one location, so these are shops and offices, close to the owner's "a new shop".
- Made-case prices kept current (added 2026-10-23, round-2 research review, D20): a budget gym's basic plan is about $15 a
  month (Planet Fitness's classic plan rose from $10 to $15 in 2024, as the reviewer reported), so the made gyms charge $15.

**ST13. Strategists reason by analogy most of the time; good ones test an analogy on its cause, not its surface.** Gavetti,
G. & Rivkin, J. W. (2005), "How Strategists Really Think: Tapping the Power of Analogy", *HBR* 83(4), 54-63.
**Introduction read; body paywalled.** Moderate for the descriptive claim, limited for the remedy as I could read it.
- Introduction: "Faced with an unfamiliar problem or opportunity, senior managers often think back to some similar
  situation they have seen or heard about"; reasoning by analogy "plays a role in strategic decision making that is large
  but largely overlooked"; "managers rarely realize that they're reasoning by analogy."
- Gavetti, Levinthal & Rivkin (2005), *SMJ* 26(8), 691-712, abstract via search record: analogy helps transfer wisdom
  from similar settings, but following an analogy too orthodoxly can be dysfunctional.
- Teaching consequence (judgement call, consistent with ST12's reference class): when a case says "it worked for X", the
  learner asks whether the reason it worked for X holds here.
- **When a rival answers (judgement call, added 2026-10-23 after the round-2 research review, D15).** Lesson 12's rule: a rival
  answers a move that takes customers it serves when it can follow without changing how it runs; its past behaviour is a
  clue, not the test. No source states it in these words. It is built from ST2 (without trade-offs "any good idea could and
  would be quickly imitated", p. 45; a rival does not follow when following would make it give up its own position, the
  Continental Lite story) and Helmer's counter-positioning (ST9: an incumbent does not copy a newcomer's model when copying
  would damage its existing business; via summaries, limited). Lovallo and Kahneman's competitor neglect (ST12) supports
  only that the rival's answer must be weighed.

**ST14. Expert entrepreneurs, unlike MBA students, start from the means they have, risk only what they can afford to
lose, build with partners, and distrust market forecasts when the future is unknowable.** Dew, N., Read, S., Sarasvathy,
S. D. & Wiltbank, R. (2009), "Effectual versus predictive logics in entrepreneurial decision-making: Differences between
experts and novices", *Journal of Business Venturing* 24(4), 287-309. **Read** (abstract, executive summary, Table 1).
Moderate (one think-aloud study, 27 experts with over 15 years and several companies founded, 37 MBA students; a small
replication found most results similar).
- Abstract: experts "identify more potential markets, focus more on building the venture as a whole, pay less attention
  to predictive information, worry more about making do with resources on hand to invest only what they could afford to
  lose, and emphasize stitching together networks of partnerships; while novices use a 'predictive frame' and tend to 'go
  by the textbook.'"
- p. 288: the MBA students "picked target segments based on predictive information given to them"; the experts
  "under-weighted, ignored and even explicitly argued against taking predictions seriously".
- Scope: this is about new ventures under real uncertainty. It complements ST12, it does not contradict it: where the
  past is a guide (a known kind of project), use the outside view; where the market does not exist yet, cap the bet at an
  affordable loss and learn by doing. That reconciliation is a **judgement call** of this note.

**ST15. Experienced executives sort messy business problems faster and into more, context-specific categories than MBA
students.** Day, D. V. & Lord, R. G. (1992), "Expertise and problem categorization", *Journal of Management Studies*
29(1), 35-47. **Abstract via search record.** Limited (one study: 38 machine-tool CEOs, 30 MBA students). Executives
sorted ill-structured problems more quickly, used a wider range of categories and drew on more problem information.
Teaching consequence: the first expert move is to name what kind of problem this is (ST16).

**ST16. The first question on any "strategic" problem is whether it is strategic at all, or a gap in running the business
well.** IESE MBA core syllabus, "Competitive Strategy" (Valentini, 2018), read in full (5 pages). Moderate (one top
school's core, consistent with Porter's OE line, ST1). The final project brief: a strategic problem "will require at least
a partial re-thinking of how a firm creates and captures value in the long term"; "make sure the problem is caused by the
firm's erroneous strategy or by competitors, rather than simply lacking operational excellence"; it names four kinds:
performance gaps, opportunity gaps, market entry, sustainability threats.

**ST17. Accurate grasp of a few key cause-and-effect principles is enough to perform well; complete knowledge is not
needed.** Gary, M. S. & Wood, R. E. (2011), "Mental models, decision rules, and performance heterogeneity", *SMJ* 32(6),
569-594. **Abstract via search record.** Moderate (experiment in a management simulation). The abstract: more accurate
mental models led to better decision rules and higher performance, and "accurate mental models of the key principles are
sufficient to achieve superior performance". Supports teaching a short way of thinking well rather than many frameworks.

## E. What MBA strategy cores teach (scope check)

**ST18. The core course is value creation, value capture, and making the advantage last, taught through cases.** IESE core
syllabus (read, ST16): "a coherent framework of Value Creation, Value Capture and Sustainability of Competitive
Advantage"; modules: introduction, industry and the firm's position, activities and capabilities, competitive dynamics;
"Strategic thinking is best learned through practice and use of strategic concepts"; corporate strategy and execution
are electives, not core. Oberholzer-Gee's HBS book is "based on [his] strategy course" (publisher text). Porter's two
articles are reprinted in core packs (Cornell's course-materials listing for the 2008 article). Moderate (one syllabus
read in full; the others by listing). I could not open Wharton, Stanford or MIT syllabi (refused or redirected). This
matches the brief's line: corporate finance, accounting, marketing, operations and negotiation are their own subjects.

## F. Is there a single method? (synthesis)

No single procedure is shared by the field; Porter, Rumelt, Lafley and Martin, and the value-based school each give a
frame, and practitioners mix them. But they **agree on a sequence of questions**, and that agreement is what the subject
teaches (judgement call on the order, built from the sources named):
1. Is this a strategy problem, or a running-the-business problem? (ST1, ST16)
2. What is the challenge, said plainly? A target or a wish is not a diagnosis. (ST5)
3. Where does this business make its money, and who takes the rest? Value created and captured; the force squeezing
   profit; the most profitable customers and products. (ST6, ST7, ST8)
4. What do we do differently, what does it give us, and what stops a rival copying it? (ST1, ST2, ST3, ST9)
5. The choice: where to play and how to win, what we will not do, and actions that fit together. (ST2, ST3, ST5, ST10)
6. Before committing: what would have to be true; how did similar bets turn out; how will rivals answer; can we afford to
   lose it? (ST11, ST12, ST13, ST14)

**Rumelt's kernel and Porter's line, reconciled (judgement call, 2026-10-23).** A plan can have the kernel's shape
(a challenge, one approach, steps that fit) and still be a plan to run the business better: "food comes out cold because
tickets have no order; we fix the order". Rumelt's test checks the shape; Porter's and IESE's question 1 checks whether
the challenge needs a strategy at all. The subject teaches the shape first (lesson 1) with a stated limit, that a
challenge which is only doing the same work worse than rivals is a running problem, and lesson 2 puts the two
side by side. So neither source is contradicted: a strategy is a kernel whose challenge is a strategic one.

The novice errors every source names, which the lessons refute (P29): a goal or wish mistaken for a strategy (ST5,
ST10); doing things better mistaken for strategy (ST1, ST16); growth for its own sake (ST4, ST6); trying to serve
everyone or copy the leader while keeping your own model, straddling (ST2, ST10); a fast-growing market taken as a good
one (ST6); a list of forces or initiatives instead of a reasoned choice (ST5, ST6); the inside view and forgetting rivals
(ST12); an analogy taken on its surface (ST13).

## G. How this kind of judgement is learned (the app's learning research applied)

Strategy here is **judging cases**: reading a messy business situation and naming what kind it is and which move fits.
That is the app's classification task (learning-science.md section 2), with one procedure inside it (the value
arithmetic, ST7).
- **Case method.** Business schools teach strategy by case discussion (ST18). I found no controlled evidence that case
  discussion beats other teaching for novices; the discussion needs a teacher and peers this app lacks. What transfers
  from the evidence is narrower: guided worked cases before solo practice (P10, D5), and **comparing two cases** that
  share a principle, which in adult management students nearly tripled later use of the principle in live negotiation
  (Loewenstein, Thompson & Gentner 1999; Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson 2003; P12). So each lesson pairs two cases with
  the same deciding feature on different surfaces, and puts look-alikes side by side.
- **Name the wrong idea** (P29): each novice error in section F is stated as the owner would put it, marked wrong, and
  corrected.
- **Practice in the form of use** (P25, D6): cases look like decisions an owner faces (an offer to expand, a big
  customer's demand, a rival's price cut), answered with the move an owner would make. A few well-known company cases
  where the source teaches the reasoning (Southwest, Continental Lite, Maytag, Paccar, Vanguard, IKEA), as Steven asked.
- **Mixed, then spaced** (P22, P24, R2): once the errors are taught, they are mixed, unlabelled, and come back on later
  days. P22's evidence is weakest for verbal categories (learning-science.md section 2), so mixing is used but not leaned
  on alone.
- **Few principles, held accurately** (ST17): a short sequence of questions practised on many cases, not a catalogue of
  frameworks.
