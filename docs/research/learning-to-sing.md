# Learning to sing: what the evidence says (2026-10-10)

Gate 1 of the `build-subject` skill for Singing: the kind of learning is a body skill (the voice and the ear together),
so the evidence is motor learning and singing research, not the classification research in `docs/learning-science.md`.
Each finding says what was read; most are abstracts and secondary summaries, and the strength is stated.

## Findings

**S1. Singing off pitch is mostly a link problem, not a hearing problem.** About 10–15% of adults sing clearly off pitch;
most of them hear pitch differences as well as good singers. Their errors are in production: the whole tune shifted,
intervals squeezed smaller, imprecise notes. The cause is a weak mapping from the heard pitch to the voice (Pfordresher &
Brown, 2007, *Music Perception*; Pfordresher & Halpern, 2013, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*). Targets outside the
singer's comfortable range make it worse. *Read: summaries of the papers.* **Design:** practice is copying heard targets
with the voice, in the learner's own range, found first; short melodies, not only single notes, because interval size is
where errors show.

**S2. A live pitch display helps beginners, most on short melodies.** Adults with poor pitch matching, randomly assigned to
one 20-minute session: only the visual-feedback group improved significantly, most on four-note melodies (*Psychology of
Music*, 2022). Beginners gain more than advanced singers (Wilson et al., 2005). A live display without a right/wrong result
did not help (Welch, 1984). *Read: abstracts and summaries; the trials are small and short.* **Design:** the pitch line
against the target while singing, plus a result after each try.

**S3. Live feedback must fade, or the learner depends on it.** The guidance hypothesis: feedback given on every try, and
especially during the action, raises performance in practice and lowers it once removed, because the learner stops using
their own sense of the sound (Salmoni, Schmidt & Walter, 1984; fading from 100% to 0% beat constant feedback on a later
test). In singing, accuracy decayed when live feedback was removed (Paney & Tharp, 2019). *Read: reviews.* **Design:** each
exercise starts with the line, then the same target with the line hidden and the result shown only after; a lesson's end
check is done without the line.

**S4. Attend to the sound, not the body.** Instructions about the intended effect beat instructions about body movements
for learning (retention g ≈ 0.58, transfer g ≈ 0.58; 113 studies; Chua, Jimenez-Diaz, Lewthwaite, Kim & Wulf, 2021,
*Psychological Bulletin*), regardless of skill level. Not tested on singing directly. *Read: abstract.* **Design:**
instructions name the target sound and the line ("land on the line", "a smooth slide"), not the larynx or the diaphragm.

**S5. Straw and humming exercises reduce effort; evidence is from voice therapy.** Semi-occluded vocal tract exercises
(straw phonation, humming, lip trills) lower the pressure needed to start the voice and the felt effort, and effects hold
with regular practice (integrative and systematic reviews, 2024–2025, mostly in people with voice disorders). Not tested as
a warm-up for singers. *Read: summaries.* **Design:** a short straw or hum warm-up is reasonable and low risk; it is not
claimed to make anyone sing better.

**S6. Spaced, short, regular practice** (general evidence: `docs/research/review-after-lessons.md`, R1–R6). **Design:** short
daily sessions; the weekly review for Singing is singing, not questions.

## What the evidence does not settle
- No controlled study was found of a months-long program for adult beginners with pitch accuracy as the outcome. The
  pilot (gate 6) and the learner's own results are the test.
- Tone (pushed, airy, nasal) cannot be judged by the app; hearing a recording of yourself is the obvious tool, and its
  benefit is not established here.
