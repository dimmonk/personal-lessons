# Reviewing after the lessons: what the evidence says (2026-10-10)

Gate 1 of the `build-subject` skill for the weekly review: the evidence first, then the design. Each finding says what was
read (an abstract, the paper, or only a secondary summary), so its strength is visible. A design decision that cites
nothing here is not made.

## Findings

**R1. Review by recalling, not by rereading.** Repeated testing produced a large gain on a delayed test while repeated
studying produced none (Karpicke & Roediger, as summarized by later reviews). Spacing out rereading helps a little;
spacing out recall helps a lot. *Read: secondary summaries.*

**R2. Successive relearning: recall each item until it is right, then again in later sessions.** Items answered wrong
come back later in the same session until answered right; the whole set is relearned in several spaced sessions. One
week after a single session people recalled about 20%; after three spaced relearning sessions, about 80%, and 77% three
weeks after the fourth (Higham et al., describing a 2018 study). It improved real course exams in an introductory
psychology course (Rawson, Dunlosky & Sciartelli, 2013, *Educational Psychology Review* 25(4), 523–548). Suggested
dose: right three times in the first session, then three relearning sessions spaced widely (Vaughn & Rawson, 2011).
Caveat: part of the gain is simply more exposure (Higham et al.). *Read: abstract opening and secondary summaries; the
exact dose needs the original paper.*

**R3. The app decides what comes back, not the learner.** When students chose for themselves when to drop flashcards,
the benefit of retrieval on exams disappeared: people drop items too early. *Read: secondary summary
(retrievalpractice.org).*

**R4. Spacing: the best gap depends on how long it must last.** Over 1,350 people; the best gap between sessions grows
with the time until the knowledge is needed, but as a share of it shrinks: about 20–40% of a one-week delay, about
5–10% of a one-year delay (Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler, 2008, *Psychological Science* 19(11), 1095–1102). For
knowledge meant to last a year, a gap of about three to five weeks per item; for a month, about a week. *Read: abstract
via summaries.*

**R5. Personal scheduling beats one schedule for all.** In a semester course, review chosen per student from their own
answer history and a memory model gave 16.5% better retention than massed study and 10.0% better than one fixed spaced
schedule for everyone (Lindsey, Shroyer, Pashler & Mozer, 2014, *Psychological Science* 25(3), 639–647). *Read:
abstract.*

**R6. Even spacing and a delayed, effortful first recall beat expanding gaps for retention over days** (Karpicke &
Roediger, 2007, *JEP: LMC*). Expanding gaps looked better at 10 minutes and worse at 2 days; the first recall's delay
mattered most. Later work found expanding schedules help only in narrow conditions (Storm, Bjork & Storm, 2010; Kang,
Lindsey, Mozer & Pashler, 2014). *Read: abstract and summaries.*

**R7. Feedback after each answer.** It corrects errors and secures right answers given with low confidence; for a
confident right answer it adds little (Butler, Karpicke & Roediger, 2008, and later reviews). Confident errors can come
back after a week, so they need to return. *Read: secondary summaries.*

**R8. Review in the form the knowledge will be used.** Testing transfers to new material and new questions (grand mean
about d = 0.40), and much more when the review asks for the same kind of response as the real use: d = 0.58 with the
response matching, d = 0.28 without (Pan & Rickard, 2018, *Psychological Bulletin* 144(7), 710–756). *Read: secondary
summary of the meta-analysis.* So a scams review shows a new message to judge, a math review a new problem to solve, a
singing review a line to sing: not a question about the lesson.

**R9. Mix the material in a review.** Practice mixed across problem types beat blocked practice on an unannounced test a
month later, 61% to 38% (Rohrer et al., 2020, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 112, 40–52; a randomized trial in 54
classes). The benefit is for telling related kinds apart; mixing unrelated subjects adds only spacing. *Read: summaries
and the WWC review.*

## What the evidence does not settle

- The exact number of questions per session. The research fixes what is asked (due items, mixed, in the real form) and
  until when (until right), not a count. A session's length follows from what is due.
- Whether a fixed weekly day beats review whenever items fall due. R4 and R5 favor per-item timing; a fixed weekly
  session is a habit choice (it makes review happen), which the evidence neither proves nor rules out.
