# Stand-in learner review: Scams, lesson 3 "Act, check or leave" (part C)

Reviewer: stand-in learner (did not build the lesson), 2026-10-11. Read: the design record's opening (endResult, realMoment,
test) and the gate 4 row for part C; the learner views `docs/learner-view/scams-l3.md` and `scams-l0.md`; the lesson data
`public/subjects/scams/l3.lesson.js` with `items-build.js`, `items-practice.js`, `items-check.js`. Ran the app at 375 px
(`node tools/serve.mjs 5616`, Playwright) through the whole lesson, the check and its result, and let one timed question
run out.

## Before

An adult with no background, on a phone. On a text that says it is from my bank, with a link and a fraud warning, I would
most likely tap the link if it looked right, or call the number in the text: that feels like the careful thing. I would
not read a code to a stranger who asks outright. I have no rule for telling a real alert from a fake one other than how
it looks, and no idea that I should go in another way. Real messages (bills, receipts, pickups) I handle fine, by habit.

## Notes, in order

- **Why screen and Step 1 (three questions).** Short and clear. Question 2, "Did I start this, just now, here?", is the
  one I leaned on for the whole lesson. Its words ("just now, here") are stricter than the answers the lesson later
  marks right (see the bill below).
- **Worked example (locked card text).** Good. The four steps of the working match the three questions; the answer
  names the app. I committed to "Check first" before the working.
- **First group, pairs (Q1 to Q6).** The pairs do their job: a code I asked for vs. a code a stranger wants back; a
  shipping note vs. a fee to release it; a real fraud alert vs. a fake one that looks the same. The real/fake alert pair
  (Q5, Q6) taught me the most: same move for both, and the "What happens next" line shows the real one was real. The
  feedback after each answer shows what my choice led to and what the right one led to, which is what made it stick.
  - Q3 (jacket shipping text): it is a **text message**, but the "check" options and feedback say "The details in the
    **email**" and "the same news the **email** did". A beginner notices and wonders what they missed.
- **Second group, 8 on a 20 second clock (Q7 to Q14).** No screen says a clock is about to start: after "You can stop
  here", the next question simply has a 20-second bar. When time ran out the screen said so plainly and waited for
  Next, which is fine. At 375 px, on an email with a context line, "Leave it" sits below the fold, so on the clock I have
  to scroll to see all three choices.
  - Q7 vs. Q8 (invoice as quoted vs. "new bank details"): the best pair in the lesson; one changed line decides it.
  - Q14 (call from "Calderwood Bank" fraud team): the context never says Calderwood is my bank, yet the right way to
    check is "the number on the back of my card". Small, but a careful reader stops on it.
- **Answerable without learning:** Q9 (virus pop-up), Q10 (boss wants gift cards), Q11 (receipt, no action needed)
  I would have got right before the lesson. They are cheap and keep the mix honest; not a problem.
- **The check intro** says "The clock only shows the time: nothing moves on until you tap Next." I read that as "no time
  limit". In fact at zero the question stops and counts as a miss, and in the check a timed-out real message counts
  toward the "at most 1 wrong among real messages" rule.

## The check, done as written (lesson closed)

I applied the three questions literally:
- Pickup code (I ordered it): do what it says. Right.
- **October power bill** ("Fenwick sends your bill every month. You pay it in the Fenwick app." The email says pay in
  the app): it wants money, I did not start it "just now, here", I have the app, so by Step 1 I chose **Check first,
  the Fenwick app**. Marked **NOT THIS ONE** ("The answer: Do what it says"), while the second answer, "The Fenwick app
  on my phone", is marked **RIGHT** on the same screen. "Do what it says" here means "pay in the app"; "check first in
  the app" means opening the same app. Both lead to the same place, and nothing unsafe happened, yet it counts as a
  real message mishandled.
- Lamp buyer asking for a pickup time: act. Right (though "Do what it says" is odd when the buyer asks nothing; I read
  it as "answer her").
- Real sign-in alert, real card alert: check in the app / number on my card. Right.
- Dentist call moving my cleaning: nothing risky, so leave it. Right, but only because the rule says so; the context
  never says whether Friday suits me, and the feedback reveals "Friday at 3 works for you" only afterwards. A cautious
  beginner who checks on the number on the appointment card is marked wrong.
- Parcel "address incomplete", "final notice" with gift cards, buyer's "tap my link", grandson's call, "account
  suspended": check through my own way. All right.
- **Ioana (online friend, five weeks, wants $1,100)**: no part of the lesson before the check practised a sender I have
  no way of my own to reach. The three questions give no answer for "wants money, I did not start it, I have no way of
  my own". I guessed "Leave it", which is right. Had I picked "Check first", the follow-up offers "A friend or relative I
  know in person" and marks it **right**, while the first answer is marked wrong, and the feedback says "Telling someone
  you know in person is the move", which is not one of the three choices. A friend is not a way to check Ioana; it is
  someone to tell.

My result: **11 of 12: passed** (real messages 5 of 6, scams 6 of 6), the one miss being the bill. A learner who follows
Step 1 literally on both the bill and the dentist call (checks both, safely) gets 2 real messages "wrong" and **fails the
check without doing anything unsafe**.

Requested checks:
- **Every genuine message treated as genuine, never called a scam:** yes. No feedback, reason or consequence line on a
  real message calls it a scam; the real alerts say "This time the link (number) was real. You could not have known".
- **The "check" answer always names a way the person already had:** yes on every item but two. Ioana: "A friend or
  relative I know in person" is not a way to reach or check the sender. Q14 (fraud call) and Q9 (pop-up: "My own
  antivirus program, or a shop I know") name ways the context never says I have.

## After

What I can do now that I could not before:
1. On a bank, card or account alert I did not start, go in through the app I already have or the number on my card,
   never the link or number in the message, **even when the message is real** (Q5, Q6, Q13, worked example). This is
   the big one: before, I would have tapped or called.
2. See "pay to new bank details" sent in a message as the thing to confirm on the number I already had (Q8).
3. Tell a code I asked for from a code someone wants from me (Q1, Q2), and a reset I asked for from one I did not (Q12).
4. Use the shop's own order page instead of a courier's fee or address link when I am expecting a parcel (Q4, check).

## The five answers

- **Gain: yes.** Going in through my own app or card number on an unasked-for money or sign-in message, real or fake,
  instead of the link or number in it (Q5/Q6, Q13, worked example).
- **Doing: yes.** Two short reading screens and one worked example; the rest is 14 practice messages and 12 check
  messages, each a decision with its consequence. Total time was more than ten minutes (about 15), but both groups end
  on "You can stop here, your place is kept".
- **Check: yes in form, flawed in scoring.** Twelve new messages in their real forms (texts, emails, a chat history, call
  transcripts), half real, 20 seconds each, nothing shown until the end: the real moment on new material. But the scoring
  of real messages contradicts the lesson's own rule (below), so a learner can fail by following what was taught.
- **Clear: no, two places block.**
  1. *The rule vs. the key on real messages.* Step 1 says "Did I start this, just now, here?" and "Do what it says only
     when you started it". The key then marks "Do what it says" right, and checking through my own way wrong, on a
     monthly bill I did not start just now (k-bill), with "NOT THIS ONE" and "RIGHT" on the same screen. Combined with
     "at most 1 wrong among real messages", this can fail a safe learner.
  2. *The no-own-way case is tested but never taught or practised* (Ioana), and its feedback contradicts itself.
  Smaller: Q3 says "email" for a text; the clock wording says it "only shows the time" but time runs out and counts;
  nothing announces the first timed group; "Leave it" below the fold at 375 px on the clock; the dentist call does not
  say whether Friday suits me; Q14 does not say Calderwood is my bank.
- **Waste:** little. Q11 (receipt) repeats Q3's lesson (a notice that wants nothing); one of them could go if time is
  tight. Nothing else could be cut without losing the gain.

## Fixes needed

1. Make the rule and the key agree on real messages. Either widen Step 1's question 2 to cover what you set up yourself
   ("an order you placed, a job you hired, a bill you always pay, a code you just asked for") and practise one such
   case in the groups, **or** stop counting "check through a way I already had" as wrong on a real message (it is
   handling it normally; show it as "safe, a minute spent"), keeping "do it their way when you had not started it"
   (the `habit` slip) as the real-message error. The bill (k-bill), whose own text says "pay in the Fenwick app", must
   not be marked "NOT THIS ONE" for opening the Fenwick app.
2. Teach and practise the no-own-way case before the check: add one line to Step 1 ("Someone you only know online who
   asks for money: you have no way of your own to check, so the answer is no, and you tell someone you know") and one
   such message in the timed group. In the Ioana item, do not offer "a friend" as a way to check the sender, or make the
   feedback match the choice that is marked right.
3. Q3: "email" → "text" in its check options and feedback.
4. Clock wording: say that at zero the question stops and counts as not answered, and announce the clock before the
   first timed group.
5. Small: on the dentist call, say whether Friday suits you; on Q14, say Calderwood is your bank; on the pop-up, say
   what "my own antivirus" is or name a way everyone has; keep all three choices above the fold at 375 px on timed
   screens if the layout allows.
