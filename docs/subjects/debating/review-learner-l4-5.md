# Learner review, debating lessons 4 and 5 (cold read)

## Lesson 4: Character, emotion, argument. PASS (with notes)

**The way of deciding, in my words.** Before answering someone, read their last line and ask what it is about. Is it about you (do they trust your sense, honesty or side)? About how they feel? Or about the reasons (a step, a number)? Tone does not tell you. A reply can be about two. Answer the thing it is about, not with more reasons. If my argument failed, check the reply for one of two causes: too many steps to follow, or it began from something they do not accept. Otherwise the argument was not the trouble.

**Blocking or misleading defects:** none found.

**Stuck, unclear, or needed a second read**
- Step 1: "Whether they can trust you: 'We believe good men...'" reads like a list with a quote jammed in. I read it twice to see it was three things. The three never get short names, so I kept paraphrasing them.
- "The argument itself" and "argument" in general are used for the reasons. I first read "argument" as the whole disagreement.
- Step 4 says a fact everyone knows can be left out. Item 8 then has "Everyone knows output jumps 30 percent", and that is the thing Kay rejects. It looks contradictory until you see that "everyone knows" was false there. Worth one line.
- The "why did it not land" question type (items 7, 8, 14 to 16) has no worked example. The worked example only covers "what is their reply about". The "two of these" type (items 5, 12, 13) also has no worked example, only one sentence in Step 5. First meeting is in a scored shuffle.
- "Too many steps" has no number. I judged by feel: 5 steps was too long, 2 was fine.
- Check 12: "you work for the vendor's old boss" is hard to parse. I needed a second read to see it was a trust jab.
- Item 4 (Joy): "I thought you were on mine" sounds hurt, so it could be read as feeling. I got to trust only because Step 2 defines goodwill ("on their side"). Acceptable, but close.
- Item 2 (Walt): he is tired, not angry or hurt. The feedback leans on Aristotle's "angry or hurt", a small stretch.
- Item 7 (Hugh): "Where did the asphalt come in?" could be read as not accepting that step. I settled on "too long" because he says "you lost me".
- Item 2 vs Item 6: both feeling replies. Item 6 is the clearer case.

**Guessable without understanding**
- Form cues work too well. A "you..." sentence means trust. "I am / my stomach..." means feeling. Questions about a number or a test mean argument. Two-part replies are the "two of these" items.
- The "why" items echo the option text: "you lost me around the third step", "I don't accept that number". Only the "neither" items (Hugh, check 16) need thought.
- Check 9 (Lou) is nearly the worked example (Carl): "What do you know about it?" Pattern-match, not new thinking.
- Answer length and position were not giveaways. Options are fixed text and the order is shuffled.

**Follow-on questions I wanted:** none that the lesson does not answer, apart from "how many steps is too many".

## Lesson 5: What a true story gets you. PASS (with notes)

**The way of deciding, in my words.** After telling a true story, look at their reply. Did any part of their view shift (a concession, "I could back a trial")? Then it is a small move, even if they were polite as well. If not, did they take the story in (sorry, thank you, "that is rough") while keeping their view? That is a hearing, not a changed mind. If they ignore it or change the subject, there is nothing to count, which does not mean the story failed. Before telling a story, check each line against what happened and cut any line that goes beyond it. An invented story can earn respect too, and it is still a lie.

**Blocking or misleading defects:** none found.

**Stuck, unclear, or needed a second read**
- Step 1: "Careful listening made the person seem better to the listener, and added no persuasion to the story." Who is "the person", and who is "the listener"? I read it three times and am still not sure. This is the muddiest sentence in the lesson, but it does not affect any question.
- Step 2 blurs two things. The studies measured respect for a view, but "a hearing" is defined by how the other person replies. I accepted the link, but it was not obvious.
- "Neither" is only ever a non-sequitur (the plumber, the game). I do not know what to call a flat "Okay." or a reply that just argues back and ignores the story. The text says "no sign of either", but never shows a borderline case.
- Item 3 (Dee): "That is a rough story." is a thin sign of taking it seriously, and Dee is curt. A beginner could pick "neither". The lesson says hearing. Defensible, but it is the line where hearing and neither blur.
- Item 5 (Ellen): she backs the plan and only suggests a change to it. I could call it a hearing (she still backs it) or a small move. The rule in Step 2 ("any part shifted") settles it, but it was not obvious on first read.
- Item 1 (Ravi): the feedback says he backs "a trial he was against before". The text never says he was against a trial. That claim is invented. The move is still clear from "I would back a one-semester trial".
- Tap items: "The lines of one block can be tapped; the rest cannot", and "a tap is the answer", are awkward. A mis-tap is final. Also "The text does give one. It is marked above" reads oddly when I pressed the "every line true" button wrongly.

**Guessable without understanding**
- Cue words give it away. "Sorry / thank you ... still / keeping my vote" always means hearing. "Maybe ... I could live with / get behind" always means move. A non-sequitur always means neither. This is the intended skill, but the cases are very uniform (items 4 and 11 and 12 are near copies).
- Tap items: the false line is the one that contradicts a number or fact in "What happened". It is easy to find by matching numbers. "Every line is true" shows up in 2 of 7 items, so it is not a strong tell.
- Answer length and position were not giveaways.

**Follow-on questions I wanted**
- "What if they argue back and do not mention the story at all?"
- "Is thanks or politeness alone enough to count as a hearing?"

**Notes (polish)**
- The neighbor and friend names repeat (Ravi, Tom).
- Step 3's "a false personal story would earn more respect than facts" is a bit odd.
- The move feedback "a step, not a win" is good and I would keep it.
