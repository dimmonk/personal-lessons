## Spot-checks against the sources
- Aristotle, Rhetoric I.1, I.2 (classics.mit.edu): confirmed "not by what people think of his character before he begins to speak"; "may almost be called the most effective means of persuasion"; enthymeme "in general, the most effective of the modes of persuasion" (I.1); "must consist of few propositions"; "cannot take in at a glance a complicated argument"; premisses "not generally admitted or believed"; "we must not make people believe what is wrong". The lesson's "he does not rank them" is correct.
- Rhetoric II.2 and II.3: confirmed "those who laugh, mock, or jeer at us" (anger); calm toward those "serious when we are serious", who "admit their fault and are sorry", who "do not insult or mock".
- Kalla & Broockman 2020: arguments alone no effect (Experiment 1, immigration); narrative exchange durable for at least four months (d = 0.08); two transgender experiments replicate (0.08, 0.04). Matches l5.lesson.js:17-19.
- Santoro et al. 2025: abstract only, as the note says. Listening improved perception of the persuader and added no persuasion to the narrative. Matches l5.lesson.js:18.
- Kubin et al. 2021: 15 studies, the personal-experience advantage in respect, strongest for issue-relevant harm, persuasion set aside. Matches l5.lesson.js:24.
- Van Bavel et al. 2021 (PNAS 118(8) e2100280118): the false-story quote and the respect-versus-belief distinction are verbatim. They also say it "remains unclear if people find personal experience persuasive", which supports the lesson's framing.
