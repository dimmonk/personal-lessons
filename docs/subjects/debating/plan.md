# Debating: plan (Field: Communication)

Sources are in `docs/research/debating.md` (F1-F10) and `docs/learning-science.md` (P/D ids).

## What you'll be able to do, and when
**After this you can** tell, early in a disagreement, what kind of conversation it is and whether it is
worth continuing, and then choose a next move (offer one fact, tell one true story, restate their view, ask for their reason) knowing what the
research does and does not show about each, **when** someone at work, in your family or with a neighbour disagrees with you.
(F1, F5, F7; P16, D6)

**Real-world test:** 12 new, unseen short exchanges (mixed: the six kinds of talk, a talk that changes kind, a person fused with
a group position). For each: name the kind, say continue / change topic / leave, and pick the best next reply from four. Pass: 10 of 12. Later returns repeat it on fresh cases (P24). A self-check in life ("I named the
type before I answered") is welcome but not scored (D4).

## The way of thinking (what experts do; research note section 1)
1. **Name the kind of talk first.** Walton and Krabbe's six kinds (persuading, deciding together, bargaining, finding out together,
   asking, quarrel) are defined by what the talk started from, what each person is trying to get and what would count as done; real
   talk is often mixed, and a shift that goes unnoticed or blocks the first aim causes the mess (F1).
2. **Expect a small move, not a win.** Corrections usually move people at least somewhat, then the gains often fade; what blocks is often a link
   between the claim and the person's group (Nyhan), and arguments alone did nothing in the best field test on immigration (F2, F5). No claim about ability.
3. **Lower the stakes before you argue** (an inference from F3, with weak support from F7/F8, not a finding). Restate their view fairly, ask for their reasons; a person with an opinion to defend
   treats your argument as one to rebut, and only someone with no axe to grind judges it fairly (F3, F7, F8). Careful listening
   improved how the persuader was seen but added no persuasion to a story (F5, Santoro et al. 2025).
4. **Use all three means, honestly.** Be credible, mind their state, keep the argument short; in one set of studies on moral disagreements personal experience
   earned more respect than facts (F6; respect is not persuasion; nothing read shows it for other disputes), and a shared story moved views a little where arguments alone did not (F5).
5. **Let someone with no stake test your own side.** People who argue a side look for support for it, and people with no axe to grind judge arguments well, so the check on your reasons is someone with nothing at stake (F3; an inference from a passage about judging others' arguments, marked in the note).

## What it teaches (every ask, and what the evidence did to it)
- **"Chess with a pigeon":** kept as one sentence in lesson 3, tied to Walton's quarrel row (the other party's aim is to hit back,
  not to be persuaded). Corrected: "pigeon" is a label for the talk, not the person (F1).
- **Bonhoeffer's stupidity:** taught as one real type (a person under a group's spell, F10). Corrected: reasons and stories do move
  many people a little, so "never reason" is wrong (F2, F5). Lesson 2 makes no claim about intelligence or numeracy (F4: the two
  studies disagree on whether skill matters, and only abstracts were read).
- **Logos, ethos, pathos:** taught from Aristotle (F9). Corrected: he calls argument the core and character the
  strongest; "logos loses" is only true of a bare fact against identity (F5, F6).
- **Argumentation theory / dialogue types:** lesson 1 (F1). "A show for an audience" was dropped as a kind: it was the lessons'
  own category, and Walton's only audience type is Debate (a formal contest to persuade a third party), which he treats with
  political debate as a mixed talk (F1). **Backfire effect:** lesson 2, as a myth (F2). **Identity
  and motivated reasoning:** lessons 2 and 9 (F3, F4). **Deep canvassing:** lesson 7, small effect stated (F5).
- **Street epistemology / Socratic questions:** lesson 6, limits stated; brand dropped (F8). **Competitive debate and
  pragma-dialectics rules:** dropped, reasons in research note section 2 (judgement call).
- Cases are set in everyday US situations; no claim about Canada versus US norms (nothing read supports one).

## Lessons (Unit 1 to 4), each with its check and source
*Built 2026-10-20, rebuilt 2026-10-21 after two failed rounds of fresh reviews (docs/subjects/debating/review-*.md). In the data one unit holds one
lesson (validator V71 refuses two lessons for one part), so the ten lessons below are ten units in the app; the four groups here are the
plan's grouping, kept in the subject record's `units`. What changed at the root: lesson 1 now teaches Walton and Krabbe's six kinds, each case
holds its own cue so one answer holds, and a talk that changes kind is asked by the line where it changes; lesson 2 teaches a way of deciding (read the cause behind the pushback, pick the move the studies back) with four parallel statements that never differ in tone or length; lesson 3 turns on what the other person does in the last
lines (answers, hits at you, threatens or blocks, comes back after a change of subject, several people at once), taps the deciding line and practises a fair aim, the words and the first act, and keeps couples out because the couple-conflict literature was not read.*
**Unit 1: Decide whether to argue**
1. *What kind of conversation is this?* Six kinds, told apart by what the talk started from, what each person is trying to get and
   what would count as done; mixed talks and shifts. Check: name the kind of 6 talks, and for 2 talks that change, tap the line
   where it changes and name the new kind. Source F1; P12 (contrast), P9.
2. *Why people dig in.* One decision a question: read what the disagreement is about (a fact someone could check; a view about a group or a policy; or the two mixed),
   with the move and the studies in the feedback (the correction for a fact, even from someone defending a side; a true story for a view; each on its own part). Backfire is rare
   at most. Arguing in a group helps when people share the goal of getting it right. After someone agrees and drops it, no source says what to do: one labeled, untested suggestion,
   not graded. Check: 8 exchanges, read the disagreement. Source F2, F3, F5; P29.
3. *Your goal, and when to leave.* Aim for the next small step the other person offers; keep going while they answer, change the subject once when
   nothing is answered, leave on a threat or block, a failed change, or a pile-on. Check: 8 talks, choose the move, tap the deciding line, then pick the
   aim, the words or the first act. Source F1, F2, F5 (small effects); the rule, aims and lines are judgement calls, marked and graded as the lesson's own rule.

**Unit 2: The three means** *(built 2026-10-21, both lessons reshaped by the evidence)*
4. *Character, emotion, argument.* Aristotle's three means (Rhetoric I.1-2, II.1-3, read in full): trust built by what you say (good sense,
   honest character, goodwill), feelings that change judgement (slights anger, seriousness and an honest sorry calm), a short argument from what
   the hearer grants. Practice is reading what the other person's last reply puts in question (trust, feeling, argument, two at once), and why an
   argument did not land (too long, a start they do not accept, or neither), with a worked example of each question type; the next line is written against a model and never scored. Check: 8
   new exchanges, 7 right. **Changed from the plan** ("label means in 8 replies, fix one"): labelling a sentence is recall of a taxonomy, one
   sentence often holds two means, and the real-use decision is what to answer; the reading of the reply is graded, and "answer the one it is about"
   is the lesson's own method, said so and not graded. Source F9; P12.
5. *What a true story gets you* (was "Why a fact alone fails, and a story helps"). **Retitled and reshaped:** the corrected research says a
   correction works somewhat on a checkable fact (F2, lesson 2), and the arguments-alone null in F5 is about attitudes toward a group, so "a fact
   alone fails" was wrong as a lesson. Lesson 5 reads what a true story got you from their last lines (a hearing, a small move, neither: respect is
   not persuasion, F6; the story effects were measured in later surveys, F5) and checks your own story line by line against what happened (no
   invented stories: F6 commentary, Aristotle I.1). The cases are moral disagreements, because the personal-experience studies covered those (F6); reading a reply as a hearing, a small move or neither is the lesson's own coding, said so. Check: 8 new questions, 7 right. What makes a story relevant, short and harm-focused stays
   in lesson 7. Source F5, F6, F9.

**Unit 3: The moves**
6. *Restate, then ask for the reason.* Fair restatement; "how would that work?" and "how sure are you?"; when they do
   not work (sacred values, deep belief). Check: choose or write the restatement, then the question, in 8 cases. Source
   F7 (marked practice-based), F8, F3; P19.
7. *Share one true experience.* Relevant, harm-focused, short, non-judging; plus one fact. Check: pick or build the
   reply from options in 8 cases. Source F5, F6.
8. *The bad moves.* Look-alikes: mocking, labelling "stupid", fact-dumping, the invented story. Check: spot the move in
   8 replies and replace it. Source F2, F5, F6, F10; P12, P29.
9. *Check your own side.* Where your own reasoning leans toward your side; let someone else test it (F3, an inference). Check: 6
   cases, find the myside move. Source F3, F4.

**Unit 4: Put it together**
10. *Mixed live exchanges.* Multi-turn cases: type, goal, then each next move, feedback names what decided. Check: the
    real-world test above on new cases. Source all; P22, P24, D6.
