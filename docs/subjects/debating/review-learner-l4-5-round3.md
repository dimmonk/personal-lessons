# Learner review, lessons 4 and 5, round 3

## Lesson 4 (Character, emotion, argument): PASS

Blocking findings: none. I judged each case from the lesson text. Each has one defensible answer.

- Trust items (1, 4, 9) turn on the reply asking about you (commission, "whose side", "your field"). Nothing else in them competes.
- Feeling items (2, 6, 10) state a feeling and drop the topic. Item 3 (Beth, sharp but about the test) separates tone from topic. Step 5 teaches that distinction.
- The two-part items (5, 12, 13) each have clearly separable halves.
- Why-not-land items: the long chains (7, 14) get a "lost the thread" reply. The bad starting points (8, 15) get a reply that contradicts the premise. The "neither" cases (check 16) have a short argument and a reply that is plainly something else.

Notes (polish):
- "Neither: the reply is not about how it was built" is clunky. "Neither: the reply is about something else" reads better.
- Cue risk: in the why-not-land items, "neither" always appears with an emotional reply. A learner could learn "feeling means neither". Add one "neither" case where the reply is a trust question.
- Cue risk: the long You-line signals "too long". Item 8 (three claims) is a fair counterweight, and one more would help.
- Item 6 is mostly clear. Mike's burning face comes from your joke, which brushes trust. "Give me a minute" settles it as feeling.
- "Deciding words" is never defined for a beginner. It is only the printed highlight.

## Lesson 5 (What a true story gets you): PASS

Blocking findings: none.

- Hearing items (3, 4, check 12, check 13) show sympathy about the story plus an unchanged view. Move items (1, 5, check 11) show a concrete concession. Neither items (2, 6, check 10) ignore the story or change the subject. The step 2 rule "any shift means move" settles the mixed replies, such as Pat's "children first, adults no".
- True-line items: each has a stated fact that clearly contradicts exactly one line. The all-true items (8, check 15) are clear.

Notes (polish):
- Cue risk: every hearing reply is "sorry/heavy, but I hold my view". A learner can guess hearing from "but/still". Add a hearing reply with no "but" word, or a neither reply with sympathy words.
- "Neither shows in what they said" could be read as "neither shows anything". "Neither a hearing nor a move" is plainer.
- In the line-check items, the false line is always the one carrying a number or a detail that differs from the facts. That makes it a matching exercise, not a judgment. Add one false line that is an inference or an added feeling.
- Check question 14 (mother) and check question 17 are fair but easy.
