# Learner review, lessons 1-3, round 4 (cold beginner read)

Caveat: the printed view shows the answers inline, so I could not answer blind. I judged each case from the lesson text first, then compared. I would have got every practice and check item right, but several only by pattern (see below).

## Lesson 1: What kind of conversation is this?  PASS (with notes)

My own words, before the first case: ask three things (what did it start from, what does each person want, what counts as finished). Claim one person doubts = persuading. Shared choice weighed for everyone = deciding together. Competing wants traded to a split = bargaining. Nobody knows, all want the answer = finding out. One person lacks, other knows and tells = asking. Lines aimed at the person, not a point or an offer = quarrel. Loudness and topic do not decide it.

Defects (minor unless marked):
- Item 2 (street committee): the opening lines (Lena leans Alder, Tom says Birch) read as two people holding rival claims. Only "let's score both" settles it as deciding. A beginner could defend persuading until that line. Moderate.
- Check 12 (caterer): Kai's "let's call them" reads a little like finding out together. Minor.
- Item 9: "after that line" is asking, but the reason says they are deciding again by the last line. Needed a second read.
- Check 18 feedback for persuading: "Until Greg's question about when..." is confusing, since Mina's line is the break.
- Step 1 lists six kinds of start but the "result" boilerplate mentions seven facets and "keep going / change the subject / leave", which this lesson never teaches.
- Draft/revision header and "Where the subject ends up" paragraph are textbook-like.
- Guessable: shift items (9, 10, 17, 18) always have the break at the first dig; quarrel is the answer every time.

## Lesson 2: Why people dig in, and what is a myth  PASS (with notes)

My own words: three causes behind pushback. Never saw the fact: give the correction, it usually helps a bit. Defending a group side: more argument is weak, a true story about someone affected moves views a little. An earlier correction wore off: treat the gain as small and maybe temporary. Backfire (correction makes them believe more) is a myth, so never choose it.

Defects:
- Guessable by cue words: "our group / church / union / local / my side" always means defending a side, and "I haven't seen / never got" or "two days later repeated" always means the other two. Items can be solved without understanding. Moderate.
- Worn-off feedback says "One repeat of the old claim does not show why it happened", right after calling worn off the answer. It undercuts the key. Moderate; confusing.
- The fourth cause and the fourth move (backfire, hold back) are never right and are removed by elimination.
- Moves are a fixed 1:1 map of causes, so the second question in each item adds nothing. Six items (1, 6, 8, Check 11, 14, 17) are near-identical "not seen" cases. Padded.
- Item 7 (poll, two partisans): "tell a true story" is an odd move for two people reading a table. The feedback admits the evidence is only "the nearest".
- Step 1: "the same numbers, presented as gun laws, got answers split by political side" is vague (which numbers?). "One account of reasoning says..." is unnamed and textbook-like. "A real experience told by each of them" is unclear (told by whom?). Needed second reads.
- Step 2's story advice never shows what a story sounds like.

## Lesson 3: Your goal, and when to leave  PASS (with notes)

My own words: look at the last lines. They still answer (even bluntly): keep going, and aim at the next small step they offered. They dig at you or an old hurt and ignore your point: change the subject once, say so, no jab. Threat or blocked way, they return after the change, or several at once: leave plainly, no last word (online, stop replying).

Defects:
- Item 6 (Vic: "Of course you do. You always side with the boss") and Item 2 (Rita) can be read as a partial answer to the point. Keep going versus change the subject is a judgment call. Moderate.
- Guessable: the "tap the line" answer is almost always the other person's line (never a "You:" line), often the last. The fair-aim right answer is the only "You and X ..." option. The right "which line" option is always the only one of the form "We won't settle this. [new topic]". "I'm going to step out now" is always right for leave.
- Item 9 and Check 17 (blocked or locked in): "move toward people outside" or "get help" is vague when the door is blocked. Minor.
- Check 11: "(you, at the right)" thread labelling is awkward.
- "That is common sense, not a finding" repeats and reads defensive. Mild padding.
- Item 7 and Check 16 are the same case twice.
