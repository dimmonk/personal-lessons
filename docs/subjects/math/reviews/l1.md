## Before

On a receipt or a bill I use a calculator or trust the printed number. I can round $3.49 to $3 if asked, but I never
make a rough answer first, so I would not notice a calculator slip. Splitting $55.42 seven ways in my head: I would not
try. Checking whether a till receipt adds up: I never do, and would not know a quick way.

## Going through it

- **The why and Step 1 (teaching).** Clear and short. The "within about a fifth, $5 either way on a $25 cart" line is
  useful: it tells me how good a rough answer must be. The split trick (round the bill to a number the group divides
  evenly) is new to me and the one example ($142.80 ÷ 6 → $144 ÷ 6 = $24) is enough. The rounding rule itself (50 cents
  and up goes up) is never said, only shown ($3.79 → $4); every price ends in 9, so it never bites here, but a real
  beginner would want the one line.
- **Step 2, worked receipt.** Easy to follow. One oddity: the end line reads "The answer: $23.43 / $23.43", so the rough
  answer is shown as $23.43 when the working just said "about $23". A beginner can wonder whether the estimate was meant
  to be exact, which is the wrong idea Step 1 just corrected.
- **Q1 (cart).** 5 + 18 + 1 + 4 + 6 = $34; exact $34.95. Fine.
- **Q2 (office order, quantities).** Rough: 55 + 27 + 24 + 28 = $134, done in my head in about 40 seconds. Exact: I
  opened the lesson's Calculator and typed it the way the lesson writes it, `5 × 11.09 + 3 × 9.09 + 3 × 7.99 + 4 × 6.89 =`.
  **The display shows 29441.94.** The in-app calculator works left to right with no memory key (checked by running its
  own `calcPress` on those keys), so a sum of products can't be typed in one go. The app then holds my answer back
  ("your answer and your estimate disagree, look again"), which is right, but nothing tells me what to do: the working
  in the feedback shows the same one-line expression. The only way through is to work out each line, remember four
  amounts with cents (55.45, 27.27, 23.97, 27.56) and add them, with no paper. If I type it wrong, the question comes
  back with new numbers "until it is right", so I am stuck in the same trap again (Q5 is another one). **This blocks a
  beginner.** (A phone's own calculator does × before +, so someone who leaves the app would be fine, but the lesson
  offers its own calculator and its working tells me to type it that way.)
- **Q3 (split $55.42 by 7).** $56 ÷ 7 = $8; exact $7.92. Good. This is the most useful new trick in the lesson.
- **Q4, Q6.** $27 vs $26.85; $48 ÷ 6 = $8 vs $8.07. Fine. **Q5** is the same quantity-order trap as Q2 (rough
  25 + 45 + 30 + 40 = $140; exact $135.22 only by doing line by line).
- **Step 5 (teaching).** Clear. But a skeptical adult will think: when does a till receipt ever add wrongly? Machine
  receipts are added by the till, and real ones carry a tax line, so on my own grocery receipt the printed total would
  sit 5 to 10% above my rough sum and I would not know whether that is "near" or "wrong". The lesson never mentions
  tax. The skill (compare a stated total with a rough sum) is real and transfers to a restaurant check, a hand-written
  bill, a friend's tally or my own calculator result, but the lesson does not say so.
- **Q7, Q8 (right totals).** $27 vs $27.85, $28 vs $28.45: right. **Q9, Q10 (missed line).** $31 vs $17.06, off by
  about $14 (the $13.39 laundry soap); $33 vs $21.16, off by about $12 (the $11.69 soap). Finding which line is missing
  by matching the gap to a line price is a genuinely good step.
- Nothing I could answer without learning: each item needs the rounding or the comparison. Partial exception: grocery
  carts always land between about $20 and $45, so guessing "$30" without adding would pass both cart items in the check
  (within 20% of $34.85 and of $27.85).

## After

What I can do now that I could not before:
1. Make a rough total of a 4 to 5 line receipt in my head in under 20 seconds, within about 5% (Q1, Q4; worked example).
2. Make a rough total of an order with quantities by rounding, multiplying and adding in my head (Q2, Q5).
3. Estimate an equal share of a bill with an awkward group size by rounding the bill to a multiple of the group
   (Q3, Q6, Step 1's example). New to me.
4. Tell a stated total that is $10 or more off from one that is right, and find the left-out line by matching the gap
   to a line price (Q7 to Q10).
5. Hold a calculator result against my own rough answer and look again when they disagree (the held-back answer in
   Step 3).

## The five answers

- **Gain: yes.** Abilities 3 and 4 above are concrete and new: a split share in the head, and catching a total that is
  off and naming the missing line.
- **Doing: yes.** About a minute of reading; the rest is typing estimates and totals and judging receipts.
- **Check: yes, with a weakness.** New numbers, no help, the real moment (a receipt, an order, a bill), feedback only at
  the end, and it matches the Gate 4 row (4 of 5 estimates within 20%; the wrong total caught among three). Weak spots:
  the one wrong-total item is a two-way choice, and the cart items are guessable at a flat $30.
- **Clear: no, one blocker.** The quantity-order exact total cannot be typed on the lesson's own calculator the way the
  working shows (gives $29,441.94 instead of $134.25), and a miss repeats until right. Smaller: the rounding rule not
  stated; the worked example shows the estimate's answer as $23.43; no word about a tax line on a real receipt.
- **Waste:** little. If time is tight, the exact total of carts in Step 4 could go (the calculator part is the bit a
  beginner can already do); everything else carries the gain.

## Fixes needed

1. **(Blocking)** Make the exact total of a quantity order typeable as written: give the lesson's calculator ×-before-+
   (as phone calculators do), or a memory / running-total key, or ask the order's exact total line by line (a box per
   line, then the sum) and change the working in Q2/Q5's feedback to "work out each line, then add the line totals".
   The working shown must be keys the calculator in front of the learner can take.
2. One line in Step 5: a real receipt's total includes tax, so it sits a little above the sum of the lines (a few
   percent); a gap of $10 or a whole item is the mistake to look for. And name where printed totals really go wrong (a
   restaurant check, a hand-written bill, your own calculator total).
3. Step 1: one line, "50 cents or more rounds up; less rounds down."
4. Worked example: show the rough answer as $23, not $23.43, at the end.
5. Check: widen the cart range (or vary line count) so a flat guess cannot pass, and consider two wrong totals among the
   receipts so catching it is not a coin flip.
