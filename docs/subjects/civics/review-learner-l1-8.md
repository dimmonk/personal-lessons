## The way of deciding, in my words (before the first practice question)

- L1: three branches, three jobs: Congress writes, the president carries out, the courts judge. Recall lesson; the questions are asked first, the answer is taught after.
- L2: the numbers come with a reason (small states equal in the Senate, House follows people, justices for life so they judge free of politics). Remember the reason, then the number.
- L3: always ask "which level, which part" before blaming the president. Federal handles money, war, army, treaties. State handles schools, police, licences, land. Everything not given to Washington goes to states or people.
- L4: find who the story names; decide federal, state or local; then decide wrote, carried out or judged.
- L5: ask "what lets them do it?" Find the line that names a law or power for them, against them, or says a court has the case. No law either way means not settled.
- L6: six things can stop an act (veto and override, court, new law, federal law, voters, amendment). Find the line that shows which is at work.
- L7: leave the page and see what outsiders say about who is behind it. Find the original, not someone's telling. Check when you cannot place the source or the story is alarming.
- L8: ask the four questions in order every time: who acted, theirs to do, what stops it, who is telling me.

I could state all eight, so no defect on that test.

## Defects that decide the verdict

**D1 (L7, Items 1 to 3, Check 6 to 8): the right answer is always the second line.** In all six "Which one do you open first?" questions, the correct line is in position 2 of 4. The lines are text in a block, not options, so the option shuffle cannot move them. A learner who taps "second" gets 6 of 6 with no reasoning. Fix: vary the position of the right line across the questions (1st, 3rd, 4th as well), and add at least one case where the outside report is not the most newspaper-looking line.

**D2 (L7, all "open first" questions): the wrong lines are labelled.** Every wrong line from the source carries the tag "(the group's own site)" or "(the page's own site)". That label is the answer. The learner never has to judge which page is the source's own. Fix: remove the tag on some questions so the learner must recognise the source's page from its name and content, as in real search results. Also add a case where the outside result is a weak one (a sponsored post, an anonymous blog) so "outside" alone is not enough.

**D3 (L7, Items 4 and 5, Check 9 and 10; also L8): "take as it stands" is decided by one planted sentence.** "You looked up X last month" means take it; "you have never heard of this site" means check. No question puts the two cues in conflict (a trusted paper with an alarming claim, an unknown source with a dull one), though Step 3 says alarming stories get checked. Fix: add one conflict case in L7 and L8, and say what wins.

## Other defects (polish unless noted)

Stuck places:

4. L1 Item 2 "Why three branches?" The answers (checks and balances, separation of powers) are not taught before the question. The table has no reason column. Fix: add one line to Step 1 on why. Same for "Cabinet" (Items 13, 14) and Congress's powers (Item 6): met cold, then taught in the feedback. Fine with the "Not yet" button, but say so up front.
5. L2 Step 1 uses "Electoral College" and "the Great Compromise" with no explanation. Item 15's feedback is the first place electors are mentioned. Fix: one sentence in Step 2.
6. Typed-answer wording (L1, L2, L3, all "why" and "power" questions). The lesson's own tables use words the matcher rejects. L3 Step 2 says "schools, police, driver's licenses"; the accepted answers are "Provide schooling and education", "Provide protection (police)" and "Give a driver's license". A learner who types "driver's licenses" or "police" is marked "Not this one". The same happens with L2 Item 4 ("so small states count as much" against "Equal representation"). The "My answer means the same" button rescues it, but a learner who misses the button sees a correct answer rejected. Fix: add the table's own wording as accepted answers, or have the tables use the accepted wording.
7. L3 Step 4 (your own answers). It names uscis.gov/citizenship/testupdates as the place to look up your representative, senator and governor. I could not tell from the screen whether that page gives them. A newcomer needs a way to find a representative by address (a "find your representative" lookup). Fix: name the lookup and say the page is for the updated test, not for names.
8. L5 Step 1 says "If Congress said nothing, it is not settled" for the president. L5 Item 1 (president orders 9 a.m. start; "no law of Congress gives the president this power") is marked "Not theirs to do". Step 2 covers it ("a power that belongs to someone else"), but the two rows pull against each other and Item 1's "Not settled" feedback ("the story does say what stands against it") needs a second read. Fix: add to the Step 1 president row "unless the power belongs to another level (schools: the states), then it is not theirs".
9. L6 Check 8 and L8 Check 7: "Which could stop it?" after the Supreme Court has already ruled. "It" can be the law (a court did just stop it) or the ruling. Feedback on "A court" says "No court case is open in the story", which is false there. A careful learner reaches amendment by reasoning that the highest court is final, but is told a wrong reason. Fix: ask "What could undo the ruling?" and change the "A court" feedback to "The highest court has already ruled".
10. L6 Item 2 (gas tax, voters angry): "Lawmakers passing a new law" is also defensible (candidates promise to cut the tax) and its feedback ("no one is making a new law") is not quite true. Fix: make the voters the only live route, or reword the feedback to "the new law would only come after the election".
11. L4: "school board" is a local body but is not in the Step 1 table (met first in Step 4's compare). Fix: add school boards to the Local row.
12. L4's "wrong idea" (the president decided it) is never tested. Every story already names its actor. Add one story where the headline blames the president for a local act.

Cues that let a learner skip some reasoning (not decisive):

13. L5 "Tap the line": the right line is always the 2nd or 3rd, never the first (the first line is always the act). In L5 and L6 the right line also always contains the words "law", "Constitution" or "court". Real stories rarely say it this plainly. Fix: add a story where the power is implied, not named.
14. L6 and L8: the keyword in the story names the stopper (sue means court, election means voters, veto means veto). Acceptable as the taught method, but L8 should include one story where two stoppers are live and the question asks for the quickest.
15. L2 Item 16, "two Cabinet-level positions", accepts 22 titles. Any two "Secretary of ..." pass. Fine for the interview.
16. L1 and L2 check passes need 16 of 17 and 18 of 20 on typed recall. Strict for a beginner, given D-6 above.
17. Real-world accuracy for a newcomer (for the research reviewer to confirm): L5 Check 11 says a city cannot set a minimum wage because "a state law says only the state may". True in some states and not in others. The story says it is a state law, so it stands as fiction, but a learner may take it as a general rule.
18. Nothing compares with Canada (a first minister and a Governor General) where a Canadian will mix up "head of state" and "head of government". The lesson does not need it, but one line in L1 would save a stall for this reader.
