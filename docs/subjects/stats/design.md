# stats: design record (build-subject skill)

Written 2026-10-10. Gates 2 to 5 were designed blind (before reading the existing lessons), then the existing lessons
were audited against them (last section). Gates 2 and 3 below revise the drafts the owner approved: the verdict gains
"cannot tell from this", "the first place it goes wrong" becomes "the line that decides it", and a plain-size answer is
added. The revision needs the owner's confirmation before gate 6.

Approved by the owner: 2026-10-10, "approve all, keep going" (gates 2, 3 and the pilot, for every subject; the pilot is still to be tried on a phone, and what it shows comes back into this record).

## Gate 1. Kind of learning and evidence

**Kinds.** (1) *Judging situations*: deciding whether a number shows what it is used to claim, and pointing to why not.
(2) *Doing a procedure*: a small calculation that recurs in almost every claim, turning a relative change, a percentage
or a bare count into a plain size ("2 in 1,000 before, 3 in 1,000 after"; reading the real change off a chart's axis).
Not designed for: the *habit* of checking unprompted (no evidence found for building it from a text app; see Gate 2,
what is out).

**Evidence.** The general documents assumed a classification task, so the task's own evidence was researched and written
to `docs/research/judging-statistical-claims.md` (C1–C10). General learning evidence comes from
`docs/research/review-after-lessons.md` (R1–R9) and the sources (not the task assumption) of `docs/learning-science.md`.

**Principles that drive the design.**
- **G1. Practise on the real input with the real response.** The item looks like what the phone shows (headline and
  article, chart, ad, post), and the answer is what a person does with it: a verdict, the line that decides it, a size.
  Not a question about a lesson, not a story told about a claim. (R8, Pan & Rickard 2018; P25; C10.)
- **G2. Whole items from the first lesson, simple to complex; drill the recurring step on its own.** Each lesson adds
  one question to whole-item practice; the plain-size calculation gets its own practice. (C9, 4C/ID, d = 0.79.)
- **G3. Teach each question explicitly and show it worked before practice; practice alone does not teach it.** (C2,
  Heijltjes et al. 2014; van Peppen et al. 2021; P10.)
- **G4. Teach specific rules with varied everyday examples, test them in a new area of life, return after a delay; do not
  promise general critical thinking.** (C1, Fong & Nisbett; Lehman et al. 1988; C2 on failed far transfer; P25.)
- **G5. Discrimination, not suspicion.** Every set has claims that hold up; rejecting a sound claim is scored as an error
  and shown to the learner. (C8, contested; P26, Burke et al. 2022.)
- **G6. Turn every risk number into "so many in 1,000", by picturing the people, not by formula.** Relative numbers
  persuade without being understood. (C5, Akl et al. 2011; C6, Sedlmeier & Gigerenzer 2001.)
- **G7. For charts, read the axis and work out the real change; a warning does not protect.** (C7, Yang et al. 2021.)
- **G8. Choose the questions by where real claims go wrong**: cause from a link, the wrong people studied, the headline
  claiming more than the study, a relative number with no base, an unfair comparison, a chart's shape. A small fixed set
  is learnable and lasts. (C4, Sumner et al. 2014; C3, Nsangi et al. 2020.)
- **G9. Commit before seeing the answer; feedback after each item, with the deciding line shown.** (P15, P19, P20, R7.)
- **G10. Mix the questions once each is taught; bring back new items on a spaced schedule the app decides.** (R1–R6,
  R9, P22, P24; Sana, Yan & Kim 2017 on statistical concepts.)

*If the learner did only this (learn these principles' practice), would they be closer to the end result?* Yes: every
principle is about doing the real moment's action, not about reading.

## Gate 2. End result

- **After this subject you can** tell whether a number shows what it is used to claim, point to what is wrong or missing
  when it does not, and say how big it is in plain terms, **when** you meet it on your phone (a headline and its article,
  a chart, an ad, a post, a report) and are about to believe, share or act on it.
- **Real moment.** Reading on a phone, a minute or two, the full item in front of you. The headline says one thing; the
  facts that decide it are often lower down in the text, or not there at all. Nobody asks you a question: you have to
  bring the questions yourself. The answer that matters is a decision (believe it, share it, act on it, or not yet) and,
  for anything about risk or money, how big it really is.
- **Real-world test.** Ten new items in their real form (headline plus article, chart, ad), from areas of life not used
  in the lessons, with no questions, prompts or hints. About four hold up, four do not show what they claim, two do not
  say enough. For each: the verdict (holds up / does not show it / cannot tell from this); the line in the item that
  decides it, or "not given"; and, where there is a risk or a change, the plain size typed ("from 2 in 1,000 to 3 in
  1,000"). **Pass:** verdict and deciding line right on 8 of 10, at most one sound item rejected, plain size right on 4 of
  5. The same test with new items four weeks later; passing both is the end result.
- **Changed from the draft.** "The first place it goes wrong" is a teaching device of an ordered key, not what a reader
  does: any problem that breaks the claim is enough, and when an item has two, either counts. "Cannot tell from this" is
  added because it is the commonest honest answer to a news item. The plain size is added because a sound claim can still
  be tiny, and relative numbers are what persuade (G6).
- **Out.** Doing statistics (significance, confidence intervals, regression); reading a study's methods section; checking a
  source on the web (lateral reading, a separate skill); spotting invented or fraudulent numbers; deciding whether an
  effect matters to you (a value judgement: the subject gives the size, you decide); making charts; building the habit of
  checking unprompted (an optional if-then plan is offered, nothing more is claimed).

*If the learner reached only this, would they handle the real moment?* Yes: the test is the real moment.

## Gate 3. Practice, feedback and delivery

- **Practice method.** The learner judges whole items in their real form, as in the test: reads the item, commits to a
  verdict, taps the sentence that decides it (or "not given"), and types the plain size where there is one. Each lesson
  teaches one new question with a single worked item (the item, the question, the deciding line, the verdict, the size),
  then a short ramp: one item with the question shown and the line pre-marked to confirm, then items with the question
  shown, then items alone. Every set has sound items and "cannot tell" items. From the third lesson, a share of items turn
  on earlier questions, unlabelled. The last lesson gives no questions at all. Review brings back new items of each part,
  mixed, spaced, chosen by the app.
- **Time.** Per lesson about 15 minutes: 2 to 3 minutes reading (the question and one worked item), 10 to 12 minutes
  judging 7 to 10 items. At least two thirds of the time is doing.
- **Feedback.** Right after each item, from the app: the right verdict; the deciding line highlighted in the item; two to
  four sentences of reading in the question's words ("the groups chose themselves, so ..."); what you would need to see
  for it to hold; the plain size worked out with the numbers. Separate running scores for problems found and for sound
  items wrongly rejected (G5).
- **Delivery check.** What the app must do, and whether it can today (engine read 2026-10-10, `public/app/lessons/`):
  - Show a multi-paragraph item (headline, body, source line) on a phone: yes (text cases exist).
  - Tap-to-choose verdicts and reasons: yes.
  - **Tap a sentence of the item as the answer** and check it: partly. (Written blind as "no"; the audit found that
    check cards already let the learner tap pieces of a case, `public/app/lessons/cards.js`.) To extend to every practice
    item, over a multi-paragraph item, with "not given" as a choice.
  - **Show a chart drawn from data** (bar and line, with real axes, including a cut axis): no image or chart in cases. To
    build (an SVG chart from a small data spec).
  - **Take a typed number and check it with a tolerance**: no (free-text notes only, not graded). To build.
  - Score sound-item rejections separately and show them: to check in `records.js`; build if missing.
  - Spaced return of new items per part, chosen by the app: partly (review exists); needs per-part item banks.
  These three or four capabilities are built before any lesson; no lesson swaps them for tap-choices because tap-choices
  exist.

*If the learner did only this practice, would they be closer?* Yes: it is the test's own action, repeated with feedback.

## Gate 4. The parts

Ordered so each builds on the ones before. Every check is a small version of the Gate 2 test: new items, real form, no
hints beyond what the part allows.

| Part | The ability | Check |
|---|---|---|
| A. Find the claim and its number | Say what the item claims (a cause, a comparison, a size, a forecast, "most people") and which number is offered for it; notice when the headline claims more than the article's own numbers or words | 5 new items: tap the claim's kind and the line with its number; verdict where the headline outruns the body. 4 of 5 |
| B. Size it plainly | Turn a relative change, a percentage or a count into "so many in 1,000 before, so many after", tell percent from percentage points, ask "out of how many" of a count | 5 new numbers in items: typed plain size, right within rounding on 4 of 5, and whether the headline's word ("doubles", "slashes") fits |
| C. Compared with what | Is there a comparison, and is it the fair one (same size groups, same period, not a picked start or end, the right base, the same mix of easy and hard cases, and counted the same way at both ends: same rule, same tool, same effort to look) | 6 new items, mixed fair, unfair and none: verdict and deciding line on 5 of 6 |
| D. Who was counted, and how | How many; who chose to answer; who was left out or only the survivors; do they match the people the claim is about (mice, one clinic, an online poll, the seller's customers); who did the counting | 6 new items: verdict and line on 5 of 6 |
| E. Cause or just a link | Were people split by chance; if not, what else could explain it (something else differs between the groups, the arrow runs the other way, a bounce back after an extreme) | 6 new items, including sound randomised ones: verdict and line on 5 of 6 |
| F. Read the chart, not its shape | Read where the axis starts and its scale, the time window, and work out the real change from the numbers | 5 new charts: typed real change on 4 of 5 and verdict |
| G. The whole item, unprompted | Bring all the questions to mixed items, with no prompt, including sound and "cannot tell" items | 8 new items: the Gate 2 test in small (verdict and line on 7 of 8, at most one sound item rejected) |

**Do the parts add up?** The test asks for a verdict (A, C, D, E, F, put together in G), the deciding line (A, C to F),
and the plain size (B, F). If every part's check passes, the test would pass. **Map:** one lesson per part, A to G,
seven lessons; review covers all parts. No lesson without a part.

## Gate 5. The lessons

Every lesson's items are new real-form items in varied areas of life (health, money, work, home, community), sound items
in every set, and an area of life in the end check that the lesson did not use (G4).

**L1. Find the claim and its number** (part A).
- Does: 2 min, one worked item (headline "X cuts Y"; the article's own words say "linked to"; the number and what it
  counts). 10 min, 8 items: tap the claim's kind and the line with the number; verdict (holds / does not show it /
  cannot tell); several items where the headline outruns the body, several sound.
- Feedback: the claim and the number highlighted; where the headline outruns the text, the two lines side by side.
- End check: part A's check.
- Why: G1, G2, G3, G8 (headline exaggeration is the commonest fault, C4), G9.
- Closer to the end result? Yes: it is the first move on every real item.

**L2. Size it plainly** (part B).
- Does: 3 min, one worked item ("halves your risk": picture 1,000 people, 4 become 2), with percent versus percentage
  points shown on the same item. 10 min, 10 items: type the plain size, then say whether the headline's word fits; some
  items have no base ("cannot tell": out of how many?).
- Feedback: the 1,000 people worked out with the item's numbers; the typed answer compared.
- End check: part B's check.
- Why: G6 (C5, C6), G2 (the recurrent step drilled), G1, G9.
- Closer? Yes: the size is part of the test and is what persuades.

**L3. Compared with what** (part C).
- Does: 2 min, one worked pair on the same topic, one fair comparison and one unfair, with the learner told what to
  compare. 10 min, 8 items mixed fair, unfair, none, including a rise that comes from counting differently or looking
  harder (more tests, a new definition); about a third turn on A or B instead.
- Feedback: the deciding line; what the fair comparison would be.
- End check: part C's check.
- Why: G3, G5, G8, G10 (earlier parts mixed in).
- Closer? Yes.

**L4. Who was counted, and how** (part D).
- Does: 2 min, one worked item (an online poll, or a study in mice reported as people). 10 min, 8 items: small samples,
  self-chosen answerers, survivors, the wrong people, the seller's own survey, and sound samples; a third on A to C.
- Feedback: the line that names who was counted, and who the claim is about, side by side.
- End check: part D's check.
- Why: G8 (C4: animal to human), G4 (C1: sample size is the best-supported teachable rule), G5, G10.
- Closer? Yes.

**L5. Cause or just a link** (part E).
- Does: 3 min, one worked pair: the same question answered by a randomised trial (holds) and by a survey of people who
  chose (does not show the cause), with the other explanation named. 10 min, 8 items including bounce-back after an
  extreme and the reversed arrow; a third on A to D.
- Feedback: the line that shows how the groups were formed; the other explanation in one sentence; what a fair test
  would be.
- End check: part E's check.
- Why: G8 (C4: the commonest exaggeration), G3, G4 (C1: Lehman et al. on confounds), G5, G10.
- Closer? Yes.

**L6. Read the chart, not its shape** (part F).
- Does: 2 min, one worked chart drawn two ways from the same data, with the real change worked out from the axis. 10 min,
  8 charts in posts and articles: type the real change, then the verdict; some charts fair.
- Feedback: the axis start and the two values marked; the change worked out; the same data drawn from zero.
- End check: part F's check.
- Why: G7 (C7: a warning does not work, reading the numbers does), G6, G1.
- Closer? Yes: charts are a real-moment input the other lessons do not train.

**L7. The whole item, unprompted** (part G).
- Does: 1 min, recall the questions before seeing them listed (retrieval, not a reread). 12 min, 10 mixed items with no
  question shown, sound, faulty and "cannot tell" in an unpredictable order, a plain size wherever one applies.
- Feedback: as in every lesson, plus the learner's two scores (problems found; sound items rejected).
- End check: part G's check (the Gate 2 test in small).
- Why: G1, G2, G5, G10.
- Closer? Yes: it is the end result.

**Review** (all parts): new items per part, mixed, on the app's spaced schedule (about a day or two, a week, three to four
weeks; R4, P24), with the Gate 2 test itself at four weeks.

## Audit of the existing lessons (2026-10-10)

Written after gates 1 to 5. Read: `public/subjects/stats/key.js`, `u1.unit.js`, sample case files (`u1.cases-drill-2.js`,
`u5.cases-drill-1.js`), and the six learner views `docs/learner-view/stats-u1.md` to `stats-u6.md` (structure, word
counts, and sample screens).

**What the existing subject is.** A classification key: one gate question ("Where does this claim first go wrong?") with
five answers in a fixed order, then a branch per answer naming one of 17 outcomes (Survivorship bias, Non-response bias,
Simpson's paradox, Regression to the mean and so on). Every unit teaches names with cards, then drills: a two-to-four
sentence story written by the author, and a tap on one name from the list the question supplies.

**Measured against gate 3.** Per unit, the teaching cards run about 6,300 to 8,100 words (learner-view count, including
check feedback); the drill's stories total about 650 to 980 words (16 to 22 items of about 40 words each). Most of a
unit's time is reading. No item contains a chart (0 mentions of chart, graph or axis in six units). No item asks the
learner for a number: the arithmetic (relative risk to counts, base rates as 1 in 6) appears only in the feedback. No
item has a headline over a longer text, no item has "cannot tell from this" as an answer, and the decisive fact is
usually stated outright in the story ("It does not say less than what", "The page gives no counts", "The maker changed
the test this year").

| Unit | Verdict | Why (against gates 4 and 5) |
|---|---|---|
| U1 Before you believe a number (the five families, "first place" order, baseline) | **goes** | Teaches the key's sorting order, which no part needs; its drill is the nearest thing to part G but on short author stories, with the answer chosen from a supplied list and no size. The idea of a baseline before Unit One survives as part G's check given first. Part A (claim and number, headline against body) is not taught anywhere. |
| U2 Four sound claims and how far each goes | **goes** | No part is "name which kind of sound claim this is". Sound items belong in every lesson (G5), not in a unit of their own. Its worked material on lottery sampling and the randomised trial is the sound side of parts D and E and is rewritten there. |
| U3 Who was counted (four named biases) | **changes** | Right part (D). Wrong practice: telling Self-selection from Non-response bias by name is not the real response; the real one is the verdict and the line that shows who was counted, on a real-form item, sound samples included. |
| U4 What the number counts (gaming a target, a new way of counting, more looking) | **changes** | Its strongest ideas (a rise from counting differently or looking harder) are real and were thin in the blind design; they are now folded into part C ("counted the same way at both ends"). Gaming a target is a policy question more than a news-number one; at most one item. The three-name taxonomy goes. |
| U5 What it is compared with (relative risk, base rate, Simpson's paradox) | **changes** | Right parts (B and C), wrong action: the learner taps "A percentage without the numbers" and reads the arithmetic in the feedback, where gate 5 has them type the plain size (G6: building the frequency picture is what teaches it). Simpson's paradox becomes one kind of unfair comparison in C, not a name. |
| U6 What caused what (no comparison group, confounding, reverse, regression) | **changes** | Right part (E). Wrong practice: picking among four named explanations on short stories. The real response is the verdict, the line that shows how the groups were formed, and the other explanation in a sentence, with sound randomised items mixed in. |

**Not covered by any existing unit:** part A (the headline claims more than the study: the commonest exaggeration, C4),
part B as something the learner does, part F (charts), part G (whole real-form items with no questions given), and the
verdict "cannot tell from this".

**Overall verdict: rebuild from scratch.** Four of six units have the right topic but the wrong practice, two have no
part, and three parts have no unit. Changing the practice changes every item, every card and the key's role, so
adjusting would rewrite everything anyway while keeping the key's shape, which is the problem. The item-writing know-how
in the existing stories (sound/faulty pairs on the same topic, varied areas of life) is worth reusing as a reference,
not as content.

**The three most important problems.**
1. **Practice does not match the real moment.** The input is a short story written so the decisive fact is stated
   outright; the response is choosing a named category from a list the question provides. In real life the item is a
   headline and article or a chart, the deciding fact is buried or missing, and nobody supplies the questions. There are
   no charts, no headline-against-body items and no "cannot tell" answer.
2. **Most time is reading, and much of it teaches names the end result does not need.** About 6,300 to 8,100 words of
   cards per unit against about 40-word drill stories; 17 outcome names, 5 families and an order rule ("first place",
   with tie-breaks) are learned, where the end result needs a verdict, a deciding line and a size.
3. **The procedure half is never practised.** Turning "cuts risk by half" or "99% accurate" into so many in 1,000 is done
   only in the feedback text; the learner never computes a plain size, so the part of a claim that persuades most (C5)
   goes untrained.
