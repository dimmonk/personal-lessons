## The way of deciding, in my own words (written before each first practice)

- l1: Say what the headline really claims (cause, link, or just a fact; about whom). Find the sentence with the number under it. Then ask whether the text itself backs the claim. If the text shows the claim goes too far (only compared groups, other people, other explanations named, small print), it does not show it. If it simply lacks something needed, I cannot tell. If the text backs it, it holds up.
- l2: Turn a count into so many in every 1,000 (count / total x 1,000), then compare it with the usual. Above the usual: "more than usual" holds. Same or below: it does not. No total or no usual: cannot tell, unless the text says in words it is usual.
- l3: Find the risk before, take the percent off, say "from X in 1,000 to Y in 1,000 over this time". Then check the headline's size: a share holds if the before/after drop by that share; "spares half the people" holds only if that many fewer people have it. Percent with no starting risk: cannot tell.
- l4: Picture 1,000 people. Count who has it, how many the test finds, how many false alarms among the rest, add the positives, see how many are real. Most real: "a positive means you probably have it" holds. Most false: it does not. Missing how common it is or the false-alarm rate: cannot tell.
- l5: Find what the number is compared with, then ask whether it is fair: same period, same counting, rates not totals, same mix. Nothing to compare with, or sizes missing: cannot tell. Unfair comparison shown in the text: does not show it.

I could state each in plain words before the practice, so no defect on that rule.

## Defects that decide the verdict

L4 (FAIL)

1. L4 Item 8 (allergy blood test, "Step 4: Questions"). Count: 60 have it, test finds 48, flags 47 of the 940 others, so 95 positive and 48 real. 48 of 95 is a little over half, so by the lesson's own rule ("If most of the positives are real, a positive most likely means you have it holds up") a careful learner who counts correctly answers "Holds up", and is marked wrong. The feedback says "about half: a positive result is close to a coin toss", which is not the rule taught. Both answers defensible, and the marked one contradicts the rule. Fix: change the numbers so the real share is clearly under half (for example 60 in 1,000, finds 8 in 10, flags 10 in 100: 48 of 142, about 1 in 3), or move the item to the "holds up" side. Re-check the other generated variants for a share between 45% and 55% (the seeded Items 11 to 14 are 28%, 44%, 87%, 94%, fine).

No other defect I found would make a beginner stall or pass without the reasoning in l1, l2, l3, l5. L4 otherwise passes: the typed counts are all arithmetically right (I checked Items 1, 2, 5, 6, 9, 11 to 14 and check 16 to 18).

## Defects that do not decide a verdict (polish and risks)

P1. L1 Check 13 (SkyBridge, "any other airline"). Marked "Does not show it". A second option is defensible: the other airlines' rates are not in the text, so "Cannot tell". The step 1 rule says "it is about other people" counts as does-not-show, and the small print says "Other airlines were not included", which is what decides it, but the feedback should say that line is the "words that say the claim goes too far". Same pattern as practice Item 1 (tire), so a learner who got that one can follow. Fix: add to the feedback "the small print itself says the others were left out".
P2. L1 Step 1 is dense: claim types, number, three verdicts, and the "missing number does not always mean cannot tell" caveat in one screen. I needed a second read. Fix: split the verdict paragraph into its own step, or give the three verdicts one-line examples.
P3. L3 never states why "a coin toss decided" matters; it just appears in reasons ("the coin toss means the spray made the difference"). L1 only hints at it in "what you would need to see". Fix: one line in L3 Step 1: "When the text says people were split by chance, a 'makes' claim can stand; if not, the text can only show a link."
P4. L3 Check 18 (tutoring): students in the program vs outside it, non-random, headline "keeps 1 in 5 from failing". Marked on size only (1 in 50). Right answer either way, but the feedback skips that the groups are self-selected. Fix: add a clause.
P5. L2 Step 1 teaches only "more than usual". Check 16 (fewer than usual) and Item 1-style claims need the mirror rule. Easy by reasoning, but add one sentence: "A claim of fewer than usual holds when your number is below the usual."
P6. L2 wording slips in the shared note "That is the usual level, not this year's count": shown on items about last month, yesterday and last week (Items 6, 8, Check 18). Fix: "not this period's count".
P7. L2 "What you would need to see" in Items 2, 6, 8: "a count above the usual ... which is 2,400" reads as if 2,400 is enough; it is the usual. Fix: "more than 2,400".
P8. L2 does not say how far above usual counts as "soar" or "hits hard"; all items are 2x or 3x or exactly equal, so no one stalls, but a 1.1x case would be arguable.
P9. L5 Item 8 (Harbor Books): website sales left out; marked "Holds up". A skeptical beginner may call that cherry-picking. The claim is explicitly about the four stores and the ad says so, so I accept it, but the feedback could say "the claim is only about the stores, and it says so".
P10. L4 Item 7 (bank): the typed counting step is skipped because no numbers are given, so the learner never sees the 1,000 people picture for this item. Fine; the review sentence in the text decides.
P11. Answer-position patterns: in every verdict question the order is Holds up / Does not show / Cannot tell, and the right answer maps to a content type, not a position, so no position shortcut. The tap-the-sentence question is often answerable by "the sentence with digits"; the real reasoning is in the verdict. Low risk.
P12. L0 baseline: Q1 option "Students who go to bed late have lower grades" is true of the survey and might look equally right to a first-time reader. It is ungraded and its job is to show the starting point, so acceptable.
