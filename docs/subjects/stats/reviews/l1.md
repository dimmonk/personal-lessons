## Before

Given a headline with its article on my phone, I would read the headline, maybe the first line, and believe it unless it
smelled like an ad. I would not read the small print or compare what the headline says with what the text says. I had no
way to name what kind of claim it is, and no "cannot tell" answer: a claim was either true or a lie to me. I would have
taken "Coffee cuts heart attack risk by 12%" at face value.

## Notes, in order

  I would not have guessed and could not apply to real life; it later contradicts Question 7 (below). The examples "30 of
  180" and "one in six" are the exact numbers of check question 9, so that check item's kind is recall, not new material.
- **Step 2 (coffee).** The best screen. Taught me: "cuts" is a cause word; look for the number in the text; "a link, not

- **Q2 (crosswalk, help shown).** The tap gives itself away: the only marked body sentence is the answer. Taught me

## After: what I can do now that I could not before

- Read past the headline to the text and the small print, and say when the text covers less than the headline: who was
  compared (tires Q1 -> airline check 12), what situation the number came from (heater Q6).
- Tell a claim that only reports a change (holds up when the counts match: crosswalk Q2 -> shelter check 13) from one that
  credits a cause the text does not show (coffee worked example, walking Q5 -> 5K check 10).
- Say "cannot tell from this" when no number in the text measures the claim, instead of calling it true or false
  (savings Q3, housing Q8 -> diner check 11).
- Find the one sentence that carries the claim's number and pass over the other numbers in the text (distractor notes in Q4,
  Q5 -> check 9, 11).
- Name the kind of claim from its words (cause, comparison, size, forecast, most people), with the caveats below.

## The five answers

- **Gain: yes.** Concretely: on a new headline plus article, I read the text and small print against the headline and say
  "holds up / does not show it / cannot tell", pointing to the sentence with the number. Before, I would have believed the
  headline. Tied to the coffee example, Q1, Q2, Q3.
- **Doing: yes.** Two short reading screens, then 13 items in their real form (news, ad with small print, post, report
  with a chart), each answered by choosing the kind, tapping the line and giving the verdict.
- **Check: yes.** Five new items in the real form (school, running, diner, airline, shelter), no help, no answers until the
  end, scoring the verdict, the sentence and the kind, which is part A's check. One weakness: check 9's numbers are Step 1's
  own examples, so its kind ask is recall.
- **Clear: nothing blocks, but four places confuse a beginner.**
  1. Step 1's "size = share below half, most people = share above half" gives the wrong answer on Q7 (94% of orders is a
     size), and the feedback does not explain. Fix: define "most people" as a claim about what people do or think with a
     "most / four in five / everyone" word, and size as how big or common anything is, whatever the share; make the kind
     feedback name the deciding word ("'94% of orders' counts orders, not what people think: a size").
  2. Q6's kind: the headline reads as a forecast and the reason says "will cut". Reword the reason ("says the heater cuts
     your bill", a cause word) or the headline.
  3. Small-print tap notes in Q1 ("the speed of the test") and Q6 ("how long the test ran") misdescribe the sentence that
     decides the verdict. Say "That says what the test compared, not the result."
  4. Q3: the marked sentence is the wrong tap and "That is not the sentence." gives no reason. Say "That sentence has no
     number in it."
  Also, check 11 (diner): "busier than ever" is a comparison word and is a deciding line, so the kind is shaky in the check.
- **Waste:** the size/most-people half-threshold rule (replace as above); Q2's tap, which the help marks give away (mark only
  the headline in the helped round, or leave the number line unmarked); the reuse of "30 of 180" / "one in six" in Step 1
  (use other example numbers). Nothing else could go without losing the gain.
