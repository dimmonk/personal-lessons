## Before

As a beginner adult: I read a headline with a number and mostly believe it, unless it is an ad, where I am vaguely
suspicious of everything. I do not read the article or the small print against the headline. I have no way to say
"this does not show it" versus "there is nothing here to check"; both feel like "hmm". I could not name what kind of
claim a headline makes.

## Going through it (as a learner)

later: the line between "how big or how common" and "what most people do or think" is never drawn, and "better" is
given as a comparison word.

**Worked example (coffee).** Good. I picked "cause" from "cuts" and the working confirmed why. What I took from it and
used later: put the headline's verb next to the article's own words; if the people compared already differed in other
ways, the headline says more than the text. The 25 vs 22 in 1,000 picture is nice but I did nothing with it here.

## After (what I can do now that I could not before)

- Read the small print or the body against the headline and see that it compares something narrower than the headline
  says (Q1 tires, Q6 heater; used on k-boarding).
- See that a cause headline rests on people who already differed, so the text does not show the cause (worked coffee,
  Q5; used on k-5k).
- Say "cannot tell from this" when the claim has no number behind it, even when other numbers are lying around
  (Q3, Q8; used on k-diner, where 14 dishes and 42 seats are not the number).
- Not reject a sound claim just because it has a number in it (Q4, Q7; used on k-reading, k-shelter).

## The five answers

- **Gain: yes.** I can now hold an ad's or a story's headline against its own small print or body and say "does not
  show it", "cannot tell" or "holds up", and point to the sentence. Before, I would have believed the SkyBridge ad.
- **Doing: yes.** About 3 minutes of reading, the rest is judging whole items in their real form. One third of every
  item, though, is the claim-kind label, which is the least like the real moment and caused all of my misses.
- **Check: not yet.** Five new items, real form, new areas of life, sound items included: the right test. But an item
  counts only if all three answers are right, and k-diner's kind answer goes against the cue Step 1 teaches ("better"
  is a comparison). A learner who learned exactly what the lesson taught loses that item on the label, which uses up
  the whole margin of a 4-of-5 rule.
- **Clear: no, in four places.**
  1. Size versus "most people" is never drawn (Q4 "four in five drive" is "most people"; k-reading "one in six" is
     size). A beginner cannot tell the rule from the screens.
  2. k-diner's kind contradicts Step 1's "better" cue.
  3. Q3's post makes two claims of two kinds; only one is accepted.
  4. Q2 "Crashes fell after new lights went in" is the classic implied-cause headline, taught as a sound comparison;
     it confuses now and will contradict the cause lesson (part E) later.
  Minor: in Q1 the headline carries the number but cannot be tapped; the prompt could say "in the text under the
  headline".
- **Waste:** the Q7 chart (it repeats the 94%); the Q5 redraw-from-zero is part F material and could wait. Nothing
  else; the set is not padded.

## Fixes needed

1. Step 1: one sentence drawing the line, e.g. "A share above half ('most', 'four in five', 'everyone') is a 'most
   people' claim; a smaller share or a count is a size." (Or merge the two kinds.)
2. k-diner: make the kind unambiguous (headline and body with no "better"), or accept both comparison and most people.
3. Q3 savings: cut "Everyone I know is moving their savings to Pinecone Bank", or accept both kinds.
4. Q2 crosswalk: replace with a sound comparison whose headline implies no cause (or make it a cause item that does
   not show it). Keep "fell after X" for the cause lesson.
5. Minor: the tap prompt says "in the text under the headline"; drop the Q7 chart.
