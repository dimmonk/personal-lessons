# Learner review, stats l0 to l5 (cold beginner, learner views only)

Note on method: the learner views print "(right)" beside answers, so I could not be truly blind. I wrote my own answer from the text before taking in each mark, and I also note where a mark could be guessed without reasoning. Scores below are my own reasoned answers.

## Defects that cut across all lessons (G)

- G1 (pattern). Tapping "Not given" is always followed by "Cannot tell"; tapping a real sentence is never followed by "Cannot tell". True in every lesson (l1 Item 3, 8, Check 11; l2 Item 3, 6, 13, 18; l3 Item 3, 6, 13, Check 18; l4 Item 3, 6, 12, Check 16; l5 Item 3, 9, Check 15). The three-way verdict is really two-way after the tap. Fix: add some "Not given" items that are really "does not show" (e.g. a number exists but is about something else), and some "Cannot tell" items where a number is tapped (the number is there but the one thing needed is missing).
- G2 (pattern). Ads are never "Holds up" (l1 three ads, l3 two, l4 four, l5 one). The "Ad" label alone gives the verdict. Fix: include one ad whose small print supports it.
- G3 (pattern). On "What is this claiming?" the distractor with a broadening word ("every", "whole", "any", "across the county/state", "anyone") is always wrong, and the right option reuses the headline's own verb. A beginner can answer without reading the text. Fix: make some right options carry "every/all" (when the headline does) and some wrong options reuse the headline verb.
- G4 (position). The sentence to tap: l5 it is the first body sentence in all 11 non-"Not given" items; l0/l1 almost always the first body sentence; l4 always the sentence starting "Among"; l3 the one starting "Without/Before". A learner can tap by position. Fix: shuffle sentence order across items.
- G5 (feedback). The two items that would teach the pattern-breaking (a number that exists but does not answer the question) are tagged only with a one-line note; nothing says why the verdict is "Cannot tell" instead of "Does not show" when the text gives a caveat vs gives nothing. A one-line decision rule on the screen after Step 1 in l1 would fix it.

## l1 What it claims, and the number it rests on

My words: find what the headline says (cause or just together, and who), tap the sentence with the number, then say if the text backs the headline, does not, or has nothing to check. I can say that after Step 2.
Check (Q9 to Q13): holds up, does not show, cannot tell, does not show, holds up. 5 of 5, though G1/G2/G4 would have got me there.
Verdict: PASS with defects.
1. Item 2 (crosswalk lights): "Holds up" is arguable. 19 against 31 over two years, no other corners, and the lesson itself says things "could explain it". Step 1 never says that a headline using "after" is a plain count claim. Fix: add a line in Step 1: "'fell after' only says it fell; it does not say why."
2. Check 11 (diner): the claim options leave out the most accurate reading, "The owner says customers like it". "Most customers like it better" is stated as the claim though only the owner says so. Fix: add that option as the right one, or make the claim "Customers like the new menu".
3. Step 1 gives three verdict words but not how to pick between "does not show" and "cannot tell" (G5).
4. Item 4 (82% "four in five"): the headline rounds 82% down, which a beginner could count as overstating. Fix: say "a little over".

## l2 How big it is: out of how many

My words: find how many it is out of, work out the count per 1,000, compare with the usual; no total means cannot tell.
Check (Q14 to Q18): holds up, holds up, does not show, does not show, cannot tell. 5 of 5, but Q17 needed a step not taught (see 1).
Verdict: FAIL.
1. Check 17 (75 of 1,500 loaves) and the bakery claim: Step 1 teaches "count the groups of a thousand, divide". 1,500 is 1.5 groups, and 75 divided by 1.5 is never shown. Item 1 and the worked example only use whole groups. Same gap in l3 (G-l3-1). Fix: teach the other direction: "if the total is not a thousand, scale: 75 of 1,500 is the same as 50 of 1,000" and give one such example in Step 1.
2. Step 3, "What you would need to see: about 2,400 or more": 2,400 is exactly the usual (6 per 1,000 of 400,000), and the lesson says "holds up only when above the usual". Fix: "more than 2,400".
3. Item 5 (bus): "54,000 bus rides a day" is rides, but the usual "30 of every 1,000 residents ride the bus" is riders. Rides and residents are not the same unit; the "exactly the usual" answer depends on treating them alike. Fix: change to "54,000 riders a day".
4. Item 3, 6, 13, 18: the tap is "Not given" though the sentence holds a count. After l1 ("The text does give one" appears when you choose it wrongly) this is a reversal; the question wording changed to "numbers you need to size it" without saying so. Fix: say on the first such screen "A count with no total is not enough".
5. Items 9 to 12 are four near-copies (same sentences, new numbers). Items 9 and 10 have the same answer (12 in 1,000, twice the usual). The learner learns the template, not the reasoning. Fix: vary the structure (put the usual first, change the unit, one item where the headline understates).
6. The result of every "more than usual" item turns on strong words (flood, soars, boom, jumped) versus plain words (double, fewer): the plain-word items hold up, the dramatic-word ones do not, except Items 9, 10 which are plain and hold. Mostly a pattern. Fix: add a dramatic-word item that holds up.

## l3 A risk, before and after

My words: a percent cut is a change; find the starting risk in 1,000, take the percent off, say from X to Y over a time; the headline holds up if it says no more than that, not if it says "spares half the people".
Check (Q14 to Q18): holds up, holds up, does not show, does not show, cannot tell. I get 5 of 5 if I read Q15 as OK, but I was torn on Q15 and Q18 (see 3, 4); 3 to 5 of 5 depending.
Verdict: FAIL.
1. Scaling to 1,000 is untaught: Item 1 (150 of 500), Item 7 (240 of 2,000), Items 9, 10 (of 2,000) need it. Step 1 teaches only taking the percent off. The feedback ("counts out of 500, out of 1,000 they are twice as big") appears only after a wrong answer. Fix: teach scaling in Step 1 (links to l2 defect 1).
2. Item 6 (fish survey, "Eating fish cuts depression by 30%"): marked "Cannot tell". It is an observational survey with a cause word, which l1 taught as "does not show". Two defensible. Fix: make it a trial, or remove the cause word.
3. Check 18 (airbag ad): marked "Cannot tell", but the feedback itself says crash tests are not real crashes, which is a "does not show" reason. Two defensible. Fix: drop the crash-test phrase or move the mark.
4. Check 15 (booster seats): marked "Holds up" on "crashes in the same kinds of cars, roads and speeds". The groups are not randomised and parents who use boosters may differ; l1 marks the same design "does not show". Fix: say the seat was assigned by lottery, or mark it "does not show".
5. "Dramatically" and "slashes" decide Item 5 and Check 17, but "halves" holds in Item 4 (6 to 3) and Check 14. Check 17 (2 to 1 per 1,000, "halves") is a halving too. The line is the adjective, not a taught size, and Step 1 lists those very words (G-pattern). Fix: teach a size rule or remove the adjectives from the decisive position.
6. Items 11 and 12 are near-copies of the worked example (Zelvin); all four "protects N% of people" items are answered by that phrase. Item 12 feedback reads "spares 1 people". Fix: vary wording; fix grammar.
7. Item 8 (helmet law): "Does not show" is right for the size reason, but a before/after with no comparison group is also a l1 problem and not mentioned. Minor.

## l4 A test says yes

My words: picture 1,000 people; count who has it, how many of those the test finds, how many of the rest it wrongly flags; then ask what share of positives are real. Most real = a positive probably means it; mostly false alarms = it does not.
Check (Q13 to Q16): holds up, does not show, does not show, cannot tell. 4 of 4 by counting (checked all the arithmetic: Check 13 540/560, Check 14 45/140, Check 15 18/116 are right).
Verdict: PASS with defects.
1. G4 and wording pattern: the tap is always the "Among ..." sentence; hedged headlines ("usually", "almost always right") hold up and unhedged ones ("means you have", "will") do not, in every item. Fix: add one "usually right" headline that fails and one unhedged one that holds.
2. Template leakage: "The test finds 90 of every 100 people who have a real fault" for cars; "people who have invasive seeds" for seed batches; "Picture 1,000 people" for cars and batches (Check 13, Check 15). Sounds wrong and costs a re-read. Fix: item-specific nouns in the generated sentence and the typed prompt.
3. Items 8 and 9 are the same numbers (18 of 116); 10 and 11 are the same (450 of 475 / 450 of 500). Fix: vary numbers and structure.
4. "90% accurate" in the headline is never tied to the two numbers in the text ("finds 90 of 100", "wrongly flags 5 of 100"). Are they the same thing? The wrong-idea box helps but a beginner will wonder. Fix: one sentence in Step 1: "'Accurate' is the headline's word for the first number; the text gives both."
5. The typed answer needs 5% of 960, 10% of 990, 5% of 940. Fine with the calculator but Step 1 never shows how to take "5 in 100" of a number. Fix: one example line.
6. Check pass rule: "none turned down a claim that holds up" with only one "holds up" item in the check means one miss on Check 13 alone fails; make clear on the screen.

## l5 Compared with what

My words: find what the number is compared with; check it is fair: same period, same way of counting, rates not totals, same mix, and not a figure people are paid to raise.
Check (Q10 to Q15): does not show, does not show, holds up, holds up, does not show, cannot tell. 6 of 6 on the verdict; on the claim question I miss Check 11 and Check 15 (see 1).
Verdict: FAIL.
1. The "What is this claiming?" key is a reinterpretation of the headline, so the claim answer and the verdict contradict. Item 2 ("Brookdale recycles twice as much as Fenwick"): the right claim is "households recycle twice as much", yet the headline says total, and the total is shown. Item 4 ("capital"): right claim is "most likely to be stolen". Check 11 ("corn leader"): right claim is "more corn for their size", although the natural reading is total, which the text shows. Check 15: "better than the old one" is not in the post. And in the worked example, Item 6, Item 7, Check 10, Check 14 the right claim is literally true in the text ("55% higher than two years ago", "60% higher than before") but the verdict is "Does not show". A beginner who answers the claim correctly is then told the text does not show it. Fix: state the implied claim in the options ("Break-ins are rising", "Sales are booming now") so the verdict follows from it.
2. Item 5 (Dale and Hillcrest): this is a reversal inside groups. Step 1 gives only the phrase "the same mix inside the totals". A beginner cannot be expected to find that 82% against 63% flips in each course; the story hands the subgroup rates over, which makes it a trick, not a method. Fix: teach it with a plain example in Step 2 (a "A / B" pair) before Item 5.
3. Check 10 (season tickets): the tap is ambiguous. The first sentence is the headline's comparison (February), the second is the fair one (last June). "Says what it is compared with" fits both. Fix: ask "Tap the sentence the headline's percent compares with."
4. The second sentence gives the answer: "same ... both years" means holds up (Item 1, 8, Check 12, 13), "started counting / only / new" means does not (Item 6, 7, Check 14). Reading one word does it (G4).
5. Check 15: two claim options are defensible ("better than the old one" and "better than other restaurants"); the post names neither. Fix: change the right option to "Customers love the new burger".
6. Step 2's A/B table is fine, but it is not a question, and the "Compare:" text just states the answer. Fix: ask which is fair before showing it.
