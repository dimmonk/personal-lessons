# Psychology: the plan (build-subject, 2026-10-18)

Replaces the design record (`design.md`, kept as history) and the pilot "What did they do?". Built from the owner's own course,
`psychology/Identifying-Psychological-Patterns-Practical-Course.md`, which is the spine: its five diagnostic questions come first and every
case is decided by them, the way Ideology's five questions are (the owner asked for that approach back for Ideology, 2026-10-17, and
for Psychology, 2026-10-18: "teach the framework first and the examples second").

**After this you can** hear a story about someone (a friend's account of an ex, a coworker's email thread, your own 2 a.m. thought) and say
what kind of thing it is, whether it is one moment or a lasting pattern, the name that fits or that there is too little to tell, and
what to do about it, **when** someone reaches for "narcissist", "gaslighting" or "toxic", or when your own head runs away with you.

**The test:** ten new stories of the kinds in the course's Lesson 6, named or marked "too little to tell", with the response that fits;
8 of 10.

**The way of thinking:** five questions, in this order, and one naming rule.
1. What kind of thing is it? A mind talking itself into something (a moment of reasoning), a move made on someone (in an interaction), a
   lasting way someone is, or an ordinary reaction that needs no name. Why: it decides which set of names you even look in; mixing the three
   is the most common error.
2. One moment or a lasting pattern? A reaction to a hard time, once or twice with no pattern, over and over for months or years,
   or the story does not say. Why: it separates a trait or a disorder from a bad day. Check the situation before the character: we blame other
   people's character and our own circumstances (the fundamental attribution error, Ross 1977), so look for stress, grief, sleep or a
   new job first.
3. What does it do for them? Ease a contradiction, protect a conclusion, keep admiration coming, keep someone from leaving, get out of blame.
   Why: the function, not the surface, separates look-alikes (attention against admiration, panic against contempt).
4. What is the nearest look-alike, and what separates them? Why: it forces the one detail that decides instead of a vibe.
5. What would prove this read wrong? Why: it keeps the name a guess you can drop, not a verdict.

**The naming rule:** a name is given only when the story shows its telltale signs together (`PSY_SIGNATURE`, items-shape.js), and a
lasting way someone is needs years and many people; otherwise "too little to tell", with what you would need to see. Judge the shape,
never how unpleasant the person is. "Toxic", "narc", "sociopath" and "main character energy" are insults, not names.

**What it teaches** (every topic of the owner's course, plus three practical additions proposed in the thread on 2026-10-18):
the five questions and the trap of pathologizing a moment; dissonance and its four moves, confirmation bias, motivated reasoning, sunk cost,
an honest change of mind; confidence, self-esteem and narcissism, grandiose and vulnerable, traits against a pattern; borderline, histrionic
and antisocial against narcissistic, unpleasant is not a diagnosis, words that are not diagnoses; defense mechanisms (denial, projection,
rationalization, displacement, intellectualization); gaslighting, DARVO, love-bombing then devaluation, projection, "everything becomes
manipulation"; the faulty claims; the mixed drill; the honest caveats. Added: **situation before character** (in lesson 1); **everyday
thinking traps** (catastrophizing, mind reading, all-or-nothing, lesson 3, from cognitive therapy: Beck 1979, Burns 1980); **anchors
and hindsight** (lesson 2b, Tversky & Kahneman 1974, Fischhoff 1975; offered in the thread and taken as the recommended pick); **what to do
once you have named it** (one response per name, taught with each name and asked in lessons 3, 6 and 7). Proposed and left out unless the
owner asks: persuasion tricks (Scams covers them) and mental-health warning signs.

**The lessons**, each opening with the questions it uses and why, a worked case answered question by question, then stories answered
question by question with feedback that names the question that decided, look-alikes side by side, earlier material mixed in from lesson 2
on, and a check on new stories.

**Decided in the build (2026-10-18), from the reviews:** every story of a lesson offers that lesson's one list of names (the list's shape never
gives the answer away); Question 1 is asked only where it decides; "ordinary" (the story shows enough to see a normal reaction, slip or
disagreement) against "too little to tell" (a name is reached for and the story has neither its signs nor enough to rule it out); the
response to each name is taught with it in every lesson; Question 2's pattern answer is "over and over, for months or years", so a move
repeated on one person fits it, while a lasting way someone is still needs many people (Question 1).

The reviews (learner and research) are in `reviews/`.

**Sources the way of thinking rests on** (the field's own practice, not a layout from another subject; the research note is `docs/research/psychology.md`):
- The order of the questions is the order of a clinical assessment: what kind of problem, how lasting (DSM-5's general criteria for a
  personality disorder: an enduring pattern, pervasive across situations, onset by early adulthood, causing impairment; APA 2013), what
  function it serves, the differential (what else it could be), and what would rule it out.
- Situation before character: the fundamental attribution error (Ross 1977; Gilbert & Malone 1995).
- Dissonance and its reduction (Festinger 1957; Aronson's self-justification work); confirmation bias (Nickerson 1998); motivated
  reasoning (Kunda 1990); sunk cost (Arkes & Blumer 1985); escalation of commitment (Staw 1976).
- Anchoring (Tversky & Kahneman 1974); hindsight bias (Fischhoff 1975).
- Thinking traps and testing a thought (Beck 1979, cognitive therapy; Burns 1980).
- Grandiose and vulnerable narcissism (Pincus & Lukowitsky 2010; Miller et al. 2011); the Cluster B criteria and their overlap (DSM-5;
  the dimensional Alternative Model in Section III); psychopathy (Hare).
- DARVO (Freyd 1997); gaslighting as a pattern that makes the target doubt their own perception (Sweet 2019); love-bombing as early
  idealization followed by devaluation (Strutzenberg et al. 2017).
- Defense mechanisms (Vaillant 1992).
