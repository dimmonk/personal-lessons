# psychology: design record (build-subject skill)

Written 2026-10-10. Gates 1 to 5 were worked blind on 2026-10-10 (the existing units, cases, key and learner views were
not read until the gates below were written); the audit of the existing lessons follows them.

Approved by the owner: 2026-10-10, "approve all, keep going" (gates 2, 3 and the pilot, for every subject; the pilot is still to be tried on a phone, and what it shows comes back into this record).

**Revised after that approval (needs one line from the owner).** The approved draft said: check what you are looking at
before calling someone "narcissist" or "manipulative", and name it from what they did. The blind pass keeps that core and
adds two things the real moment needs: whether the act shows one bad moment or a pattern (the main error, O1), and what
to do next (ask, watch, set a limit, get help). It also adds the costly miss: a real pattern of control told as if it
were nothing (O6). The draft's test ("you say what it is") became a test of doing: tap, judge, choose the next step.

## Gate 1. The kind of learning, then the evidence

**Kinds.** Judging situations: an account of what someone did, with a word attached, has to be judged (does the word fit,
is this one moment or a pattern). A habit rides on it: in real life the label comes first and fast, so the learner has to
stop and run the check before using the word. The app can train the judgment; it can only prompt the habit.

**Evidence.** `docs/research/reading-other-people.md` (O1 to O9, added for this subject); `docs/research/review-after-lessons.md`
(R1 to R9); `docs/learning-science.md`, used for its sources only, not for its assumption that every subject is a key of
named outcomes.

**Principles that drive the design.**
1. **One act is not a trait.** People read a lasting disposition from behavior the situation explains (O1, Gilbert &
   Malone 1995). Most items turn on this step, and "one moment, ordinary reason" is a frequent right answer.
2. **Each popular word is checked against what it requires.** Harm and disorder words have stretched to milder cases
   (O2, Haslam 2016); most people called narcissists do not have the disorder, which by definition is a lasting pattern
   across situations (O5, DSM-5 range 0–6.2%). Each word is taught with its requirement and the everyday look-alike it
   gets stretched over.
3. **A concrete "what else?" step, not a warning.** "Be fair" leaves the bias intact; asking "what else would explain
   this, what would I need to see?" removes it (O3, Lord, Lepper & Preston 1984; "be careful" null results in
   learning-science P14).
4. **Asking beats imagining.** Taking the other's perspective raises confidence, not accuracy; getting it does (O4,
   Eyal, Steffel & Epley 2018). "Can't tell yet: ask them" is a right answer.
5. **Discrimination, not suspicion: ordinary cases in the mix.** Fraud training that included legitimate offers made
   people warier of the bad ones without much effect on the good (learning-science P26, Burke et al. 2022); about half of
   all accounts are ordinary, so the learner is trained not to over-label.
6. **The costly miss is control, and it is told apart by pattern.** Coercive control is several kinds of control
   together, repeated (monitoring, isolation, money, threats), not one fight (O6, Johnson's typology); it usually
   arrives minimized. Knowing the blame-reversing tactic DARVO reduced its effect on observers (O7, Harsey & Freyd 2020).
7. **Practice on instances with feedback, in the real form.** Bias training works as practice with personal feedback
   and held at two months (O8, Morewedge et al. 2015); transfer is larger when practice asks for the same response as
   real use (R8, Pan & Rickard 2018, d = 0.58 vs 0.28). The input is an account as it arrives, the response is a judgment
   and a next step.
8. **Mix the look-alikes, after each is met once.** Mixed practice helps tell related kinds apart (R9, Rohrer et al.
   2020; learning-science P22, limited for verbal categories, so a judgement call to be measured).
9. **Spaced returns on new accounts, scheduled by the app.** Recall in later sessions until right, on a different
   account each time (R1 to R5, learning-science P24).
10. **The real-life step is a plan, and it is honest about its evidence.** If-then plans help held goals (learning-science
    P26, Sheeran et al. 2025); none was tested for carrying a judgment into one's own heated moments.

## Gate 2. The end result

- **After this subject you can** check a word like "narcissist", "gaslighting" or "manipulative" against what the
  person actually did: say what they did, whether it shows one bad moment or a pattern, and what to do next, **when** a
  real person's behavior upsets you, or a friend tells you about someone and reaches for the word.
- **Real moment.** The label arrives first: in your own head after an argument, or in a friend's message ("he's such a
  narcissist", "I think she's gaslighting me"). You have one side of the story and one or a few incidents. Sometimes it
  arrives the other way: a friend describes something worrying and plays it down ("he just likes to know where I am").
- **Real-world test.** Ten new accounts in the forms they arrive in (a friend's message, a text thread you are in, your
  own note after an argument), some with a word attached, some without; about half ordinary, at least two a real pattern
  of control. For each, the learner (1) taps the line that is what the person did, (2) judges: the word fits / an
  ordinary moment with a likely reason / can't tell yet / a pattern of control, and (3) picks the next step: ask them,
  watch whether it repeats, name the behavior and set a limit, or get outside help. **Pass:** at least 8 of 10 judgments
  right, no control pattern missed, at most one ordinary moment given a label, and the next step fits the judgment in at
  least 8. Repeated with new accounts three or more weeks later.
- **Out.** Diagnosing anyone; clinical disorders beyond what the popular word needs; therapy and self-help (emotions,
  habits, sleep); reasoning biases in decisions, money or news (Statistical Claims and Scams cover those); conversation
  scripts beyond the next step; facts and history of psychology; a key of named outcomes to memorize; crisis response
  beyond "this is a safety matter: here is who to call".
- **Most useful end result?** Considered and rejected: general cognitive biases (far transfer is weak, learning-science
  P25, and other subjects hold them); self-help psychology (a behavior-change subject of its own). The label moment is
  the one a non-specialist meets weekly, where the common error (over-labeling) and the costly error (missing control)
  both happen.
- If the learner did only this, would they be closer to the end result? This is the end result; the test is the real
  moment's own action.

## Gate 3. How it is practiced, and whether the app can deliver it

- **Method.** Judging new accounts in the real form, many of them, mixed, about half ordinary. Each item runs the same
  four steps: tap what the person did; choose what else would explain it, or what you would need to see (principle 3);
  judge; pick the next step. From the third lesson on, most accounts carry the word someone reached for, because that is
  how the moment arrives.
- **Time.** About 80% of each lesson is judging accounts. Reading is one short card per new idea (what a word requires
  and its look-alike, two to four lines) and one worked account per lesson, done step by step.
- **Feedback.** Immediately after each item, from the app: the line that decides it; what the word requires and
  whether this account has it; the fair one-line description of what was done; why the next step fits. A missed item
  returns later in the same session on a new account, and in the weekly review on later days. An optional typed
  rewrite ("say it from what they did") is compared by the learner to a model line, not graded.
- **Delivery check.**
  - Show an account as it arrives, with its source line ("From a friend:", "Your note after the argument"): plain case
    text does this; a chat-thread look is nice, not needed.
  - Tap one line of an account: exists (`tap` items, `public/app/lessons/ask.js`).
  - One item with several steps, each scored: exists for case items, but those are tied to a branching key of named
    outcomes. **Must be checked before the pilot:** the four steps here are a fixed sequence, not a key; if the case
    engine cannot run a fixed sequence without a key, that item type is built first.
  - Mixed items, returns on new accounts, weekly review: exist (`records.js`, `review.js`), keyed per name; here the
    returned unit is the judgment (fits / ordinary / can't tell / control) per word.
  - Typed own-case line and if-then plan: exist (the transfer and plan inputs in `cards.js`, `review.js`).
- If the learner did only this, would they be closer to the end result? Yes: it is the test's own action, repeated with
  feedback.

## Gate 4. The parts of the end result

| Part | Ability | Check (small version of the gate 2 test) |
|---|---|---|
| A | **What they did.** Tell the act from the reading of it (motive, feeling, label). | 6 new accounts: tap the line that is what was done; 5 right. |
| B | **One moment or a pattern.** What one act can and cannot show; the ordinary reason; "can't tell yet" and what to find out; next steps *ask* and *watch*. | 8 new accounts: ordinary moment / repeated / can't tell yet, plus the next step; 7 right. |
| C | **Words about how someone treats you** (gaslighting, manipulative, passive-aggressive, stonewalling, love-bombing): what each requires against its look-alike; next step *name it and set a limit*. | 8 new accounts with a word attached: fits / doesn't / can't tell; 7 right, at most one false "fits". |
| D | **Words about who someone is** (narcissist, toxic, casual "bipolar", "psycho"): a lasting pattern across situations and people, told by more than one side; otherwise say what they did. | 6 new accounts: earns it / says nothing about who they are; 5 right, and the fair rewrite chosen. |
| E | **A pattern of control**, told apart from ordinary conflict, including when it is played down; DARVO; next step *get outside help*. | 8 new accounts, half control told minimized: every control case flagged, at most one false alarm. |
| F | **The whole check, mixed, and your own.** All words and judgments together, in every form; the next step fits the judgment. | The gate 2 test. |

Order: A before everything (every later step starts from what was done); B before C and D (a word cannot fit before the
account shows a pattern); C before D (behavior words are smaller claims than character words); E after C (control is
the serious end of "how someone treats you"); F last.

Map: A → L1; B → L2; C → L3; D → L4; E → L5; F → L6. The next step is taught in pieces where its judgment is taught
(B: ask, watch; C: set a limit; E: get help) and integrated in L6. No lesson without a part.

Sum check: if A to E pass, the learner can tap the act, judge one moment against pattern, check each word and spot
control; F adds mixing and the real forms. Passing all six is the gate 2 test.

## Gate 5. Each lesson

**L1. What they did** (part A)
- Does: one card, the difference between the act and the reading of it, on one worked account split line by line
  (2 min). Then 12 accounts in mixed forms: tap the line that is the act (8 min); for 4 of them, pick the fair rewrite
  of the label line ("he's so selfish" → "he ate the leftovers I'd saved without asking") (3 min).
- Feedback: the act line; why each other line is a motive, feeling or label.
- End check: part A's check.
- Why: principles 1, 7 (input and response of the real moment), learning-science P15 (commit before the model answer).
- Only this? Yes: separating the act from the word is the first move of every real check.

**L2. One moment or a pattern** (part B)
- Does: one card, what one act can show and the three answers, with a worked account that runs the "what else?" step
  (3 min). Then 14 accounts, half one-offs with an ordinary reason: tap the act, choose what else would explain it,
  judge, pick *ask* or *watch* (12 min).
- Feedback: the situation that explains it, or the repeat that rules it out; why asking beats guessing.
- End check: part B's check.
- Why: principles 1, 3, 4, 5.
- Only this? Yes: most over-labeling is a one-off read as a trait.

**L3. Words about how someone treats you** (part C)
- Does: five words, each introduced in two to four lines with its look-alike, two at a time (gaslighting / remembering
  it differently; manipulative / asking openly; then passive-aggressive / tired and curt; stonewalling / asking for a
  break; love-bombing / keen early on), each followed by 4 accounts on that pair (about 14 min with the cards); then 8
  mixed accounts with the word someone reached for (5 min). Next step adds *name the behavior and set a limit*.
- Feedback: what the word requires, the line that has it or lacks it, the fair description.
- End check: part C's check.
- Why: principles 2, 5, 8 (each pair met, then mixed).
- Only this? Yes: these are the words the real moment carries. Risk: five words is a long lesson; split into two at
  the pilot if first-try accuracy or completion drops.

**L4. Words about who someone is** (part D)
- Does: one card, what a "who someone is" word requires (many situations, over time, more than one side) and how rare
  the disorder behind "narcissist" is; "toxic" named as a word with no fixed meaning (3 min). Then 12 accounts with such
  a word attached: earns it / says nothing about who they are; then choose the fair rewrite (10 min).
- Feedback: what the account would need to contain to earn the word; the rewrite.
- End check: part D's check.
- Why: principles 1, 2, 5.
- Only this? Yes: "narcissist" is the subject's headline moment.

**L5. When it is control** (part E)
- Does: one card, control as several kinds together and repeated, how it is usually played down, and DARVO (3 min);
  one worked account told minimized (2 min). Then 14 accounts, half control told minimized, half ordinary conflicts with
  strong words: tap the controlling acts (two kinds), judge, pick the next step, with *get outside help* now among them
  and a named helpline in the feedback (11 min).
- Feedback: the kinds of control present, or why a hard fight is not control.
- End check: part E's check.
- Why: principles 5, 6, 7.
- Only this? Yes: it is the one miss that matters for safety, and the earlier lessons push toward "ordinary", so this
  lesson sets the other boundary.

**L6. The whole check, and your own** (part F)
- Does: 16 mixed accounts across every word and judgment, in every form, with the full four steps (13 min). Then,
  optional: one line about a real person you have labeled, written as what they did, and an if-then plan ("if I catch
  myself calling someone a ___, then I'll first say what they did") (3 min).
- Feedback: as before, per step.
- End check: the gate 2 test.
- Why: principles 7, 8, 10.
- Only this? Yes: it is the end result's own test with feedback.

**Across lessons.** The weekly review brings each word and each judgment back on new accounts, mixed, until right in
several sessions (principle 9).

## Audit of the existing lessons (2026-10-10)

Read after gates 1 to 5 were written: `public/subjects/psychology/key.js`, `subject.js` and
`docs/learner-view/psychology-u1.md` to `u4.md` (the learner views print every card, case and drill item).

What exists: a branching key of 16 names. Unit One teaches the first question ("What are you looking at?": a choice and
its reasons / something done to someone / a lifelong pattern / just a one-off); Units Two to Four each teach one branch.
Every unit is cards (14 to 23, mostly reading, with short checks), then a 15 to 22 item drill that asks the learner to
run the key's questions and name a third-person story, then spaced returns.

| Unit | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| U1. Before you call it manipulation (the four families) | **changes** | Its best ideas are parts A and B: one week with a cause is not a pattern (cards 10 to 12), and the last drill stage checks a label someone used ("That told me everything I need to know about him"), which is the real moment's own action. But the practice is sorting omniscient stories into four families, two of which serve no part ("A choice and its reasons" is about reasoning biases); nothing asks the learner to separate what was done from the reading of it, "can't tell yet" is never an answer, and there is no next step. |
| U2. Reasoning (rationalizing, sunk cost, confirmation bias, motivated reasoning, following the facts) | **goes** | Serves no part: these are biases in a person's own decisions, outside the end result (gate 2 "Out"; Statistical Claims holds evidence-weighing). Its claim-checking stage is well made, for the wrong subject. |
| U3. Something done to someone (gaslighting, turning the blame around, love-bombing, projection, a normal back-and-forth) | **changes** | Right part (C, and part of E): each word against "a normal back-and-forth" is the right contrast, and the gaslighting-versus-settled-disagreement pairs are good. Wrong practice: the narrator settles the facts the learner never has in life ("the camera shows him taking two bills", "the signed sheet is in the office"), so the check of the word is never made against one side's account; the response is a key route and a name, not "does this word fit, and what now"; manipulative, passive-aggressive and stonewalling, the words people actually use, are missing; serious cases are named and left there. |
| U4. A lifelong pattern (grandiose and vulnerable narcissism, borderline, histrionic, antisocial, just how they are) | **goes** | Its opening message is part D exactly (most people called narcissists are not; check the years), but the unit then trains the opposite: naming five personality disorders from 20-to-40-year omniscient life summaries ("his two grown sons never call him", "her last four partners have all left"). The learner never has that evidence about a real person, so the practiced skill is diagnosing from a dossier, which the subject's own limits forbid. |

**Overall verdict: rebuild from scratch.** The parts the gates need are partly present (one moment against a pattern;
words against their ordinary look-alikes; checking a label), but no unit's practice is the real moment's input and
response, so changing units would mean replacing their cases, items and drills anyway: a patch. What can be carried
into the rebuild as source material, not kept as units: U1 cards 10 to 12 and its claim stage (for L2 and L4), U3's
look-alike pairs (for L3, rewritten as one side's account), U4's opening card (for L4), and the `limits` text in
`subject.js`.

**The three most important problems.**
1. **The practice is not the real moment.** Real life hands you a word and one side's account of one or a few
   incidents; the lessons hand you a neutral narrator who has already checked the camera, the chat and thirty years of
   the person's life, and ask for a name from a 16-name key. Same input? No. Same response? No: the response in life is
   "does this word fit, can I even tell, what do I do", and "can't tell yet: ask" is never a right answer.
2. **The costly miss is never trained.** Stories that show a pattern of control (a partner who tells her that her memory
   is going until she writes conversations down; a husband whose guilt-trips cancel her trips "each time") are filed as
   "Something done to someone" or "Gaslighting" and left there: no notion of control as a pattern, no next step, no
   "this is a safety matter". The subject teaches the learner not to over-label and never sets the other boundary.
3. **Half the subject serves no part, and most time is reading.** U2 teaches decision biases outside the end result, U4
   practices diagnosing personality disorders, and Unit One spends half its families on choices and lifelong patterns.
   Each unit is 14 to 23 cards of reading (10,600 to 21,100 words in the learner view) before the drill, and the drill's
   action is naming, not checking a word against what was done.
