# Ideology: the plan (build-subject, 2026-10-17)

Replaces the design record (`design.md`, kept as history). Built from the owner's own course,
`political-ideologies/Identifying-Political-Ideologies-Practical-Course-v3.md`, which is the spine: the owner learned
best from it and asked for its approach back ("the original political ideology lessons had an approach of teaching
specific questions ... and then when you answer this question, you can point it at the right ideology", chat,
2026-10-17).

**After this you can** read a post, a speech, a platform or a quote and name the ideology it speaks for, or say there is
too little to tell, by answering the deciding questions, **when** someone argues a position or throws a label like
"socialist" or "fascist".

**The test:** twelve new passages of the kinds in the course's Lesson 5 (a manifesto line, a platform, a speech, a post),
named or marked "too little to tell"; the learner finds the deciding questions, the check asks only the family; 10 of 12.

**The way of thinking:** five questions, in this order, and one naming rule.
1. Who owns (or should own) the businesses and farms, the means of production? Private, the state, the workers, or private
   but directed by the state. "Directed" is one definition for every lesson, the course's: the owners keep their firms and the
   state steers what they make toward national goals, from tariffs, industrial policy and screening foreign money at the mild
   end to state orders on what to make and charge, with strikes banned (corporatism), at the strong end. Fascism and national
   populism both do it, so Question 1 alone never separates them; Questions 3 and 4 do (a rebirth story; a leader who replaces
   elections).
2. Who is the story about, the main unit? Class, nation, race or identity group, the individual, tradition or faith, or the
   people against an elite. For race or identity, the direction check: is the group's rank called natural and to be kept, or
   unjust and to be undone? The same noun points to opposite families.
3. What drives history, the central drama? Class conflict, national decline and rebirth, racial struggle, growing individual
   freedom, a divine or traditional order, or a pure people against a corrupt elite.
4. What does it want done with elections and the constitution? Keep and widen them; go beyond them, a party ruling for the
   workers; abolish them for a leader or movement; keep the vote but attack the courts, the press and the civil service (national
   populism); do away with the state altogether (anarchism); or defend and restore them.
5. What is the end state? A classless society, a reborn nation, a racial order, open competition with no end, a restored old
   order, equal outcomes between groups, or open-ended.

**The one naming rule** (taught on a screen in lesson 1, restated wherever it is used, the whole table in lesson 6): a family
is named only when the passage shows that family's telltale answers; otherwise "too little to tell", with what would settle it.
The telltale answers are written once, in `items-shape.js` (`IDEO_SIGNATURE`), and each lesson prints the rows of the families it
teaches: Marxism, class as the unit and class conflict as the engine (ownership may go unsaid, because Marxism is an analysis);
Marxism–Leninism, a party rules for the class and the state owns the businesses (a passage that shows only the party is
too little); democratic socialism, social ownership won through elections; social democracy,
private ownership plus welfare and regulation; anarchism, worker ownership and no state; market socialism, worker- or
socially-owned firms competing in markets; fascism, the nation, its decline and rebirth, and a leader who replaces elections (both, or the course's cluster; a rebirth story
alone or a leader alone is not enough);
Nazism, race as a natural rank as the unit; classical liberalism, the individual, private ownership, a limited state;
conservatism, inherited institutions kept and changed gradually; reaction, an old order brought back; national populism, the
people and the nation against an elite, inside elections; identity-egalitarianism, identity groups as an unjust rank to undo.
Applied strictly: every passage keyed to a family shows that family's signature in its own words (or is keyed "too little to
tell"), and every feedback line that names a family uses these words; one answer is a hint, never a name. Then: classify by what is wanted, never by methods or by a party's name. Methods that travel: one-party rule, a leader cult,
secret police, censorship, repression, violence. Mass mobilization (compulsory youth movements, a mass party with
paramilitaries) separates fascism from an ordinary dictatorship, never from Leninism (Questions 1 and 2 do that); violence
alone is a method, violence praised as purifying the nation is a mark; a leader cult alone is a method, a leader who says he is
the people's will is a mark.

**What it teaches** (every ask of the owner's, from the course and the old record's list):
the five questions and the direction trap; the socialist family (Marxism, Marxism–Leninism, democratic socialism,
social democracy, anarchism and syndicalism, market socialism); fascism (the working definition, the cluster of markers,
fascism against Nazism, against ordinary dictatorship, against reactionary conservatism); the "Nazis were socialists"
claim; conservative against reactionary; classical liberalism against the American "liberal"; what a text about fair
treatment wants the government to do (stay out, pay for a fair start, or change the rules); the look-alikes
(authoritarianism, totalitarianism, populism, communism's two meanings, corporatism, nationalism, statism, technocracy)
and the horseshoe error; the two contemporary formations (national populism, identity-egalitarianism); a mixed drill.

**The lessons**, each opening with the questions it uses, a worked case answered question by question, then passages
answered question by question with feedback that names the question that decided, look-alikes side by side, mixed
earlier material from the second lesson on, and a check on new passages. Each is 12 to 15 minutes by an honest count (reading
at 200 words a minute, 20 seconds an answer in practice, 15 in a check), and the first screen states the minutes. The honest
counts (2026-10-17, after the second reviews, by that formula without the mixed-in review items): lesson 1 about 14.5, 2 about 14.7,
3 about 13.4, 4 about 13.3, 5 about 11.4 (stated twelve), 5b about 11.8, 6 about 14.0. No passage appears twice anywhere in the
subject (practice, check, review items): a scan of six-word runs over every item and every look-alike pair in the lesson text is
part of the pass.
