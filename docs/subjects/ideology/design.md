# ideology: design record (build-subject skill)

Written 2026-10-10. Gates 1 to 5 were worked on 2026-10-10, blind (before the existing lessons were read); the audit of
the existing lessons follows them. The end-result sentence is the approved one. The real moment, the test and the
practice were sharpened after the approval (a policy as well as words; the "too little to tell" answer; tapping the
deciding words; a check on each side); they keep the approved meaning, and the owner should confirm the sharper wording.

Approved by the owner: 2026-10-10, "approve all, keep going" (gates 2, 3 and the pilot, for every subject; the pilot is still to be tried on a phone, and what it shows comes back into this record).

## Gate 1. Kind of learning and evidence

**Kind: judging situations** (does this label fit these words?), resting on declarative concepts (what each label
requires). Not knowing facts for their own sake: a label's definition matters only as the thing the learner checks
against. Not a habit: the subject does not try to make the learner check labels more often, only to check them right
when they do (stated under "out", gate 2).

Evidence: `docs/research/judging-political-labels.md` (L1–L8, written for this gate), `docs/learning-science.md`
(classification principles; its "diagnostic classification" frame is not assumed here, only its sources), and
`docs/research/review-after-lessons.md` (R1–R9).

The principles that drive the design:

1. **Learn each label from provided examples, test it on new ones** (L1: Rawson, Thomas & Jacoby 2015; Zamary & Rawson
   2016; Wissman et al. 2023). A label is a short list of what it requires plus several worked examples; practice is
   judging new examples, never restating a definition.
2. **Practice in the real form: same input, same response** (R8: Pan & Rickard 2018, d = 0.58 matched vs 0.28; P25).
   The input is a label pinned on words or a policy; the response is a verdict and the deciding words.
3. **Refute the meaning the learner brings** (P29: Schroeder & Kucera 2022, g = 0.41; L2: Gallup 2018, socialism read
   as equality or free services). Each label family opens with its most common wrong reading, marked wrong, then the line.
4. **Contrast look-alikes in pairs** (P12: Alfieri et al. 2013, d ≈ 0.50): a welfare program beside public ownership,
   a nationalist beside a fascist text, with the learner told what to compare.
5. **Discrimination, not suspicion** (P26: Burke et al. 2022): earned labels, unearned ones and "too little to tell"
   appear in the same practice, so the learner does not learn that every label is wrong.
6. **Party cues override content; give a "too little" answer and score accuracy** (L3: Cohen 2003; L4: Bullock et al.
   2015). The words are judged apart from who said them and the commentary around them; cues return in later items;
   the learner sees their accuracy on labels thrown from each side (P23 requires 3).
7. **Few requirements per label, anchor examples, quote the evidence** (L5: Mikhaylov, Laver & Benoit 2012; Benoit et
   al. 2016; Hawkins' anchor speeches). Simple schemes are judged reliably; items experts would argue over are not
   scored as right or wrong.
8. **Each label is checked on its own question** (L6: Mudde 2004, populism is thin and rides on left or right; L7:
   fascism needs more than nationalism or authoritarianism).
9. **Feedback after every item, decisive words first** (R7, P19, P20), then **mixed practice of confusable labels**
   once each is introduced, measured because the evidence for verbal categories is weak (P22, R9).
10. **Spaced relearning in the same form** (R2, R4, R8): the weekly review is new labelled items, not questions about
    the lessons.

## Gate 2. End result

- **After this subject you can** check for yourself whether a political label ("socialist", "fascist", "populist",
  "liberal" and the like) is earned by the words or policy it is pinned on, **when** you meet it in a post, a headline,
  a speech clip or an argument. (The approved sentence, unchanged in meaning.)
- **Real moment:** a label pinned on quoted words or a policy: "This is socialism" over a health-care plan; "fascist"
  under a clip of a speech; "far-left" in a headline about a mayor's housing plan; a relative calling a candidate a
  communist. It arrives on a phone, mid-scroll, with the speaker's side usually visible.
- **Real-world test:** ten new items not met in practice, each a label pinned on quoted words or a policy, in the form
  of a post or headline; five labels thrown from the left, five from the right; some earned, some not, some too thin
  to tell. For each the learner answers **earned / not earned / too little to tell**; if not earned, the name the words
  do earn (or none of the subject's names); and taps the words that decide. **Pass: 8 of 10 verdicts right with the
  deciding words found, and at least 4 of 5 on each side.**
- **The labels covered:** free-market (capitalist), social democrat (welfare state), socialist, communist (also
  "Marxist"); liberal in its US sense and in its classical sense, conservative, libertarian, progressive; populist;
  nationalist and nativist; authoritarian; fascist; and "far-left" / "far-right" as umbrellas built from the above.
- **Out:** verdicts on any living person or party (the subject judges words, not people); whether an ideology is good;
  history of ideas and thinkers; whether a quote is genuine or in context (that is source checking, another skill);
  what a policy would do; labels with no agreed meaning ("woke", "globalist"), which the subject names as such and does
  not teach; traditions outside Europe and North America; making the learner check more often (a habit, not taught).

Is this the most useful end result? The other candidates were "know what each ideology believes" (knowing, but the
learner never meets an ideology in the abstract; they meet a label on something) and "place any text on the map"
(judging, but with no label in front of the learner it answers a question nobody is asking them). The label check is
the moment the learner actually meets, and it needs the other two only as far as the check uses them. Kept.

## Gate 3. Practice, and whether the app can deliver it

**Method.** The learner judges labelled items in the real form: a label pinned on words or a policy, styled as a post,
headline or quoted remark; they answer earned / not earned (with the name the words earn) / too little to tell, and tap
the sentence that decides (principles 1, 2, 7). Each label family opens with one card of what each label requires
(two to four requirements, plain words), one card that refutes the common wrong reading (3), one worked item, and one
contrast pair (4). Then judging. First items show the label's requirements beside the passage; later items hide them
(fading support, P17). Earned, unearned and too-thin items are mixed throughout (5), labels thrown from both sides in
equal number, and from lesson 4 on some items carry party cues (6).

**Time.** Reading is at most a quarter of any lesson (three to four minutes of fifteen); judging is the rest. A lesson
with more reading than judging is rejected at gate 5.

**Feedback.** After every item, at once: the verdict; the deciding words highlighted in the passage; each of the
label's requirements marked present or missing; the name the words earn; and, after a miss, one line on the learner's
own choice (9). At the end of each lesson and in the review, the learner's accuracy on labels thrown from the left and
from the right, side by side (6).

**Delivery check** (from a look at the engine's item types, `public/app/lessons/ask.js`, not at the lessons):
- Show a short passage and take a choice among names: exists (`case`).
- Tap the deciding sentence in a passage: exists (`tap`, a case's `segments`).
- A label pinned on words with a three-way verdict (earned / not earned + which name / too little to tell) scored with
  the tapped words: **not as such**. The `claim` item asks what a name needs, which is near but not the same response.
  Must be built (or `claim` reworked) before any lesson.
- Show a label's requirements beside the passage, then hide them: to be confirmed; build if absent.
- Record each item's side (label thrown from the left or right) and show accuracy per side: **not present**; build first.
- Review in the same form: the weekly review (`review.js`) draws items; it must draw these items in this form.

## Gate 4. The parts of the end result

Ordered so each builds on the ones before. Every check is a small version of the gate 2 test: new labelled items, verdict
plus deciding words, without the requirements shown.

| # | Part | Its check |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Find what the words ask for**: the demand, apart from tone, insult, the commentary around the quote and who said it; or that there is no demand (too little) | 5 new labelled items: tap the sentence that states what should be done, or say there is none; 4 of 5 |
| 2 | **Economy labels**: free-market, social democrat, socialist, communist. The line: welfare and taxes inside private ownership vs public or worker ownership of the main businesses | 5 new labelled items from this family, both sides; 4 of 5 verdicts with the deciding words |
| 3 | **The US left–right words**: liberal (US and classical), conservative, libertarian, progressive | Same, this family; 4 of 5 |
| 4 | **Us-and-them labels**: populist (pure people against a corrupt elite, left or right), nationalist and nativist (the nation first; outsiders as a threat) | Same, this family, with party cues on some; 4 of 5 |
| 5 | **Power labels**: authoritarian (power gathered in one hand, courts, press, elections or opponents to be curbed), fascist (nation above all, rebirth from decline, democracy to be ended, violence against named enemies), and the far-left / far-right umbrellas | Same, this family, mixed with part 4 look-alikes; 4 of 5 |
| 6 | **Labels in the wild**: every family mixed, both sides, party cues, earned, unearned and too-thin items | The gate 2 test |

Map both ways: lessons 1 to 6 serve parts 1 to 6, one each. Parts 2 to 5 together cover every label in gate 2's list;
part 1 is the first step of every check; part 6 is the test itself. If each part passes, the gate 2 test passes, except
for the mixing and the party cues, which only part 6 trains, and that is why it exists. Nothing else is taught.

## Gate 5. Each lesson

Every lesson: about fifteen minutes; three to four minutes of reading at most; the rest is judging items in the real form
with feedback after each; the end check without the requirements shown. "If the learner did only this, would they be
closer to the end result?" is answered under each.

1. **What the words ask for** (part 1). Do: one card on the job (find the demand; the label, the tone and the speaker are
   not evidence), 1 min; a worked item, 1 min; then 12 items, each a labelled post: tap the sentence that states what
   should be done, or say "no demand here"; distractors are insults, slogans, the commenter's own words (10 min).
   Feedback: the demand sentence highlighted, why the tempting sentence is commentary or tone. Check: part 1. Why: 2, 6, 7.
   Closer to the end result? Yes: every later verdict starts here, and "too little to tell" is learned here.
2. **Socialist or not?** (part 2) — the pilot. Do: the wrong idea first ("socialism means the government pays for
   things"), marked wrong, then the line (1 min); a card per label with its requirements, 2 min; a contrast pair
   (a public health plan inside private ownership vs taking over the hospitals), 1 min; then 14 labelled items:
   "socialist", "communist", "free-market" pinned on plans and quotes, earned, unearned and too thin, both sides
   (11 min). Feedback: verdict, deciding words, requirements present or missing, the name earned. Check: part 2. Why:
   1–5, 7, 9. Closer? Yes: "socialist" is the label the learner meets most, and this is the real check on it.
3. **Liberal, conservative, libertarian** (part 3). Do: the wrong idea ("liberal means the same everywhere"; "libertarian
   is just very conservative"), 1 min; requirement cards, 2 min; a contrast pair (a US liberal and a classical liberal
   text), 1 min; 14 labelled items (11 min). Feedback and check as above. Why: 1–5, 9. Closer? Yes: these are the words
   on most posts and self-descriptions.
4. **Populist, nationalist, nativist** (part 4). Do: the wrong ideas ("populist means popular or plain-spoken";
   "nationalist means patriotic"), 1 min; requirement cards, 2 min; a contrast pair (a left populist and a right
   populist speech, the same people-against-elite structure), 1 min; 14 items, some with a party cue
   ("said a Republican governor"), both sides (11 min). Feedback adds: populism never decides left or right. Check:
   part 4. Why: 1–6, 8. Closer? Yes, and part 5 needs it.
5. **Authoritarian, fascist, far-left, far-right** (part 5). Do: the wrong idea ("fascist means a strong state or anyone
   cruel"), 1 min; requirement cards and the line drawn (fascist needs all four; nationalism or authoritarianism alone
   does not earn it), with the note that historians draw it differently, 2 min; a contrast pair (a nationalist speech
   and a fascist one), 1 min; 14 items mixed with part 4 look-alikes (11 min). Check: part 5. Why: 1–5, 7, 8.
   Closer? Yes: "fascist" is the label most often thrown and least often earned.
6. **Labels in the wild** (part 6). Do: one card (this is the real form, mixed; it will feel harder; why), 1 min; then
   20 items from all families, both sides, party cues, earned and unearned and too thin, in the post and headline form
   (14 min); then the learner's own per-side results. Feedback as above. Check: the gate 2 test. Why: 2, 5, 6, 9.
   Closer? It is the end result.

Review: the weekly review draws new labelled items from the lessons done, mixed, in the same form (principle 10; R8).

## Audit of the existing lessons (2026-10-10)

Read after gates 1 to 5 were written: `public/subjects/ideology/` (subject, key, units) and
`docs/learner-view/ideology-u1.md` to `u5.md`. The five units hold 102 cards (29 of them short checks), 89 drill items
and 22 return stories. Every unit has the same shape: the key's first question "Whose side is it on?" with five answers,
then a branch of finer names per unit, each name taught by a long "meet" card with a "How to spot it" list, a check, a
look-alike card, a worked story, a drill that walks the key's questions and asks for a name, and returns in that form.

| Unit | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| u1 Whose side is it on (5 answers) | **goes** | Its part is the key's first question, a sort ("workers", "the nation", "the old ways", "everyone's rights", "no side") that no one throws at a text; it serves no gate 4 part. Its tap items, its notice-type "no side" stories and its claim items ("communism on wheels" over a bus-lane plan) are the right form and are kept as material for lessons 1 and 6. |
| u2 Workers against owners (7 names) | **changes** → lesson 2 | The part is right and its opener names the right wrong idea ("a plan to tax big companies is communism"); the welfare-versus-ownership line matches L2. But the practice names unlabelled stories by walking the key, half its seven names are rarely thrown (market socialism, anarchism, "Marxism" as an explanation, "class politics on its own"), and "free-market" is missing. 28 cards before the drill. |
| u3 The nation (5 names) | **changes** → lessons 4 and 5 | The part is right (nationalist, populist, fascist). The line for fascism is any text that speaks for one people and ends elections or critics, which awards "fascist" to every nationalist dictatorship (L7: the scholarly minimum separates fascism from authoritarian nationalism); "authoritarian" has no name of its own. Populism sits only under "the nation", and the u1 tie-break ("Workers against owners" wins) means a left-populist text can never be called populist (against L6). |
| u4 The old ways (2 names) | **changes** → lesson 3 | "Conservative" is a label the learner meets; the keep-versus-restore line is sound. Practice is the unlabelled key walk; the unit's two extra cases about mistaken names are the closest thing to the real form and are short. |
| u5 Everyone's rights (3 names) | **changes** → lesson 3 | The classical-versus-US "liberal" line is the one gate 2 wants. "Libertarian" is folded into classical liberalism with no social side; "group equality" is given "identity politics" and "social justice" as other names, contested labels the design keeps out. Practice is the unlabelled key walk. |

**Overall verdict: rebuild from scratch.** No unit is what gate 5 would have designed; four have the right territory and
the wrong practice, one serves no part. Per the skill this is a rebuild through the gates, not a patch: lessons are
written from the gate 5 blocks. The existing passages, tap items and feedback lines are raw material to be checked and
reused where they fit a labelled item, not lessons to keep.

**The three most important problems**
1. **The real moment is almost never practiced.** The real moment is a label pinned on words; the practice is an
   unlabelled invented story to be named by walking a five-answer key. One of 89 drill items (plus one worked claim)
   shows a thrown label, none shows an earned one, and no item asks "earned, not earned, or too little to tell". Same input, same response:
   no (principle 2).
2. **The names follow the key's tree, not the labels people meet, and the two hottest lines are drawn wrong.** Twenty
   names, many rarely thrown, while "authoritarian", "free-market", "libertarian" (its own), "progressive" and
   "far-left / far-right" are missing; "fascist" is awarded to any nationalist rule that ends elections, and a
   left-populist text cannot be called populist. A learner who passes would mislabel the cases that matter most.
3. **Mostly reading and key machinery, and nothing against the side the learner is on.** 73 teaching cards, many of
   several paragraphs, plus drill items that ask which key question separates two names; no party cues, no labels
   thrown from both sides by design, no per-side results, so the main distortion in the real moment (L3: Cohen 2003)
   is never trained.
