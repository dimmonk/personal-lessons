# Learner review, round 2, Strategy l1 to l14

Reviewer: cold learner (project manager, two small businesses, no business training). Read only the learner views. I answered each question before reading the marked answer.

## L1 Goal, wish, or strategy?
Way of deciding in my words: find the line saying what is in the way and why; if none, it is a goal/wish (or a to-do list if steps are listed); if there is one but steps do not serve one way past it, to-do list; challenge + one approach + steps serving it = strategy.
Check score: 6 of 6 by reasoning.
Verdict: PASS with 4 defects.
1. Item 8 (Sam, food truck) and Step 2 disagree. "A third of our weekday line gives up and leaves" is a number with no cause, which Step 2 says is not a challenge, yet it is marked as the challenge; the feedback adds a cause ("cannot serve the people already waiting") the text never says. Fix: add the cause to the line ("...because the wait is twenty minutes") or change the feedback.
2. Items 1, 2, 7, 11, 15 (the strategy ones): the approach line starts "So", "Our answer", "From now on" and the challenge is the line before it. Answerable by wording. Fix: vary one strategy item so the approach is not signposted.
3. Step 1 "One limit" paragraph (running the business better is not a strategy) has no example and is deferred to "comes next"; I could not apply it yet. Fix: one two-line example or remove until taught.
4. Item 12 (Lena): "Most of our work comes from people who saw a kitchen we built for a friend" can be read as a dependence problem; marked as plain background. Slightly arguable. Fix: make the line clearly neutral.

## L2 Better, or different?
Way of deciding: ask whether doing the same things the rivals do, only better, would fix it. Rivals do the same work better = run it better. Business already as good, gains copied, or rival cost out of reach = choose a different position.
Check score: 6 of 6 by reasoning.
Verdict: PASS with 3 defects.
1. Length/wording pattern on the "tap the line" question. In the "different position" items (3, 4, 6, 8, check 13, 14, 15) the right line is the longest of the options, and it often contains "already as ... as" or "cannot". Fix: shorten those lines or lengthen decoys with equal detail.
2. The second question ("What does this business need?") is given away once the line is tapped; the two questions test one decision twice. Fix: ask only the second, with the line tap as the help.
3. Step 1 never says what "position" means in one plain sentence ("who you serve and what you do differently"); I inferred it from the cleaning-firm example. Fix: add the definition.

## L3 Value created, and who keeps it
Way of deciding: four numbers on a line (least staff/suppliers would take, cost, price, most customer would pay); value = top minus bottom; split into three. A move grows value only if customers would pay more by more than it adds to the work, or suppliers would take less; a change in price or pay alone only moves value.
Check score: 7 of 7 (all arithmetic checked by hand: correct; all move questions right).
Verdict: PASS with 4 defects.
1. Eight near-identical three-part arithmetic sets (items 1, 2, 3, 9, 10, check 16, 17, 18) against seven judgment questions. The arithmetic is a formula drill, not a decision. Fix: cut to four sets; use the space for more "grow, move or shrink" cases.
2. "Value stick" is used in every picture and in the Step 4 prose ("the stick gets longer") but never defined in words as a bar. Fix: one sentence in Step 1 before the first picture.
3. Items 6 and 21 (staff stay or would take less at the same pay) are the hardest and the only two like them; the feedback for a wrong "only moves" answer is the same generic line. Fix: one more worked case in Step 4, or specific feedback ("pay did not change, the least they would take did").
4. Wrong-number feedback exists for one or two typed values only; any other wrong number gets no explanation of what it was. Fix: add a generic "start from the four numbers" fallback.

## L4 Who squeezes the profit
Way of deciding: read the report for the one outside party taking the most money: a few big buyers or a middleman, a supplier you cannot replace, easy entry bringing price cutters, a substitute, or alike rivals fighting on price. Compare sizes when two press. Fast growth in an easy-entry trade goes to newcomers.
Check score: 6 of 6 by reasoning.
Verdict: PASS with 3 defects.
1. Every decoy sentence announces its own weakness ("neither cuts prices", "many wholesalers", "no new firm in years"). The task becomes finding the one sentence without a "calm" signal. Real reports do not do this. Fix: in at least four items make the decoy sentences neutral facts the learner must judge.
2. Two questions per item (tap the line, then pick the force) test one decision twice; the force follows directly from the line. Fix: ask one, or make the second ask about the move it points to.
3. Item 3 (Paccar): rivals "fight hard" is stated in the same report, so rivals and buyers are both pressing; only the stem wording ("by choosing its customers") decides. Defensible but fiddly. Fix: say in the stem "Which force did Paccar's choice of customer step around?" and keep as is, or drop the rivals sentence.

## L5 Find the core
Way of deciding: for each line subtract only the costs that would stop if it stopped; judge by what is left, not sales. The core is the line customers come for (a rival cannot match it) that earns the most; if the top earner is something every rival sells alike, the core is the distinctive one. A losing line: try fixing price or cost; if that cannot work, keep it only if it feeds the core, else drop it.
Check score: 4 of 4 by reasoning; I recomputed all twelve shares by hand and they are right.
Verdict: PASS with 5 defects.
1. Item 7 (Hal): the right answer is a share of 187.5%. A beginner will distrust a share over 100%. The one-line explanation is in Step 1 but not in the item feedback. Fix: add to the item feedback "over 100% because two losing lines take $14,000 back", or change the numbers so no share passes 100%.
2. The distinctive line is always named in the first note in the same words ("no other X in town does", "drivers come from across town"). The learner can pick the core from the notes alone and skip the arithmetic. Fix: in half the items make the distinctiveness show only in the numbers or in a customer remark that needs reading.
3. Keep-or-drop notes follow a fixed order (price note second, tie-to-core note third) and the tie note is always blunt ("come only for the wash"). Answerable by position. Fix: shuffle note order and sometimes give a tie that is partial.
4. Step 5 (own business) asks for "direct cost" per line with no help on splitting staff hours or shared materials, which a small owner will not have in the books. Fix: add a worked line on estimating staff hours per line.
5. Twelve items repeat the same three-question block (core, share, keep or drop); only the Step 1 rules for traps (top earner copied, tie to core) vary. Fix: fewer repeats, add a case where two lines are both distinctive.

## L6 What you say no to
Way of deciding: ask whether the new work needs the same people, hours, room or promise as the old, in opposite ways. No pull: it fits beside (same customers, idle hours). Pull and one side dropped: trade-off. Pull and both kept (even with trimming): straddle.
Check score: 6 of 6 by reasoning.
Verdict: PASS with 4 defects.
1. Wording gives the answer. Every trade-off note says "turn down", "no longer", "drops", "from now on we only"; every straddle note says "same people/chairs/tables/hours"; every no-pull note says "same customers". Fix: write some trade-off notes without the dropping verb (the reader must see what was lost) and some straddles that mention no shared resource until the learner is asked what it needs.
2. Item 8 (Sam, diner): the pull is asserted ("same cook on the same grill at dinner"), but all-day breakfast and dinner steaks do not obviously collide, so "no pull" is defensible. Fix: state that the grill is full of breakfast orders at dinner time, or choose a clearer case.
3. Check 17 (Tom): he cuts odd parts, drops one piece of the builder side, yet it is marked a straddle. The "trimming is still a straddle" sentence is in Step 1, but trimming that visibly drops a line reads as a trade-off. Fix: make the cut tiny (one aisle) so the learner can see it is not a choice.
4. Step 1 ends "whether it is wise is judged later" and a trade-off is described only as "serves the side it kept better". I wanted to know if giving up $40,000 of walk-ins (item 15) was right. Fix: one sentence saying a trade-off can still be a mistake, and where that is judged.

## L7 Do the pieces fit?
Way of deciding: state the position (who, and what they get that others do not). Hold each activity against it: does it serve those customers and help the other activities? Costly or odd can still fit; the trade's usual way does not if it pulls against the position. Replace a misfit with the activity that serves the position and also helps the others.
Check score: 5 of 5 by reasoning.
Verdict: PASS with 5 defects.
1. The check rule "None thought running the business better would fix what needs a different position" is lesson 2's rule. It does not fit this lesson, so the learner cannot tell what to avoid. Fix: replace with a l7 rule, e.g. "None kept an activity that serves a different customer".
2. The "Every activity fits" button, when wrongly chosen, shows "The text does give one. It is marked above." That wording belongs to l1's "No line" button and makes no sense here (it refers to a challenge). Fix: "One activity does not fit; it is marked above."
3. The position is printed in one line above each note and the misfit nearly always contradicts a word in it (quiet/loud, early/10 a.m., cheapest/attendant). Fix: sometimes state the position more broadly so the learner must infer what it rules out.
4. Second question has a fixed template: one option is "another kind of customer", one is "the usual way done harder", one is the answer. Elimination works without reasoning. Fix: vary the distractors; include one that serves the position but fights another activity.
5. Item 5 (Leo): a cafe where racers wait could reasonably help same-day service; the feedback says it "does nothing", which is arguable. Fix: choose a distractor that clearly serves no one in the position.

## L8 Will it last?
Way of deciding: two questions. What does the difference give (customers pay more, or it costs less)? If nothing, it is worthless. If something, what stops a rival getting it: only we have it and cannot be got, many fitting pieces a rival would need all at once (unless it already runs that way), customers would lose by leaving, or nothing. It lasts only with a benefit and a barrier.
Check score: 6 of 6 by reasoning.
Verdict: PASS with 4 defects.
1. The notes echo the option wording. "Sold to any bakery/groomer/plumber" means Nothing; "all at once" means fit; "lose a season" or "years of records" means leaving cost; "no new docks" means only ours. Nine of eighteen barrier answers are "Nothing". Fix: write several notes in plain description without the echo, and add a "Nothing" decoy that sounds like a barrier (a patent about to expire, a long head start).
2. Step 1 says a lower cost can last through know-how built over years or being much bigger than any rival, but no question option covers size or know-how. A learner with a size-based cost advantage has no answer. Fix: add that option to the barrier list or drop the sentence and cover it in a later lesson.
3. Check 18 (Hal): "the piano stores send their buyers only to us" can also read as "a deal no one else can sign" (only ours), not just trust. Two options are defensible. Fix: remove the exclusivity wording from the stores line.
4. Check 15 (Rosa): the same barrier that works against shops fails against one particular rival, so "Nothing" is right only because the rival already runs the same way. The Step 1 sentence covering it is easy to miss. Fix: put it in the worked example or Step 2.

## L9 Growth that keeps the position
Way of deciding: name the core (what customers come for and what earns most); ask whether the offer serves the same kind of customer with the same activities. Yes: deepens. Different customer or different activities (even if the same people do them): blurs. Ignore the money.
Check score: 6 of 6 by reasoning.
Verdict: PASS with 4 defects.
1. Every question has two options, so guessing gets half right, and the check passes at 5 of 6. Fix: add a third verdict (for example "serves the core but needs something the core lacks, so check first") or ask the learner to name the deciding line before answering.
2. Money pattern. The blurring offers carry the big figures ($900,000, $120,000, $150,000, triple the members, 300 houses, a 20,000-visitor mall); the deepening offers carry small ones ($40,000, a $60,000 van). Step 1 says money does not decide, yet money predicts the answer. Fix: put a big figure on at least three deepening offers and a small one on three blurring offers.
3. The lesson never says what to do after "deepens" or "blurs" (take it, decline it, change it). Rosa's $60,000 van "deepens", but should she buy it? I stopped unsure. Fix: one sentence in Step 1: "Deepens means worth pricing and testing; blurs means decline or reshape it."
4. Item 3 (Leo, wheel dealer): becoming a dealer for a maker is arguably a new activity (stocking, selling), though the note says the mechanics fit them. Fix: say the wheels are ordered per customer, not stocked, or choose another case.

## L10 Where to play and how to win
Way of deciding: a real plan names which customers (and where) and why they would pick you over the rival they use now, and the two fit. Otherwise name the trap: do it all, head-on, many fronts, something for everyone, a dream, copycat, or a misfit (customers chosen but the way to win is not what they value).
Check score: 6 of 6 by reasoning.
Verdict: PASS with 4 defects.
1. The check rule "None tried to serve everyone at once, or kept two ways that pull against each other" is lesson 6's rule. The second half has nothing to do with this lesson. Fix: write a l10 rule, e.g. "None took a plan that names no customer for a real choice".
2. Item 13 (Joe, "the future of the lake and everyone who loves it") is both a dream (no reason to pick him) and something for everyone ("everyone who loves it"). Two options defensible. Fix: drop "everyone who loves it".
3. Step 1 says "six traps", but the questions offer seven (the misfit has no number or name in Step 1). Fix: name the misfit in the list or say "six traps and one mismatch".
4. The wrong-answer feedback for the seven-way trap question is the same long paragraph repeated six times per item; it names the right trap but does not say why this learner's pick was tempting. Fix: for the two nearest traps (dream vs something for everyone, do it all vs many fronts) give a distinguishing line.

## L11 What would have to be true
Way of deciding: state the decision, list the moves, write what must be true for each; weigh what backs each (done, paid, signed, counted = evidence; opinions and "I'm sure" = talk; a contrary fact = evidence against); test first the least likely (a fact against, then talk, then evidence), cheaply; if it fails drop or reshape the move; if it holds test the next least likely; choose the move whose conditions held, and only then write the full plan.
Check score: 4 of 4 by reasoning.
Verdict: PASS with 3 defects.
1. Check 14 (Jill) contradicts Step 1 and Item 10. After one condition of the night room held, the answer is "choose the night room". Step 1 and Item 10 say that when a test holds you test the next least likely condition of the same move, and only then choose. The night room's other conditions were never tested. Fix: say in the item that all its other conditions are backed by evidence, or change the answer to "test the next condition of the night room".
2. Keyword pattern: the talk lines say "sure", "think so", "expects so", "like the idea"; the fact-against lines carry "though", "less than expected", "closed last spring". The right answer is the one with those words. Fix: sometimes write the contrary fact without signal words, or the talk as a flat claim.
3. Item 7 (Omar): the drive "is 40 minutes at noon, jammed from 4 p.m." and the court closes at 5 p.m.; whether that is a fact against needs arithmetic the note does not finish. A beginner might call "work all year" (talk only) the answer. Fix: state "leaving at 4:15 it arrives after 5".

## L12 How did others like it do?
Way of deciding: (1) start the forecast from the share of many ventures alike in what makes them work or fail, moving only for a real difference (a fact others lacked, like paid deposits), never for being sure or knowing the trade; (2) a rival answers if you take its customers and it can follow without changing how it runs, else it lets it go; (3) "it worked for them": find the reason and check it is true here.
Check score: 7 of 7 by reasoning; I recomputed every share (all correct).
Verdict: PASS with 4 defects.
1. Three separate skills in one lesson (base rate, rival's answer, borrowed example) with only two to four questions each beyond the arithmetic. The rival and reason questions are binary. Fix: split the lesson, or add three more questions each with a third option.
2. Five identical sets (items 5, 6, 7, 14, 15): estimate the share, work it out, pick where the forecast starts. The forecast question is answered by the option that contains the figure you just computed; the two decoys are always "near 100%". Fix: vary the decoys (start at 50%, start from the best year only) and cut to three sets.
3. "Will it work here for the same reason?" notes always hand over the reason in a sentence beginning "It worked because". The skill taught is to find the reason; the questions only ask whether a stated fact holds. Fix: in half the items state only what happened and let the learner pick the reason from options.
4. Item 9 (Leo's rival, 20 years of no price cuts, marked "will answer") rests on "a shelf is all it needs", which is the note's own claim. Step 1 says past behaviour is only a clue, which helps, but a learner can still defend "let it go". Fix: add that the rival sells kids' bikes to the same families on the same shelves.

## L13 A bet you can afford
Way of deciding: first ask whether anyone can already see who would buy (people already buy it and many like it have a record: start from the record; never offered to these customers: try it small with what you have and partners). Then size the stake to what you could lose and still keep business and household going: whole project fits and record is sound, go in full; too big, cut it down (rent, part, partner); no version fits, not now.
Check score: 6 of 6 by reasoning.
Verdict: PASS with 4 defects.
1. Every note hands over "what we could lose" as a number. Step 1 defines it but never says how an owner works it out. A real owner cannot do this lesson without that step. Fix: add a two-line method (cash buffer, household bills for a stretch, the business's other commitments) and one question that makes the learner find the number.
2. The sizing answer is always the option whose dollar figure sits under the "could lose" figure, with the big figure as the decoy and "forecast"/"poll friends" as the other decoy. Elimination by arithmetic works with no reasoning about records. Fix: include cases where the smallest listed option still exceeds the limit, and where a mid-size option is wrong for another reason.
3. Item 5 and check 13 (record sound, whole stake affordable): the marked answer says a trial first "would only delay". A careful owner could defend a cheap trial as sensible. Fix: add a stated cost to the trial (a lost season, a lease clock) or soften to "adds little".
4. Item 10 (Ben, dogs in a gym) mixes both kinds in one idea; it is the best item but has no "which way" question, and it comes late in the practice. Fix: teach the mixed case in Step 1 and move this item to the worked example.

## L14 Run the six questions
Way of deciding: take any decision through six questions in order: (1) run better or different position; (2) what is in the way and why; (3) where the money comes from and who squeezes it; (4) what is different, what it gives, what stops a copy; (5) the choice (where to play, how to win, what it will not do, growth that deepens the core); (6) what must be true, test the weakest, record or small trial, stake within what can be lost, expect the rival's answer.
Check score: 10 of 10 by reasoning.
Verdict: PASS with 4 defects.
1. Check 13 (Ray, grooming van) needs a working day the note never gives. Six dogs at 70 minutes plus 20 minutes of driving is about nine hours only counts as "a fact against" if the day is shorter. Fix: state "the groomers work an eight-hour day".
2. Check 7 (Rosa, same-day shirts): the right price is "$3 more" because it splits the value, but lesson 3 never taught choosing a price, and the trial shows 60 customers actually paid $4. A learner can argue $4 and it is not offered. Fix: either teach "a price between the two splits the value" in l3, or ask only "does it grow the value".
3. Each check question names its own tool ("What kind of move is this?", "Which condition should Ray test first?", "Which customers are the core?"), so the learner never decides which of the six questions applies. The lesson is called "run the six questions" but the check is ten separate drills with the question handed over. Fix: make at least three check items open ("what should the owner do?") with the six questions available as the learner's own checklist.
4. Item 2 (Omar) asks what stops a copy of the law-firm business and marks "only we have it" because of the court contract, but the contract is with the filing office, not the law firms who are the money. A learner can argue it is trust or fit. Fix: say the contract is what brings the firms their same-day service.
