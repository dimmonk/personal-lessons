# The lesson pattern

Fieldcraft's tagline is "Pragmatic knowledge". A lesson is practical: a reader finishes it able to recognise the thing in real life and knowing what to do about it. It is not a research report and not a list of labels.

## What a unit contains

1. **An opening card: what this unit is for.** Two to four sentences: what the reader will be able to do, where it turns up in ordinary life, and which question of the subject's key this unit answers, quoted in the key's exact words.

2. **One card per idea.** The heading is the idea's name, the same name the key, the drill options and the feedback use. Plain name first; the technical term once, in brackets, if people will hear it. The body always has these parts, in this order, each as long as it needs to be (there is no length limit; never squeeze an idea into a phrase):
   - **What it is.** In ordinary words. No jargon; any unavoidable term is explained in the same sentence.
   - **Example.** One concrete, everyday case.
   - **Sounds like** (or **Looks like**). How it shows up: the sentence you would hear, the detail you would see.
   - **Catch it.** The one question to ask yourself. Then say which answer of the key this is, in the key's exact words.
   - **What to do.** The practical move once you have spotted it.
   - **Don't confuse it with.** The nearest look-alike and the one difference.

3. **A card that puts them side by side.** The key's question for this unit and each of its answers, in the key's exact words, with the name each leads to. A short table or list.

4. **A worked example.** One new case walked through the key's questions in order, saying at each step which words in the case decide the answer, ending in the name.

5. **Practice.** New cases (never a card's own example again). The answer options are the exact names from the cards. Every explanation says which question decides it and which detail in the case gives it away.

## Rules

- **One name per idea, everywhere.** Card headings, the key's answers, drill options, feedback and specimen explanations use the same words. If real life uses another word for it, say so once on its card.
- **Teach it before you ask it.** Every question and answer option the reader meets in a drill or in the full determination has been shown and explained on a card first, in the same words.
- **Plain language.** Write for a smart adult who has never studied the subject. Everyday scene first, technical term second. No codes (Q1, D2, R1) in anything the reader sees.
- **Practical.** Every idea ends in what to do about it.

## The pattern, written out (Psychology: when reasoning protects you instead of informing you)

People bend their reasoning to protect what they did, spent, or believe. You do it too. If you can name which bend is happening, you know what to do about it.

**1. The excuse after the fact** (psychologists call it reducing cognitive dissonance)
- **What it is:** you did something that doesn't fit how you see yourself, so you add a reason why it's fine.
- **Sounds like:** "It was only once, it doesn't count."
- **Catch it:** did the reason appear only after the act?
- **What to do:** change the behaviour, or admit your standard is lower than you claim.

**2. Throwing good money after bad** (sunk cost)
- **What it is:** carrying on because of what you've already put in, not what you'll get out.
- **Sounds like:** "I've already spent three years on this."
- **Catch it:** "If I were starting fresh today, would I choose this?"
- **What to do:** decide on future cost and benefit only.

**3. The double standard for evidence** (confirmation bias)
- **What it is:** evidence that agrees with you gets a pass; evidence against you gets picked apart.
- **Sounds like:** "Who funded that study?" (never asked about the studies you like)
- **Catch it:** "Did I ask the same questions of the evidence I liked?"
- **What to do:** give both sides the same test.

**4. Deciding first, researching second** (motivated reasoning)
- **What it is:** the answer was chosen before looking, and the "research" only collects support.
- **Sounds like:** "I looked into it and I was right," after reading four reviews titled "Why it's worth it".
- **Catch it:** "Could anything I found have changed my mind?"
- **What to do:** write down what would change your mind before you look.

**5. Following the facts** (the healthy one)
- **What it is:** every fact gets the same test, and you end up where the facts point, even if that means changing your mind.
- **Sounds like:** "I checked, and I was wrong."
- **Don't confuse it with** weakness or flip-flopping.

**Try it**
1. "We've come too far to cancel the renovation now."
2. "One donut won't hurt, I had a rough day," from someone proud of her diet.
3. She shares every article that supports her diet and calls the one critical study "junk" without reading it.
4. He wanted the phone before it launched, watched four glowing reviews, and says he did his research.
5. "The numbers came back worse than I expected. I'm changing the plan."

Answers: 1 sunk cost · 2 excuse after the fact · 3 double standard · 4 decided first · 5 following the facts.
