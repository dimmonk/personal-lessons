# Presenting new material to novices: research notes for Fieldcraft

Cluster: how to present new material to novices (order of explanation, example and practice; how much guidance; how to write and chunk the explanation).
Researcher notes for the synthesizer. Written 2026-10-04. Scope: adults doing self-study on a phone, learning a diagnostic-classification skill (read an unlabelled case, run a short question key, name the outcome).

## How sources were verified

- Every citation was checked against the Crossref DOI registry (title, authors, venue, year, volume and pages must match) in this session.
- Findings were then read from an abstract retrieved from ERIC, Crossref, OpenAlex or an authors' preprint, or, where the publisher blocked access, from a reputable secondary report. Where only a secondary report was available the entry says so.
- VERIFIED-ONLINE means both checks passed. UNVERIFIED-FROM-MEMORY means I could not confirm it online this session and the claim should be treated as a lead, not a fact. Nothing in the findings below leans on an unverified source except where it is labelled.
- Effect sizes are reported as the authors report them (d or g, Cohen-style). Most lab studies measure learning after minutes to a few days, not months.
- All wording is paraphrased. Study populations are named because this app serves adults, and a lot of this literature studied school children.

## Short answer to the cluster question

For a novice, the best-supported sequence is: orient briefly, explain the idea explicitly and completely in small segments, show fully worked cases with the reasoning visible and sitting next to the rule, compare contrasting cases, then move gradually from "studying the route" to "supplying part of the route" to "doing it alone", with feedback throughout. Guidance should be heavy at the start and be allowed to shrink as competence grows, because the same support that helps a beginner can reduce what a competent learner gains.

Two things in the evidence bear directly on how thin lessons are written. Novices learn more from worked cases and from cohesive, explicit text than from unaided problem solving, unguided discovery, or text that leaves the connections for them to infer (F1, F5, F16). And "less is more" results are about removing irrelevant material, not about shortening necessary explanation (F13, F16). The unit the research says to keep small is the segment, one idea at a time (F10), not the total amount of content. I found no research finding that sets an upper limit on how much explanation a lesson may contain.

The main live disagreement is whether learners should attempt problems before being taught (productive failure). The evidence for it is real but narrow, comes from teacher-led classrooms, and is mixed for novices with little prior knowledge and for younger learners (F6, F7).

## Implications at a glance (for the synthesizer)

These are the design rules the findings below support, with the finding that backs each one. Strength varies; see each finding.

1. Open each unit with a short organizer, a plain-words statement of what the learner will be able to do, and a "terms you will see" card. (F22, F23, F11)
2. Teach in one-idea cards, each one complete enough to be understood by a reader with no background, with no cap on how many cards a unit needs. Cut only what is irrelevant to the route. (F10, F13, F8, F16)
3. One wording per concept, used identically in cards, key questions, options, drills and verdicts; where two terms must coexist, map them once. (F24, F11, F16)
4. Show worked specimens before asking for unaided work: the whole route, each step named in the key's own words, the deciding cue highlighted, the rule on the same card as the case. (F1, F2, F12, F15, F20)
5. Include contrasting pairs of cases and point at the structural cue, not the story. (F19)
6. After a worked case, make the learner do something with the explanation (say which detail decided it) and give feedback immediately. (F17, F18)
7. Build each drill as a ramp: route given and name asked, then partial route, then the whole thing cold. Put faulty-claim items after the correct reasoning is in place. (F3, F21)
8. Design for the true novice by default; make shortcuts for competent readers opt-in. (F4)
9. No unguided discovery. Any try-first step must be scaffolded and followed by explicit comparison with the real key. (F5, F6, F7)
10. Check understanding often, and treat low first-attempt drill accuracy as a sign the cards under-explained. (F8)

## A. The order of explanation, example and practice

### F1. Novices learn more from studying worked solutions than from solving equivalent problems unaided.
- What the research shows: The worked-example effect is among the oldest and best-replicated findings in this literature. A meta-analysis of 55 studies (43 articles, 181 effect sizes) found a medium advantage of studying worked examples over problem solving on mathematics performance, g about 0.48, with participants ranging from elementary school to adulthood and most studies on middle-school algebra or geometry. A separate experiment with secondary-school novices on electrical-circuit troubleshooting found that worked examples, and example-problem pairs, produced lower cognitive load and better learning than problem solving alone. The theoretical account (cognitive load theory) is that a beginner solving a problem spends scarce working memory on searching for a method, which leaves little capacity for building the understanding that makes the method transferable; the experiments above measure the outcome, not the mechanism.
- Sources:
  - Barbieri, C. A., Miller-Cotto, D., Clerjuste, S. N., & Chawla, K. (2023). A meta-analysis of the worked examples effect on mathematics performance. Educational Psychology Review, 35(1), Article 11. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (citation confirmed in Crossref; the numbers above were read from two independent secondary reports because the publisher page was blocked). Meta-analysis.
  - van Gog, T., Kester, L., & Paas, F. (2011). Effects of worked examples, example-problem, and problem-example pairs on novices' learning. Contemporary Educational Psychology, 36(3). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2010.10.004 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Experiment, secondary-school students.
  - Sweller, J., & Cooper, G. A. (1985). The use of worked examples as a substitute for problem solving in learning algebra. Cognition and Instruction, 2(1), 59-89. https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0201_3 . VERIFIED-ONLINE for existence and title only; I did not retrieve its abstract, so the original experimental details are not reported here. Experiments.
  - Sweller, J., van Merrienboer, J. J. G., & Paas, F. G. W. C. (1998). Cognitive architecture and instructional design. Educational Psychology Review, 10(3), 251-296. https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1022193728205 . VERIFIED-ONLINE for existence; its twenty-year update (Educational Psychology Review, 2019, 31, 261-292, https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5) was read in ERIC and describes the theory as resting on the capacity and duration limits of working memory and the unlimited store of long-term memory. Review.
- Strength: strong for novices in well-structured domains, because a meta-analysis plus decades of experiments agree. The evidence base is dominated by mathematics and science problems and by school-age learners, and the measures are mostly immediate or short-delay tests.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The advantage shrinks and reverses as learners become competent (F4). In the meta-analysis, worked examples that contained correct solutions outperformed ones that contained incorrect solutions, and studies that added self-explanation prompts to worked examples showed a smaller advantage than studies without prompts (the authors flag the prompt result as needing more work; see F18). Worked-example benefits shown in math do not automatically carry to every domain; F2 covers the closest evidence for a classification task.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Before a learner is asked to run the key on a specimen, show them a complete worked specimen: the case, the first question and why that question is asked, the answer and what it rules out, each following step, and the name reached, with the reasoning in plain sentences. Drills come after this, not instead of it. A unit that moves from a short definition straight to a drill is asking the novice to do the thing that this literature says consumes the attention they need for learning.

### F2. The worked-example advantage extends to recognising and classifying, not just to calculating.
- What the research shows: The task this app trains is recognition: look at an unlabelled case and decide what it is. In two experiments, higher-education students who were novices at recognising a designer's style were more successful after studying worked examples than after completing problem-solving tasks, within a lecture-then-tutorial format. The benefit held for learners with moderate visual-literacy skills. A different line of work taught student teachers the ill-structured skill of argumentation through examples (N = 71): studying examples taught knowledge about argumentation, and prompts that pointed attention at the underlying principles were needed to build the skill itself.
- Sources:
  - Rourke, A., & Sweller, J. (2009). The worked-example effect using ill-defined problems: Learning to recognise designers' styles. Learning and Instruction, 19(2), 185-199. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2008.03.006 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Two experiments, university students.
  - Quilici, J. L., & Mayer, R. E. (1996). Role of examples in how students learn to categorize statistics word problems. Journal of Educational Psychology, 88(1), 144-161. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.88.1.144 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via OpenAlex, matches the Crossref record). Three experiments, college students.
  - Schworm, S., & Renkl, A. (2007). Learning argumentation skills through the use of prompts for self-explaining examples. Journal of Educational Psychology, 99(2), 285-296. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.2.285 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC; bibliographic details confirmed in Crossref). Experiment, student teachers.
- Further evidence: a second line of work concerns learning to categorise problems by type. College students who studied worked example problems grouped by solution type (t test, correlation, chi-square) later sorted new problems by their structure rather than their cover story, and worked examples that emphasised structure led to more correct choices of the statistical test than examples that emphasised surface features, an effect strong for lower-ability students and not for higher-ability students (details in F19).
- Strength: limited to moderate. Only a few experiments test the effect on recognition or ill-structured skills, and the best one is conditional on learners having some baseline skill.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The recognition result applied to novices with moderate visual literacy, so complete beginners may need more than examples. Examples alone taught declarative knowledge of argumentation but not the skill, which required prompts about principles.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: A specimen is a worked example for a classification task, and it should be presented as one in the lesson, with the route shown first. The explanation of why each option was chosen should name the principle in the same words that appear in the outcome definitions, because the argumentation result says that showing instances without pointing at the principle teaches facts about the examples rather than the skill.

### F3. Order within practice matters: show the example first, then a problem, and fade the support gradually.
- What the research shows: In an earlier experiment with 46 Dutch secondary-school students learning statistics, worked-out problems and partially worked-out problems that the learner had to complete both beat conventional problem solving, and the completion strategy did best. With secondary-school novices in a later study, example-problem pairs (study an example, then solve a similar problem) did well, whereas problem-example pairs did no better than problems alone; also, studying only examples did no worse than alternating examples with problems. A "fading" procedure goes further: problem-solving elements are integrated into example study one at a time, for example by leaving out one more solution step each time, until the learner solves the whole problem. In experiments with ninth graders and with two groups of US college students, fading beat plain example-problem pairs on near transfer, but the far-transfer benefit was unreliable. When learners were also prompted to name the principle behind each worked step, fading produced medium to large effects on both near and far transfer without taking longer.
- Sources:
  - Paas, F. G. W. C. (1992). Training strategies for attaining transfer of problem-solving skill in statistics: A cognitive-load approach. Journal of Educational Psychology, 84(4), 429-434. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.84.4.429 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Experiment, secondary-school students.
  - van Gog, Kester, & Paas (2011), as in F1. VERIFIED-ONLINE.
  - Renkl, A., Atkinson, R. K., Maier, U. H., & Staley, R. (2002). From example study to problem solving: Smooth transitions help learning. Journal of Experimental Education, 70(4), 293-315. https://doi.org/10.1080/00220970209599510 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Three experiments.
  - Atkinson, R. K., Renkl, A., & Merrill, M. M. (2003). Transitioning from studying examples to solving problems: Effects of self-explanation prompts and fading worked-out steps. Journal of Educational Psychology, 95(4), 774-783. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.95.4.774 . VERIFIED-ONLINE. Two experiments.
  - Renkl, A., & Atkinson, R. K. (2003). Structuring the transition from example study to problem solving in cognitive skill acquisition: A cognitive load perspective. Educational Psychologist, 38(1), 15-22. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep3801_3 . VERIFIED-ONLINE. Theoretical analysis with supporting evidence.
- Strength: moderate. The fading studies are several, include adults, and agree on near transfer, but they come largely from one research group and no meta-analysis of fading turned up in my searches.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Far transfer needs the principle-naming prompts. Fading only makes sense if learners are moving from novice toward competence; the authors themselves say a fading scheme should adapt to each learner's prior knowledge, which a fixed lesson cannot do.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Within a unit, make the drill items a ramp, not a flat set. Early items show the whole route and ask the learner to supply only the last piece (the name, or one answer in the key). Middle items leave out more of the route. Final items ask for the whole route cold. Because the app scores name and route separately, this maps neatly: route given and name asked is the easiest rung; route and name both asked is the hardest. Put a short worked case before the first rung.

### F4. Guidance that helps novices can hurt learners who have become competent (the expertise reversal effect).
- What the research shows: A review of the literature found that many instructional techniques that work well for beginners lose their benefit, or even do damage, for learners with more experience. (The usual explanation is that guidance an experienced learner no longer needs still has to be processed, which adds load; the abstract I read does not test that account separately.) A 2025 meta-analysis (60 experimental studies, 176 effect sizes, about 5,900 participants) reports that learners with low prior knowledge learned more from high-assistance instruction (d about 0.51), while learners with high prior knowledge learned more from low-assistance instruction (d about -0.43), with the size of the effect depending on how prior knowledge was assessed, the education level of the sample and the domain. A theoretical paper argues that this reversal is one case of the element-interactivity effect: the same material is demanding or easy depending on the learner's expertise.
- Sources:
  - Kalyuga, S., Ayres, P., Chandler, P., & Sweller, J. (2003). The expertise reversal effect. Educational Psychologist, 38(1), 23-31. https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via OpenAlex and checked against Crossref). Review.
  - Kalyuga, S. (2007). Expertise reversal effect and its implications for learner-tailored instruction. Educational Psychology Review, 19(4), 509-539. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-007-9054-3 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Review.
  - Tetzlaff, L., Simonsmeier, B., Peters, T., & Brod, G. (2025). A cornerstone of adaptivity: A meta-analysis of the expertise reversal effect. Learning and Instruction, 98, 102142. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142 . VERIFIED-ONLINE for existence in Crossref; the numbers come from two independent secondary reports (the same figures both times) because the publisher page was blocked. Meta-analysis.
  - Chen, O., Kalyuga, S., & Sweller, J. (2017). The expertise reversal effect is a variant of the more general element interactivity effect. Educational Psychology Review, 29, 393-405. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-016-9359-1 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Theoretical review.
- Strength: strong for the general pattern, because there is now a meta-analysis, though the individual techniques pooled together are heterogeneous and "prior knowledge" is measured in many ways.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The effect is an interaction, so it only matters if the learner population varies in knowledge. For a true beginner audience it argues for more support, not less. Its practical use depends on being able to tell who has the knowledge, which usually means a test.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: The audience is adult self-studiers with wildly different starting points (a reader may already know basic probability or some civics). Default every lesson to the full novice version, because the stated audience is novices and the meta-analytic size of the benefit of assistance for them (about +0.51) is as large as the cost of redundant assistance for knowledgeable learners (about -0.43), so the default should follow who the learners are. Make shortcuts for competent readers opt-in, for example a quick check at the start of a unit whose success lets the learner skip the worked cases, rather than building a sparse default and hoping beginners cope. Do not read this finding as a licence to trim the novice lesson.

### F5. Unguided discovery loses to explicit instruction; scaffolded (guided) discovery can match or beat it.
- What the research shows: A meta-analysis of 164 studies separated two questions. Across 580 comparisons, explicit instruction beat unassisted discovery (d about -0.38 against discovery). Across 360 comparisons, enhanced or assisted discovery beat other forms of instruction (d about 0.30). The authors conclude that discovery helps when it comes with feedback, worked examples, scaffolding or elicited explanations. A later meta-analysis of 72 inquiry-learning studies found that adding guidance had positive effects on learning outcomes overall (d about 0.50), and that the type of guidance did not change the learning-outcome result. A large meta-analysis of Direct Instruction curricula (328 studies, almost 4,000 effects, with reading, maths, language and spelling outcomes) found consistently positive, moderate-to-large effects, but those are structured programmes delivered with a teacher, not self-study.
- Sources:
  - Alfieri, L., Brooks, P. J., Aldrich, N. J., & Tenenbaum, H. R. (2011). Does discovery-based instruction enhance learning? Journal of Educational Psychology, 103(1), 1-18. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021017 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (title confirmed in Crossref; numbers read from a reputable secondary summary of the abstract; note that one open index displays a wrong abstract for this DOI, so do not rely on indexes alone). Meta-analysis.
  - Kirschner, P. A., Sweller, J., & Clark, R. E. (2006). Why minimal guidance during instruction does not work. Educational Psychologist, 41(2), 75-86. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Position paper and review.
  - Hmelo-Silver, C. E., Duncan, R. G., & Chinn, C. A. (2007). Scaffolding and achievement in problem-based and inquiry learning: A response to Kirschner, Sweller, and Clark (2006). Educational Psychologist, 42(2), 99-107. https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520701263368 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Critical response.
  - Lazonder, A. W., & Harmsen, R. (2016). Meta-analysis of inquiry-based learning: Effects of guidance. Review of Educational Research, 86(3), 681-718. https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654315627366 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Crossref-linked index and ERIC). Meta-analysis.
  - Stockard, J., Wood, T. W., Coughlin, C., & Rasplica Khoury, C. (2018). The effectiveness of Direct Instruction curricula: A meta-analysis of a half century of research. Review of Educational Research, 88(4), 479-507. https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654317751919 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC; bibliographic details confirmed in Crossref). Meta-analysis.
- Strength: strong for "pure discovery is worse", contested for the broader "explicit versus problem-based" debate.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Kirschner et al. themselves say the advantage of guidance recedes once learners have enough prior knowledge to guide themselves (consistent with F4). Their critics argue that problem-based and inquiry methods are heavily scaffolded and should not be lumped in with unguided discovery, which is why the meta-analytic split between assisted and unassisted discovery matters.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: No card should ask a novice to infer the concept, the names or the route without support. Anything that invites discovery, such as "what do these three cases have in common?", must come with the answer, feedback and an explanation attached, not leave the learner to work it out and wonder if they got it right.

### F6. Problem-solving before instruction can beat instruction-first, under narrow conditions.
- What the research shows: "Productive failure" and "invention" designs have learners attempt an unfamiliar problem, usually without success, before being taught the canonical method. A 2021 meta-analysis of 53 studies (166 comparisons) found a moderate advantage for problem-solving-first over instruction-first (g about 0.36), larger (g about 0.37 to 0.58) when the design followed the productive-failure principles closely; grade level, length of intervention and study design moderated it, and trends reversed (favoured instruction first) for learners in grades two to five and for domain-general skills. The authors' publication-bias-adjusted estimate was larger still (about 0.87), which is a model-dependent correction and should be read cautiously. A review of the design literature found that the problem-first approach produced learning gains only when it included contrasting cases or when the later instruction built on the students' own solutions. In individual studies, eleventh-grade physics students who failed at ill-structured problems then outperformed peers on transfer, adolescents who invented formulas with contrasting cases before being told transferred better than those told first (equal on routine word problems, and the benefit held for lower as well as higher achievers), and undergraduate physics students who explored first showed better conceptual and equal procedural knowledge.
- Sources:
  - Sinha, T., & Kapur, M. (2021). When problem solving followed by instruction works: Evidence for productive failure. Review of Educational Research, 91(5), 761-798. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via OpenAlex, matches Crossref record). Meta-analysis.
  - Loibl, K., Roll, I., & Rummel, N. (2017). Towards a theory of when and how problem solving followed by instruction supports learning. Educational Psychology Review, 29(4), 693-715. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-016-9379-x . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Systematic review.
  - Kapur, M. (2008). Productive failure. Cognition and Instruction, 26(3), 379-424. https://doi.org/10.1080/07370000802212669 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Experiment, eleventh-grade students, collaborative.
  - Schwartz, D. L., Chase, C. C., Oppezzo, M. A., & Chin, D. B. (2011). Practicing versus inventing with contrasting cases: The effects of telling first on learning and transfer. Journal of Educational Psychology, 103(4), 759-775. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025140 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Two experiments, adolescents.
  - Weaver, J. P., Chastain, R. J., DeCaro, D. A., & DeCaro, M. S. (2018). Reverse the routine: Problem solving before instruction improves conceptual knowledge in undergraduate physics. Contemporary Educational Psychology, 52, 36-47. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2017.12.003 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (existence in Crossref; finding read from a secondary report of the abstract). Experiment, undergraduates.
- Strength: contested. A meta-analysis supports it on average, but the design requirements are demanding, the evidence is concentrated in STEM and a small number of research groups (including the proponents), and head-to-head studies elsewhere disagree (F7).
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Learners need enough knowledge to produce attempts; the later instruction must be explicit and must compare the learners' attempts with the canonical method; most studies ran in classrooms with a teacher and small-group discussion; younger learners and domain-general skills favoured instruction first.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: A "try first" step is defensible only in a restricted form: unlabelled cases to sort or compare before the categories are named, immediately followed by a card that explicitly sets the learner's intuitive grouping against the real key and names the real distinguishing features. It should not be the default opening of a unit for a true novice, and it should never replace explanation. All the supporting studies had a teacher or peers to carry the consolidation, which a phone lesson lacks (see "What the research does not tell us").

### F7. Several head-to-head tests favour instruction first, especially for beginners and children.
- What the research shows: In a randomized experiment with 122 second and third graders, giving conceptual instruction before problem solving produced better procedural and conceptual knowledge than delaying it until after problem solving, and improved the quality of the children's explanations during problem solving. In 57 third graders, a high-guidance then high-guidance sequence produced stronger understanding than low then low, including at a five-month test, and there was no advantage to putting low guidance first. In a study comparing a fully guided worked example, a partly guided attempt and an unguided attempt before explicit instruction, delayed transfer did not differ across the three, though the worked example lowered cognitive load; the authors concluded that different sequences can reach similar outcomes while serving different goals. The meta-analysis in F6 also found a reversal for grades two to five.
- Sources:
  - Fyfe, E. R., DeCaro, M. S., & Rittle-Johnson, B. (2014). An alternative time for telling: When conceptual instruction prior to problem solving improves mathematical knowledge. British Journal of Educational Psychology, 84(3), 502-519. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjep.12035 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Randomized experiment, children.
  - Matlen, B. J., & Klahr, D. (2013). Sequential effects of high and low instructional guidance on children's acquisition of experimentation skills: Is it all in the timing? Instructional Science, 41, 621-634. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-012-9248-z . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Experiment, third graders.
  - Likourezos, V., & Kalyuga, S. (2017). Instruction-first and problem-solving-first approaches: Alternative pathways to learning complex tasks. Instructional Science, 45, 195-219. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-016-9399-4 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC; participant details were not in the abstract and were not retrieved). Experiment.
  - Sinha & Kapur (2021), as in F6.
- Strength: moderate. These are individual experiments, mostly with children, and they use different problem types from the productive-failure studies. They do not overturn F6, but they show the effect is not general.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Fyfe et al. say they compare their results with earlier contrasting findings to outline a framework for when instruction should or should not be delayed; I did not retrieve the details. The adult-novice case in an unfamiliar domain is the least tested.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: When in doubt, explain first. The unit order of "lesson cards, then that unit's drill" is consistent with the better-supported novice sequence; the research adds that the cards must contain the whole explanation and a worked case, and that the drill should be a ramp (F3), not a test of material that was only gestured at.

## B. Writing and chunking the explanation

### F8. Rosenshine's synthesis: teach in small steps, give detailed explanation and many examples, and spend more time presenting, with practice after each step.
- What the research shows: Rosenshine's 2012 article distils ten principles from three bodies of work: cognitive science on working-memory limits, observational studies of the teachers whose classes made the biggest achievement gains, and research on cognitive supports (modelling, thinking aloud, scaffolds). The concrete points that matter here: new material should be presented in small amounts with practice after each step; the most effective mathematics teachers in one study spent roughly 23 of 40 minutes presenting, demonstrating and working examples, against about 11 minutes for the least effective, and used the extra time for more explanation, more examples and checking understanding; a success rate of roughly 80 percent during practice appeared optimal (82 versus 73 percent in one fourth-grade study); and hands-on or experiential work was done after, not before, the basic material was learned. His expanded list of 17 principles includes "give clear and detailed instructions and explanations", "use more time to provide explanations", "provide many examples" and "limit the amount of material students receive at one time".
- Sources: Rosenshine, B. (2012). Principles of instruction: Research-based strategies that all teachers should know. American Educator, 36(1), Spring 2012, beginning p. 12. https://www.aft.org/ae/spring2012/rosenshine . VERIFIED-ONLINE (full text read from the publisher's PDF; also indexed in ERIC). Narrative synthesis of observational teacher studies, cognitive science and cognitive-support research.
- Strength: moderate. It agrees with the experimental evidence in F1, F3 and F5, but the quantitative claims come from observational studies of school teachers, and the package has not been tested as a whole.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The research is on teacher-led classrooms with children and relies on the teacher checking every student's answers in real time. The 80 percent figure comes from a small number of elementary mathematics studies and is an association, not a tested target. Rosenshine's account of "small steps" is about the amount of new material per step, not about how much explanation each step receives.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Read "small steps" as small conceptual steps, each one fully explained, not as short text. One card should carry one idea and everything needed to understand that idea, including its worked example. Add a quick check after every few cards, not only at the end of the unit. Treat low first-attempt accuracy on a unit drill as a sign that the cards before it explained too little, and design drills so that a learner who read the cards properly succeeds most of the time, not half of the time. Hands-on application belongs after the basics, which is how the unit sequence (cards, then drill) already runs.

### F9. Merrill's First Principles of Instruction are a useful design checklist, but the evidence for them as a package is thin.
- What the research shows: Merrill examined a range of instructional design theories and extracted five conditions under which learning is promoted: learners work on real-world problems, existing knowledge is activated as a foundation, new knowledge is demonstrated to the learner, the learner applies it, and the learner integrates it into their own world. As evidence of effect, one correlational study of 464 students in 12 university courses found that students who agreed their instructors used these principles and who agreed they experienced "academic learning time" were about five times more likely to reach a high level of mastery and about 26 times less likely to reach a low level, as judged independently by the instructors.
- Sources:
  - Merrill, M. D. (2002). First principles of instruction. Educational Technology Research and Development, 50(3), 43-59. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02505024 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Theoretical synthesis.
  - Frick, T. W., Chadha, R., Watson, C., & Zlatkovska, E. (2010). Improving course evaluations to improve instruction and complex learning in higher education. Educational Technology Research and Development, 58, 115-136. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11423-009-9131-z . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Correlational study, undergraduates and graduates, student-rated.
- Strength: limited as an empirical claim. The five conditions summarise other bodies of evidence (activation F22, demonstration F1, application F3, integration F19) but I found no experiment that tests the package against an alternative, and the main supporting study is correlational and rests on student perceptions.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: "Real-world problem first" must not be read as "unaided problem solving first"; the evidence in F5 to F7 does not support that for novices. The abstract I could retrieve does not detail how Merrill sequences the problem and the demonstration, so I do not claim more than the five conditions.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Use it as a unit-level audit, not as proof. Each unit should show the kind of case the learner will face, activate what they already believe, demonstrate the route on a worked case, let them apply it, and then have them integrate it, for instance by classifying a case from their own life or in the cross-subject mixed drill.

### F10. Break material into learner-paced segments at idea boundaries; segmenting is about chunk size, not about leaving content out.
- What the research shows: A meta-analysis of 56 investigations (88 comparisons) found that learner-paced, meaningful segments produced small-to-medium gains in retention and transfer relative to a continuous presentation, lowered reported cognitive load, and increased learning time. The effect also held when the designer paced the segments. Learners with high prior knowledge benefited more than learners with little or none on retention, which is the opposite of what one might expect and is not explained in the abstract.
- Sources: Rey, G. D., Beege, M., Nebel, S., Wirzberger, M., Schmitt, T. H., & Schneider, S. (2019). A meta-analysis of the segmenting effect. Educational Psychology Review, 31, 389-419. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC; also confirmed by a second secondary report). Meta-analysis.
- Strength: moderate. A meta-analysis supports it, but the bulk of the studies used narrated animations or similar multimedia rather than text cards, and effects are small to medium.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The novice-versus-expert moderation shows that segmenting alone does not rescue a learner who lacks the background to interpret the segments. Segmenting increases time on task, so any comparison of segmented against unsegmented material that holds time fixed would not show the same gain.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Cards one at a time are already a form of learner-paced segmentation, which is supported. Cut at idea boundaries, give each card a single point, and let the number of cards grow as the explanation needs; the research is about the size of each piece, not about total length. Pair segmenting with pre-training (F11) and complete worked cases (F1) rather than treating it as a substitute for them.

### F11. Teach the names and the parts of the key concepts before the main explanation (pre-training).
- What the research shows: Mayer's reviews report that people learn more from a multimedia lesson when they first learn the names and characteristics of its main components. His chapter-level summaries report support in 5 of 5 tests (median effect about d = 0.85) in one book chapter and in 13 of 16 tests (median about d = 0.75) in a handbook chapter. The example lesson in one of those summaries is a narrated animation of how a physical system, a car's braking system, works, and the benefit appeared on problem-solving transfer tests.
- Sources:
  - Mayer, R. E., Mathias, A., & Wetzell, K. (2002). Fostering understanding of multimedia messages through pre-training: Evidence for a two-stage theory of mental model construction. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 8(3), 147-154. https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898X.8.3.147 . VERIFIED-ONLINE for existence and bibliographic details; its abstract was not retrievable, so I rely on the reviews below for the finding. Experiments.
  - Mayer's chapters on the pre-training principle (Cambridge University Press): https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/multimedia-learning/pretraining-principle/01791D57F5D4164251269E6DF56A8BF1 (reports 5 of 5 tests, median d = 0.85) and https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-managing-essential-processing-in-multimedia-learning-segmenting-pretraining-and-modality-principles/DD24C2F48B9B1277CE59F78276110258 (reports 13 of 16 tests, median d = 0.75). VERIFIED-ONLINE (chapter summaries read). Reviews by the originator of the principle.
- Strength: moderate to limited. The effect sizes look large, but they come from the principle's originator, from a small set of lab studies on narrated animations with college students, and I found no independent meta-analysis.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The studies targeted multimedia explanations of mechanisms, not classification tasks. The chapter summaries I retrieved do not say who benefits most, and I did not verify claims that it helps beginners more than experts, although that is the expected direction given F4.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Before the explanation, give a short card that introduces each key term in plain words, says what it looks like, and gives one example, using the exact words that the outcome names, key questions and drill options will use later (see F24). In this app the "components" are the outcome names, the diagnostic questions and the options, so pre-train those.

### F12. Signal what matters: highlight the cue that decides the answer and show how the material is organised.
- What the research shows: A meta-analysis of 103 studies (12,201 participants, students of all ages, university students and adults, mostly laboratory studies) found that signalling (highlights, bolding, colour, organisers, pointers) improved retention (g about 0.53) and transfer (g about 0.33) and lowered reported cognitive load. A second meta-analysis on signalling in multimedia learning environments reaches the same general conclusion. Moderator results were inconsistent for prior knowledge.
- Sources:
  - Schneider, S., Beege, M., Nebel, S., & Rey, G. D. (2018). A meta-analysis of how signaling affects learning with media. Educational Research Review, 23, 1-24. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2017.11.001 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (existence in Crossref; the numbers were read from a German-language review by a university clearinghouse and agree with a second search summary). Meta-analysis.
  - Alpizar, D., Adesope, O. O., & Wong, R. M. (2020). A meta-analysis of signaling principle in multimedia learning environments. Educational Technology Research and Development, 68(5), 2095-2119. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11423-020-09748-7 . VERIFIED-ONLINE for existence only. Meta-analysis.
- Strength: moderate to strong. Two meta-analyses agree on a positive effect, but most of the evidence comes from lab studies with a mix of materials.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Signals only help if they point at what matters; a page with everything highlighted signals nothing, and cues that attract attention to irrelevant material would fall under F13. Findings on whether prior knowledge moderates the effect are inconsistent.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: In every worked specimen, mark the phrase in the case that decides each answer in the key, in a consistent visual style, and say why it decides. Use the same marking for "the cue that tells you" across all seven subjects. Put the structure of the unit up front (what the three or four ideas are and how they connect) as a signal for the learner to organise their reading.

### F13. Remove interesting-but-irrelevant material; do not remove necessary explanation.
- What the research shows: A review and meta-analysis of 39 experimental effects found that people learn more deeply from instructional messages that leave out "seductive details" (interesting but irrelevant text, pictures or anecdotes), with small to medium effects on retention and medium effects on transfer. In a classic set of experiments with 163 college students, a summary consisting of short captions with simple illustrations depicting the steps of a process led to recall and problem-solving transfer as good as or better than the full textbook text, with or without a summary. The summary there was built around the steps of the process, so it was brief but not empty.
- Sources:
  - Rey, G. D. (2012). A review of research and a meta-analysis of the seductive detail effect. Educational Research Review, 7(3), 216-237. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2012.05.003 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Review and meta-analysis.
  - Mayer, R. E., Bove, W., Bryman, A., Mars, R., & Tapangco, L. (1996). When less is more: Meaningful learning from visual and verbal summaries of science textbook lessons. Journal of Educational Psychology, 88(1), 64-73. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.88.1.64 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Three experiments.
- Strength: moderate. Two bodies of evidence point the same way, but the "less is more" result is a comparison against a long, diffuse textbook passage, not against a thin explanation.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: This finding is about relevance, not length. It is easy to misapply it as "shorter is better". The same literature that supports cutting irrelevant detail supports adding missing connective explanation for novices (F16), and Rosenshine's observation that better teachers spent more time presenting (F8). Rey's own review calls into question each of the four proposed explanations for the effect.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Cut anecdotes, jokes, trivia and long digressions that are not part of the route to the answer. Keep every step of the diagnostic reasoning and every definition that a later question relies on. A lesson with two paragraphs of necessary explanation is consistent with this research; a lesson with two phrases of necessary explanation leaves out material the novice needs.

### F14. Write in a conversational, direct voice.
- What the research shows: A meta-analysis of studies that rewrote text in a conversational style (second-person address, polite forms, a more visible author) found reliable benefits for retention (d about 0.30) and transfer (d about 0.54), and for learners' self-reported friendliness and effective cognitive processing, but not for self-reported interest or learning assistance. The clearest boundary was instructional time: effects were small and not statistically reliable in studies longer than about 35 minutes.
- Sources: Ginns, P., Martin, A. J., & Marsh, H. W. (2013). Designing instructional text in a conversational style: A meta-analysis. Educational Psychology Review, 25(4), 445-472. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-013-9228-0 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Meta-analysis.
- Strength: moderate. A meta-analysis supports it, though most of the studies are short lab lessons, and the abstract gives no breakdown by learner age.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The benefit faded in lessons longer than about 35 minutes, though each unit here is short. Conversational voice that adds chatter belongs under F13 rather than here.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Address the learner as "you", use plain words, and say what the idea means in ordinary language before introducing the term. This directly answers the owner's complaint that some language is too technical: where a technical term is unavoidable, introduce it in plain words first and reuse it identically afterwards.

### F15. Keep related pieces of information together so the learner does not have to hunt for the match (split attention).
- What the research shows: A meta-analysis of 50 independent studies found that reducing the spatial or temporal separation between related pieces of information (for example, putting labels or explanations next to the thing they describe) gave learning gains, particularly for complex materials. The review says the benefits are for novices.
- Sources: Ginns, P. (2006). Integrating information: A meta-analysis of the spatial contiguity and temporal contiguity effects. Learning and Instruction, 16(6), 511-525. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2006.10.001 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Meta-analysis.
- Strength: moderate to strong for diagram-plus-text materials; limited for text-only lessons, which is my inference from where the studies come from.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The studies mostly involve diagrams or animations and their labels. For a lesson built from cards, this is an analogy: how far apart the rule, the example and the question text sit.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: In a worked specimen, put the rule and the reasoning next to the case that illustrates it, on the same card. When a later card relies on a definition from an earlier card, repeat the definition in a line rather than expecting the learner to remember it or go back. On the phone, a learner cannot see two cards at once, so integration must happen inside each card.

## C. Making worked cases do their work

### F16. Novices learn better from cohesive, explicit text that spells out the connections; high-knowledge readers do not.
- What the research shows: In experiments with junior-high students reading science texts, coherent text improved comprehension, but readers with enough background knowledge did better, on deep questions, with a less coherent text that forced them to make inferences. The same pattern, that readers with little background need the connections stated and readers with more do not, points in the same direction as F4. Revising fifth-grade social-studies text using a text-processing approach led to better recall and better answers to questions, and a follow-up found the revised text still helped when students were first given relevant background knowledge. Revising an expository text by repairing places where readers had to infer missing links improved how well it conveyed the author's intent, in experiments with 170 undergraduates and 125 Air Force recruits.
- Sources:
  - McNamara, D. S., Kintsch, E., Songer, N. B., & Kintsch, W. (1996). Are good texts always better? Interactions of text coherence, background knowledge, and levels of understanding in learning from text. Cognition and Instruction, 14(1), 1-43. https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1401_1 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Two experiments.
  - Beck, I. L., McKeown, M. G., Sinatra, G. M., & Loxterman, J. A. (1991). Revising social studies text from a text-processing perspective: Evidence of improved comprehensibility. Reading Research Quarterly, 26(3), 251 ff. https://doi.org/10.2307/747763 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC; Crossref gives only the start page). Experiment, fifth graders.
  - McKeown, M. G., Beck, I. L., Sinatra, G. M., & Loxterman, J. A. (1992). The contribution of prior knowledge and coherent text to comprehension. Reading Research Quarterly, 27(1), 78 ff. https://doi.org/10.2307/747834 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC; Crossref gives only the start page). Experiment, students (grade level not stated in the abstract).
  - Britton, B. K., & Gülgöz, S. (1991). Using Kintsch's computational model to improve instructional text: Effects of repairing inference calls on recall and cognitive structures. Journal of Educational Psychology, 83(3), 329-345. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.83.3.329 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Two experiments, adults.
- Strength: moderate. Four papers from three research groups, including one with adults, agree for low-knowledge readers; the effect of coherence for high-knowledge readers is the reverse, as F4 predicts.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The reverse cohesion effect means experts can profit from gaps. For a beginner audience this is not an argument for leaving gaps. I did not retrieve word counts, so I cannot say by how much revisions lengthened the texts, but repairing an inference gap by definition means putting the missing information into the text.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: The owner's complaint that the "flow of information" does not build understanding is the property this literature studies. Write each card so a reader with no background can follow it without filling in links: state why this idea follows the last one, define each term where it is first used, say what a result means ("so you can rule out X"), and use the same word each time you mean the same thing, because repeated reference to the same concept is what lets readers connect sentences into one structure (that last point is my reading of the Kintsch model underlying the Britton and Gülgöz revisions, not a finding I confirmed in their abstract). This is writing the missing explanation in, not padding.

### F17. Instructional explanations help conceptual learning, but not automatically.
- What the research shows: A meta-analysis of 21 experiments on explanations added to example-based learning concluded that the benefit of instructional explanations per se was minimal; that explanations helped more for conceptual than for procedural knowledge; and that they were not necessarily better than prompting learners to explain to themselves. A companion framework paper explains why explanations frequently fail to help, drawing on classroom, tutoring, text, computer and multimedia research, and sets out general guidelines for designing them.
- Sources:
  - Wittwer, J., & Renkl, A. (2010). How effective are instructional explanations in example-based learning? A meta-analytic review. Educational Psychology Review, 22(4), 393-409. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-010-9136-5 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC; the same abstract appears in the Semantic Scholar record flagged as publisher-held). Meta-analysis.
  - Wittwer, J., & Renkl, A. (2008). Why instructional explanations often do not work: A framework for understanding the effectiveness of instructional explanations. Educational Psychologist, 43(1), 49-64. https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520701756420 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Framework review.
- Strength: moderate. The meta-analysis is on a modest number of studies, and the result "minimal on average" depends on what counts as an explanation.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: This is a caution against assuming that more words equal more learning. The abstract does not list which design guidelines the framework proposes, so I do not report them. Conceptual outcomes benefit more, and classification is a conceptual skill, which is in this app's favour.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Explanation does not do its work by being present. After an explanation, make the learner do something with it (F18), and aim the explanation at the confusions a beginner will actually have, the "why" behind each distinction, not at restating the definition. Judge an explanation by whether first-attempt drill performance improves, not by whether it exists.

### F18. Prompting learners to explain examples to themselves helps, when it is done well.
- What the research shows: A meta-analysis of 64 research reports (69 effect sizes) found that prompting self-explanation raised learning with a medium effect (g about 0.55), across solving problems, studying worked problems and studying text, with the authors coding subject area and level of education as moderators and calling it a potentially powerful intervention across a range of instructional conditions. The earlier foundational study found that stronger students produced many explanations linking the steps of worked examples to principles, monitored their own understanding well, and then relied less on the examples, whereas weaker students did not. Combining fading with prompts that ask the learner to name the principle behind each step produced medium to large effects on near and far transfer (F3).
- Sources:
  - Bisra, K., Liu, Q., Nesbit, J. C., Salimi, F., & Winne, P. H. (2018). Inducing self-explanation: A meta-analysis. Educational Psychology Review, 30, 703-725. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Meta-analysis.
  - Chi, M. T. H., Bassok, M., Lewis, M. W., Reimann, P., & Glaser, R. (1989). Self-explanations: How students study and use examples in learning to solve problems. Cognitive Science, 13(2), 145-182. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1302_1 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Crossref-linked index). Observational study of "good" and "poor" students studying physics examples.
  - Atkinson, Renkl, & Merrill (2003), as in F3.
- Strength: moderate to strong overall, with an unresolved conflict: the worked-examples meta-analysis in F1 found a smaller worked-example advantage in studies that added prompts than in studies that did not.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The Bisra authors note that scripted prompts have limits and recommend exploring computer-generated prompts. The F1 result suggests prompts may cost time or load for some learners, and I did not find a resolution. In the argumentation study (F2) the authors recommend prompts directed at principles when the aim is to build the skill.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: After a worked specimen, ask the learner to say which detail decided a given answer and why, using a tap on the highlighted phrase or a multiple-choice "because" statement (typing on a phone is costly; this is a practical choice, not a research claim), and reveal the correct reasoning straight away. The app already scores the route separately from the name, which is a built-in self-explanation prompt; the lesson cards should rehearse it before the drill requires it.

### F19. Comparing cases side by side teaches the underlying structure rather than the surface.
- What the research shows: A meta-analysis of 57 experiments (336 tests) found that case comparison led to better learning than other ways of studying cases, including studying them one after another, a single case, or traditional instruction (d about 0.50). Four moderators mattered: the objective of the comparison, whether a principle was presented, the content, and the delay before testing. Asking learners to find similarities between cases, giving the principle after the comparison, using perceptual content and testing immediately all went with larger effects. In the closest classification-like study, students who studied statistics word problems grouped by solution type were more likely to sort new problems by their structure rather than by their cover story, structure-emphasising examples did better than surface-emphasising ones, and worked-out structure-emphasising examples helped students choose the right statistical test, an effect strong for lower-ability students and not for higher-ability students.
- Sources:
  - Alfieri, L., Nokes-Malach, T. J., & Schunn, C. D. (2013). Learning through case comparisons: A meta-analytic review. Educational Psychologist, 48(2), 87-113. https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2013.775712 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Meta-analysis.
  - Quilici, J. L., & Mayer, R. E. (1996). Role of examples in how students learn to categorize statistics word problems. Journal of Educational Psychology, 88(1), 144-161. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.88.1.144 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via OpenAlex, matches the Crossref title). Three experiments, college students.
  - Quilici, J. L., & Mayer, R. E. (2002). Teaching students to recognize structural similarities between statistics word problems. Applied Cognitive Psychology, 16(3), 325-342. https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.796 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read). Experiments, college students.
- Strength: strong for comparison in general, moderate for the structure-versus-surface result because it comes from a few experiments in one domain.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The benefit of delivering the principle after comparison fits the productive-failure line (F6), which is one reason "principle last" is not simply wrong, but note that the evidence for "principle after" is a moderator within comparison studies, not a head-to-head with instruction first for complete novices. The effect was strongest for immediate tests, so durability is less certain.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: This is the closest published match to the app's core skill. Include pairs of cases in the lesson cards: two that look alike but belong to different outcomes, and two that look different but belong to the same outcome, with the decisive feature pointed out. Direct attention to the structural cue the key tests, not the topic or story of the case. Naming the principle either before, in the explanation, or immediately after the comparison is supported; leaving the comparison unexplained is not.

### F20. Label the steps of the route, and show the principle behind each step.
- What the research shows: Worked examples whose steps are grouped under explicit subgoal labels improved undergraduate problem solving in two laboratory experiments, and had similar and perhaps stronger effects in a third, online experiment with in-service K-12 teachers (adults) learning programming. A review of the worked-example literature organised its findings into design principles for writing examples.
- Sources:
  - Margulieux, L. E., Catrambone, R., & Guzdial, M. (2016). Employing subgoals in computer programming education. Computer Science Education, 26(1), 44-67. https://doi.org/10.1080/08993408.2016.1144429 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Three experiments.
  - Atkinson, R. K., Derry, S. J., Renkl, A., & Wortham, D. (2000). Learning from examples: Instructional principles from the worked examples research. Review of Educational Research, 70(2), 181-214. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543070002181 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC; the abstract does not list the principles, so I do not report them). Review.
  - Catrambone, R. (1998). The subgoal learning model: Creating better examples so that students can solve novel problems. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 127(4), 355-376. https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.127.4.355 . VERIFIED-ONLINE for existence and title only; abstract not retrieved.
- Strength: limited to moderate. The adult online experiment is the closest to this app's setting, but it is programming, with one research group.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: I did not find a test of subgoal labelling in a non-procedural classification task.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Each question in the determination key is a natural subgoal. In worked specimens, name each step with the same words the key uses for that question, so that what the learner studies is what they will later see and do. That also makes the route explicit and gradable.

### F21. Examples with errors, and varied examples: they help conceptual understanding, but only for learners who already have the basics.
- What the research shows: In two classroom experiments within an algebra tutoring program, explaining worked examples during practice improved conceptual understanding relative to practice alone, and incorrect examples, alone or mixed with correct ones, may have been especially beneficial for conceptual understanding. In contrast, in two experiments on probability problems, a mixture of correct and incorrect solutions improved far transfer only for learners with favourable prior knowledge, whereas learners with poor prior knowledge did better with correct solutions only; incorrect solutions also reduced the amount of principle-based self-explanation. The worked-examples meta-analysis in F1 likewise found that correct examples outperformed incorrect ones overall.
- Sources:
  - Booth, J. L., Lange, K. E., Koedinger, K. R., & Newton, K. J. (2013). Using example problems to improve student learning in algebra: Differentiating between correct and incorrect examples. Learning and Instruction, 25, 24-34. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2012.11.002 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Two in vivo experiments, school students.
  - Große, C. S., & Renkl, A. (2007). Finding and fixing errors in worked examples: Can this foster learning outcomes? Learning and Instruction, 17(6), 612-634. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2007.09.008 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Two experiments on probability problems (participant details not in the abstract I read).
  - Barbieri et al. (2023), as in F1.
  - Paas, F. G. W. C., & van Merrienboer, J. J. G. (1994). Variability of worked examples and transfer of geometrical problem-solving skills: A cognitive-load approach. Journal of Educational Psychology, 86(1), 122-133. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.86.1.122 . VERIFIED-ONLINE for existence; its abstract (read in ERIC) confirms only that worked examples supported transfer in 60 Dutch secondary technical school students. That varied examples transfer better is UNVERIFIED-FROM-MEMORY and I do not rely on it.
- Strength: contested for error examples, because the two lines disagree on novices, and the resolution (prior knowledge) is consistent with F4. Limited for example variety, because I could not verify the key claim.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Error examples can crowd out explanations of the principle. I did not verify the age range of the probability-study participants from the abstract.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: The app's faulty-claims drill (errDrill) should come after the learner has met the correct reasoning and has done at least one drill successfully, and should show the fault and the corrected reasoning together. Do not open a unit for a beginner with wrong examples. Whether to vary the surface of examples while keeping the structure constant is plausible, in line with F19, but I do not have a verified experiment for it in this cluster.

## D. Orienting the learner before the new material

### F22. Advance organizers and activating prior knowledge help, mainly by giving the learner something to attach the new material to.
- What the research shows: A meta-analysis of 135 published and unpublished studies found that advance organizers had a facilitative effect on both learning and retention, and examined grade level, subject, presentation mode and ability as possible moderators. A review argued that earlier reviews concluding that organizers do not work had limitations and that organizers can affect learning in suitable circumstances. A broad review of 183 sources found that prior knowledge generally has positive effects on performance, with the size of the effect depending on how performance was assessed.
- Sources:
  - Luiten, J., Ames, W., & Ackerson, G. (1980). A meta-analysis of the effects of advance organizers on learning and retention. American Educational Research Journal, 17(2), 211-218. https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312017002211 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Crossref-linked record). Meta-analysis (old; the abstract gives no effect size).
  - Mayer, R. E. (1979). Can advance organizers influence meaningful learning? Review of Educational Research, 49(2), 371-383. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543049002371 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Review.
  - Hartley, J., & Davies, I. K. (1976). Preinstructional strategies: The role of pretests, behavioral objectives, overviews and advance organizers. Review of Educational Research, 46(2), 239-265. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543046002239 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Review.
  - Dochy, F., Segers, M., & Buehl, M. M. (1999). The relation between assessment practices and outcomes of studies: The case of research on prior knowledge. Review of Educational Research, 69(2), 145-186. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543069002145 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Review.
- Strength: moderate. The meta-analysis is old, the abstract does not give an effect size, and there is a long history of inconsistent findings, so I would expect a modest benefit.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: A well-known earlier review concluded organizers do not help, and the 1979 review exists because that conclusion was challenged. That organizers help low-prior-knowledge learners more is something I recall from this literature but could not verify online; treat it as UNVERIFIED-FROM-MEMORY. Prior knowledge raises performance; my inference, not a tested claim, is that beginners with no hooks may need more of an organizer.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Open each unit with a short organizer that says in plain words what the unit is about, how its parts relate, and what the learner can already use from everyday experience, and that is more general than the details to come. For the seven subjects this probably means starting from a situation the reader has met (a persuasive email, a headline, a price) rather than from the subject's vocabulary. Whether the reader has met such a situation is itself an assumption the lesson should not make without a check.

### F23. Stating objectives helps a little; asking questions about the coming material first helps more, with a conflicting result on feedback.
- What the research shows: In three laboratory experiments, presenting learning objectives before each passage improved later retention of the passage content compared with no objectives; turning the objectives into multiple-choice pretest questions improved it more than reading them as statements; and in the third experiment, giving feedback on the pretest answers lowered final performance. Five experiments with 1,573 participants who studied expository passages found that pretests before studying, with or without feedback, helped memory on tests five minutes and 48 hours later, and pretesting gave higher overall scores than posttesting. An earlier set of five experiments found that failing to answer pretest questions before reading still improved later learning relative to extra study time.
- Sources:
  - Sana, F., Forrin, N. D., Sharma, M., Dubljevic, T., Ho, P., Jalil, E., & Kim, J. A. (2020). Optimizing the efficacy of learning objectives through pretests. CBE Life Sciences Education, 19(3), ar43. https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.19-11-0257 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in ERIC). Three experiments.
  - Pan, S. C., & Sana, F. (2021). Pretesting versus posttesting: Comparing the pedagogical benefits of errorful generation and retrieval practice. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 27(2), 237-257. https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000345 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read from the authors' preprint). Five experiments.
  - Richland, L. E., Kornell, N., & Kao, L. S. (2009). The pretesting effect: Do unsuccessful retrieval attempts enhance learning? Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 15(3), 243-257. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016496 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Crossref-linked record). Five experiments.
  - Hartley & Davies (1976), as in F22, for the older review of behavioural objectives.
- Strength: moderate for the pretest effect on fact retention, limited for objectives. All of these measure retention of facts from expository text passages (participant details are not in the abstracts I read), not skill at classifying.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The two studies disagree on feedback: one found feedback on pretest answers lowered final scores, the other found the pretest advantage held with or without feedback. I would not build on either result alone. None of the retrieved evidence concerns application skills.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: A line stating what the learner will be able to do ("tell these two outcomes apart from a real case") is cheap and low-risk. A couple of ungraded preview questions at the start of the unit, using the real key questions, are better supported than a statement alone, for fact retention; they also double as the F6 "try first" step and as a signal of what the learner already knows (F4).

## E. Using the same words everywhere

### F24. Memory retrieval works best when the cue at the test matches what was learned, which argues for one wording per concept across lesson, key, drills and verdicts.
- What the research shows: Two classic lines of memory research say that what is retrievable depends on the match between how material was encoded and the cue or task at retrieval. The encoding-specificity principle holds that a retrieval cue is effective to the extent that its content was stored with the original event. Transfer-appropriate processing holds that performance depends on the match between the processes used when learning and those needed at test, and was shown with word-recognition tasks where rhyme-based encoding beat meaning-based encoding on a rhyme test.
- Sources:
  - Tulving, E., & Thomson, D. M. (1973). Encoding specificity and retrieval processes in episodic memory. Psychological Review, 80(5), 352-373. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0020071 . VERIFIED-ONLINE for citation (Crossref) and for the principle as a secondary summary of the abstract; I did not read the full paper. Experiments.
  - Morris, C. D., Bransford, J. D., & Franks, J. J. (1977). Levels of processing versus transfer appropriate processing. Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 16(5), 519-533. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(77)80016-9 . VERIFIED-ONLINE for citation (Crossref) and for the finding as reported in secondary summaries. Experiments.
- Strength: limited for this application. The principles are well established in memory research, but these are word-list experiments and I found no experiment that tests whether changing the wording between a lesson and its quiz affects classification learning.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Real learners often understand paraphrases, and understanding the idea is different from matching a cue. A search for direct evidence on terminology consistency between instruction and assessment returned no experimental study, only practitioner guidance.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: This answers the owner's complaint that the lessons and the questionnaire use different words. The research is indirect, but it points the same way as F11 and F16: choose one name for each concept and use it in the card, the key question, every option, the drill item and the verdict. Where two terms must coexist (a jargon term and a plain one), put both in one place with an explicit "also called" and show the learner the mapping once, then pick one for everything that follows. Check the "keeps" options and outcome names against the lesson text so every phrase the learner must recognise at the key has already appeared in the cards.

## Tensions inside this cluster

1. Explain first versus try first. Cognitive load research (F1, F3, F5) and Rosenshine (F8) favour explicit instruction first for novices. Productive-failure research (F6) reports gains for problem-first, and a meta-analysis supports it on average. The literature resolves this partly through moderators: problem-first works when learners can generate something from prior knowledge, when the later instruction explicitly builds on their attempts, when contrasting cases are used, and for conceptual rather than procedural outcomes; instruction-first wins for younger learners, for domain-general skills, and in several direct tests with children (F7). A fair reading for adults who are real beginners on a new subject, studying alone, is explain first, with comparison cases, and with try-first used sparingly and consolidated explicitly. Much of the strongest evidence on each side comes from research groups that advocate that side.

2. More guidance versus expertise reversal. Heavy support helps novices and hurts competent learners (F4). The resolution is to design for the novice by default and let competent learners skip, not to thin the support for everyone. The cost is that a one-size lesson will be partly redundant for some readers; the meta-analytic magnitudes are similar in both directions (about +0.51 and about -0.43), so the case for a novice default rests on the app's audience being novices, not on redundancy being cheap.

3. Less is more versus more is more. The coherence and seductive-detail literature (F13) says to cut material, and Mayer's "less is more" study found summaries matching full text. Rosenshine (F8), the text-cohesion experiments (F16) and the instructional-explanation work (F17) say novices need more explanation, not less. These do not truly conflict: the first removes irrelevant material, the second adds relevant connective material. No study I found sets a limit on how much necessary explanation a lesson can hold; the research puts the limit on how much new material arrives per segment (F10), not on total length.

4. Segmenting helps high-knowledge learners more than novices (F10), yet cognitive load theory predicts novices need segmenting most. The meta-analysis flags it but does not explain it. A plausible, unverified reading is that segmenting alone does not supply missing knowledge; pre-training (F11) and complete worked cases (F1) do. Treat segmenting as necessary but insufficient.

5. Self-explanation prompts are strongly supported in general (F18), but the worked-examples meta-analysis in F1 found a smaller advantage when prompts were added. Possible reasons include added time or load; the literature does not resolve it. Use short, answerable prompts with immediate feedback rather than open-ended ones.

6. Error examples. They improved conceptual understanding in algebra tutoring but hurt or did nothing for low-prior-knowledge learners in probability, and the worked-examples meta-analysis favoured correct examples (F21). The resolution is prior knowledge, again the F4 interaction: sequence errors late.

7. Pretest feedback. One study found feedback on pretest answers lowered final performance, another found the pretest advantage held with and without feedback (F23). Unresolved.

8. How far to trust the framework. Cognitive load theory underlies many of these findings. Its founder acknowledges that replication failures have been part of its history and argues they were resolved by refining the theory (Sweller, 2023, Educational Psychology Review, 35, https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09817-2, VERIFIED-ONLINE, abstract read in ERIC); an independent critique raises open conceptual, methodological and application issues (de Jong, 2010, Instructional Science, 38, 105-134, https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-009-9110-0, VERIFIED-ONLINE, abstract read in ERIC). The practical findings above (worked examples, segmenting, signalling, expertise reversal) are backed by meta-analyses that stand without the theory, so I treat the framework as an explanation, not as the evidence.

## What the research does not tell us

- Whether any of this holds for a self-paced, phone-based, card-by-card lesson for adults learning to classify real cases. The nearest studies are classroom or laboratory studies with children, secondary students or undergraduates, dominated by mathematics and physics; the closest adult online study I found (a synchronous remote physics class with an instructor) still had a live teacher (DeCaro, Isaacs, Bego, & Chastain, 2023, Frontiers in Education, 8, https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2023.1215975, VERIFIED-ONLINE via publisher page, 78 undergraduates).
- How long an explanation should be. There is no finding that gives an optimal or maximum length for a card or a unit. The evidence supports controlling the amount of new material per segment, and supports adding connective explanation for novices; it does not support a word budget.
- How much to fade, and when. Fading studies adapt to learners' prior knowledge or use fixed schedules in short experiments; no evidence tells us the right ramp for a seven-subject app with no per-learner tracking.
- Whether using one consistent wording across lesson and assessment improves classification learning. The memory-research mechanisms point that way (F24), but I found no direct experiment.
- Long-term effects. Most of the studies above test learning minutes to a few days later. Effects at weeks or months, and the effect on real-world diagnostic use, are mostly untested.
- Which kind of prompt or contrast works best on a phone, where typing is expensive and attention is fragmented. The prompt and comparison literature does not address this.
- Whether the productive-failure advantage appears for adult beginners in a new subject with no teacher; the meta-analysis does not isolate this group.
