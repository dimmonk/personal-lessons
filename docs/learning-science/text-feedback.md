# Explanatory text, feedback and length for novices: research notes for Fieldcraft

Cluster: how explanations should be written and how long they should be, and what feedback should say (explanatory text for novices, cohesion and terminology, elaboration versus brevity, plain language and jargon, examples and analogies, feedback content and timing, mobile delivery).
Researcher notes for the synthesizer. Written 2026-10-04. Scope: adults doing self-study on a phone, learning a diagnostic-classification skill (read an unlabelled case, answer a short key, name the outcome, with name and route scored separately).

## Short answer to the cluster question

**How should an explanation be written?** For a novice, the evidence favours explanation that states every link in the reasoning (what the thing is, why it matters, how to recognise it, how it differs from its nearest neighbour) over terse text that leaves the reader to infer the links (F1, F2, F3). It supports one name per concept, defined where it is first needed and then repeated rather than swapped for synonyms (F4, F5). It supports concrete cases and carefully built examples and near-misses next to a definition (F12), and analogies only with an explicit mapping and an explicit limit (F13). And it warns that authors who already understand the topic cannot reliably see which steps they skipped (F6), and that a smooth-reading card can feel clear without having taught (F25). The determination key is a procedure, so each key question needs its rationale in the lessons, because steps alone help initial performance while principles help learning and transfer (F24). The best-known pro-brevity result, minimalist software manuals, was short but task-first and rich in error recovery, and does not support thin explanation of a conceptual skill (F23).

**How long?** No study sets a length. What the literature actually supports is a distinction between three things that "short" lessons tend to blur: (1) cutting material that is irrelevant to the learning goal, which helps (F7); (2) cutting material that is relevant, such as connective reasoning, definitions and examples, which does not help novices and often hurts (F1, F2, F3, F8); and (3) cutting the amount shown at once, that is, small learner-paced segments, which helps (F10). So the unit to keep small is the chunk on screen, not the total teaching. There is no research basis for a ceiling on how much a unit may explain.

**What should feedback say?** After a wrong answer: the right answer, the reason in the lesson's own words, and why the chosen option does not fit, most important sentence first (F14, F15, F20). After a right answer: a short reason, because correct answers reached with low confidence or by the wrong route are hidden errors (F18). Keep it about the task and the reasoning, not about the person (F16). Immediate for new, difficult material; leave "delay" to a later review (F17). Confident errors are the best teaching moments and need to come back later (F19). Feedback depth should shrink as the learner becomes competent (F21).

## A. Writing the explanation

### F1. Novices understand more from text that spells out the connections between ideas than from terse text that leaves the reader to infer them, and only readers who already know the domain can afford the terse version.
- What the research shows: A line of experiments by McNamara, Kintsch and colleagues compared versions of the same passage that differed in how explicitly the relations between ideas were stated. In junior-high students reading a biology text (comprehension measured by free recall, written questions and a key-word sorting task), the authors report advantages for globally coherent text and for more explanatory text. In a second experiment that varied local and global coherence separately, low-knowledge readers did better with the coherent text and high-knowledge readers did better with the minimally coherent one; the authors argue that gaps forced the knowledgeable readers to infer the missing relations. That advantage for the gappy text showed up only on measures of deep ("situation model") understanding, not on recall or text-based questions. A history-text study found the same pattern, with the high-coherence text better on recall and multiple choice and the high-knowledge readers doing better on open-ended questions after the low-coherence text, and little effect of a one-week delay. Reading the low-coherence text first helped high-knowledge readers; low-knowledge readers benefited from high coherence whatever the order. Outside this lab group, a principled repair of the places where a text called for an unstated inference produced a version that conveyed the author's intentions better than the original in 170 undergraduates and 125 Air Force recruits, revising fifth-grade social-studies text from a text-processing perspective raised recall and question scores, and repairing causal links helped both more- and less-skilled readers on a difficult history text but not on an easy one.
- Sources:
  - McNamara, D. S., Kintsch, E., Songer, N. B., & Kintsch, W. (1996). Are good texts always better? Interactions of text coherence, background knowledge, and levels of understanding in learning from text. Cognition and Instruction, 14(1), 1-43. https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1401_1 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Two experiments.
  - McNamara, D. S., & Kintsch, W. (1996). Learning from texts: Effects of prior knowledge and text coherence. Discourse Processes, 22(3), 247-288. https://doi.org/10.1080/01638539609544975 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Two experiments (population not stated in the abstract).
  - McNamara, D. S. (2001). Reading both high-coherence and low-coherence texts: Effects of text sequence and prior knowledge. Canadian Journal of Experimental Psychology, 55(1), 51-62. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0087352 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Experiment.
  - Britton, B. K., & Gülgöz, S. (1991). Using Kintsch's computational model to improve instructional text: Effects of repairing inference calls on recall and cognitive structures. Journal of Educational Psychology, 83(3), 329-345. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.83.3.329 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, ERIC version). Two experiments.
  - Beck, I. L., McKeown, M. G., Sinatra, G. M., & Loxterman, J. A. (1991). Revising social studies text from a text-processing perspective: Evidence of improved comprehensibility. Reading Research Quarterly, 26(3), 251-276. https://doi.org/10.2307/747763 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, ERIC version). Experiment, fifth graders.
  - Linderholm, T., Everson, M. G., van den Broek, P., Mischinski, M., Crittenden, A., & Samuels, J. (2000). Effects of causal text revisions on more- and less-skilled readers' comprehension of easy and difficult texts. Cognition and Instruction, 18(4), 525-556. https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1804_4 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Experiment.
- Strength: strong for the claim that low-knowledge readers gain from explicit, coherent text (many experiments over three decades, different texts and populations, same direction). Moderate for the knowledge-by-coherence interaction, which later work shows is not simple (below).
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence:
  - The "reverse cohesion" benefit of gappy text is narrower than first thought. In 143 college students it was restricted to readers who had high knowledge but weaker comprehension skill; skilled high-knowledge readers did better with the high-cohesion text. For low-knowledge readers, high cohesion helped inferential (bridging) questions but not text-based questions. A biology study found the benefit of high cohesion depended on reading skill, with skilled readers gaining more.
  - The gap benefit is measured on deeper questions only, and only after the reader fills the gap successfully, which is more likely for people who already know the domain.
  - Linderholm et al.: repairs did nothing for the easy text. Benefits show up when the text is hard for the reader.
  - Almost all of this is short expository passages read once, tested within minutes to a week, with school students or undergraduates. Not adults with low literacy, not multi-session self-study.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Every subject is new to the learner, so lesson cards should be the high-coherence version: state each relation in words (because, so, which means, unlike, instead), say why each step is taken, and make every "this" and "it" unmistakable. Do not trim the connective reasoning to save words. Leaving gaps for the learner to fill is a technique for people who already know the material; in this app the right place for inference practice is the drills and the key, where the app can check the answer, not the lesson prose.
- Additional sources used above (same tags): Ozuru, Y., Dempsey, K., & McNamara, D. S. (2009). Prior knowledge, reading skill, and text cohesion in the comprehension of science texts. Learning and Instruction, 19(3), 228-242. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2008.04.003 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, ERIC). Experiment, college students. O'Reilly, T., & McNamara, D. S. (2007). Reversing the reverse cohesion effect: Good texts can be better for strategic, high-knowledge readers. Discourse Processes, 43(2), 121-152. https://doi.org/10.1080/01638530709336895 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Experiment, n = 143.

### F2. A 2023 meta-analysis of text rewriting in STEM found only a small average benefit, concentrated in clarity, elaboration and personalization, with low-knowledge learners gaining most; simplifying text and adding cohesion cues did not reliably help.
- What the research shows: The meta-analysis pooled 45 experimental studies (6,477 learners) that rewrote STEM texts to be easier. The average effect of the rewrites was small (g = 0.15). Personalization and increases in clarity and elaboration had positive effects. Reducing complexity and increasing cohesion did not have significant effects. Learners with low prior content knowledge benefited more than learners with high prior knowledge. A separate experiment on fourth graders reached a similar conclusion in its own terms: simply adding cohesion cues, without adding explanatory information, is unlikely to be enough.
- Sources:
  - Strohmaier, A. R., Ehmke, T., Härtig, H., & Leiß, D. (2023). On the role of linguistic features for comprehension and learning from STEM texts: A meta-analysis. Educational Research Review, 39, 100533. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2023.100533 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (citation in Crossref; findings from the institutional repository record of the abstract and a second search summary, because the publisher page was blocked). Meta-analysis.
  - McNamara, D. S., Ozuru, Y., & Floyd, R. G. (2011). Comprehension challenges in the fourth grade: The roles of text cohesion, text genre, and readers' prior knowledge. International Electronic Journal of Elementary Education, 4(1), 229-257. https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1070456.pdf . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, ERIC). Experiment, children.
- Strength: moderate. One meta-analysis, a small average effect, many kinds of "linguistic modification" pooled, abstract-level reading only (I did not retrieve the moderator tables).
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: STEM texts only; I could not confirm the age mix of the included studies. The null for "increasing cohesion" sits uneasily next to F1, which shows benefits for repaired coherence. The likeliest reconciliation is that "cohesion" in rewrites ranged from cheap surface cues (repeating words, adding connectives) to true repair of missing explanatory links, and only the latter reliably helps; the fourth-grade study points the same way, but this is my reading, not something the meta-analysis tested.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Rewriting a card to be "simpler" by cutting is the least supported move. What is supported is adding the missing explanation (clarity, elaboration), writing to the reader (personalization, for example direct "you" address in a case), and giving the most help to the least knowledgeable reader, which is the target reader of Unit 1. Do not treat sprinkling connective words over unchanged content as a repair.

### F3. Readability formulas and "short sentences, common words" are unreliable guides for novice lessons; rewrites that chase a formula can delete the logic and make the text harder.
- What the research shows: A case study of four texts compared the originals (for adults) with adaptations for less skilled readers. Some adaptations, such as splitting complex sentences into clauses and swapping vocabulary, appeared to be driven by readability formulas and were not always successful, and some made the text harder to understand. The adaptations that worked best came from writers who attended to the discourse topic, the logical order of ideas, the background knowledge the reader needs, and syntax choices that do not change length, not from writers fitting a formula. A computational validation of Coh-Metrix found that widely used readability indexes (for example Flesch-Kincaid) failed to separate the high-cohesion from the low-cohesion versions of texts taken from published discourse-psychology experiments, while cohesion measures did. The Coh-Metrix developers describe five factors that carry most of the variation in text difficulty: word concreteness, syntactic simplicity, referential cohesion, causal cohesion and narrativity. On the other side, a randomized trial with 488 adults found that a plain-language redesign of a health recommendation raised the proportion of comprehension questions answered correctly by about 20 percentage points for one of the two recommendations tested.
- Sources:
  - Davison, A., & Kantor, R. N. (1982). On the failure of readability formulas to define readable texts: A case study from adaptations. Reading Research Quarterly, 17(2), 187-209. https://doi.org/10.2307/747483 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Qualitative text comparison.
  - McNamara, D. S., Louwerse, M. M., McCarthy, P. M., & Graesser, A. C. (2010). Coh-Metrix: Capturing linguistic features of cohesion. Discourse Processes, 47(4), 292-330. https://doi.org/10.1080/01638530902959943 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Validation study.
  - Graesser, A. C., McNamara, D. S., & Kulikowich, J. M. (2011). Coh-Metrix: Providing multilevel analyses of text characteristics. Educational Researcher, 40(5), 223-234. https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189x11413260 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Review.
  - Sayfi, S., Charide, R., Elliott, S. A., et al. (2024). A multimethods randomized trial found that plain language versions improved adults' understanding of health recommendations. Journal of Clinical Epidemiology, 165, 111219. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2023.11.009 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, Europe PMC; the figure is the mean difference for the WHO recommendation, the rest of the results sentence was cut off in the retrieved abstract). Randomized trial. A companion trial with 295 parents (Elliott et al., 2023, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2023.06.018) also found significantly higher understanding scores in the plain-language group (P = 0.014). VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract).
- Strength: moderate. Davison and Kantor is a small qualitative case study from 1982, and the Coh-Metrix result is about whether a measure discriminates text versions, not about learner outcomes. The plain-language trials are real randomized evidence but on health recommendations with an online sample.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The plain-language redesign in the trial was a whole reformatting done with patient partners, not word substitution, so it does not show that shorter sentences or simpler words alone help. I found no experiment isolating sentence length for adult novices; "keep sentences under N words" is a rule of thumb here, not a finding. A formula is probably fine as a crude screen for extreme outliers, but that use is untested in what I retrieved.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Do not make a grade-level score the quality gate for a card. Gate on: every technical word is either defined or avoided; every inferential step is written down; a real novice can restate the card. A long sentence that carries a because-chain is better than two short sentences with the "because" deleted. Plain language in the sense of the trials (organised around what the reader needs to do or decide, written with readers) is supported; "plain" as a sentence-length count is not.

### F4. Using one name for one concept, and repeating it instead of swapping synonyms, is supported by one small direct experiment, word-memory work and sound indirect reasoning; direct evidence for multi-part instruction (lesson plus key plus quiz) is thin.
- What the research shows: In an experiment that measured reading time, text appreciation and persuasiveness, replacing repeated words with synonyms made the text seem more attractive but less comprehensible and slower to read, more so when readers skimmed and when the synonyms were weaker. In a word-memory study, a synonym in the same language was a weaker aid to recall than exact repetition of the word. Coh-Metrix treats referential cohesion, the degree to which sentences refer back to the same things, as one of the five main factors in text difficulty. A classic memory result says that what is remembered depends on whether the processing at study matches the demands of the test, which is the general reason to teach and test in the same terms. For novices, a separate result bears on why variation hurts: novices categorise problems by their literal surface features, while experts categorise by underlying principle, so a novice who meets a different word for the same idea may not see it as the same idea.
- Sources:
  - Oversteegen, L., & van Wijk, C. (2003). Lexical alternation versus word repetition: The effects of synonyms on reading time, text appreciation, and persuasiveness. Document Design, 4(2), 150-167. https://doi.org/10.1075/dd.4.2.05ove . VERIFIED-ONLINE (citation; findings secondary: a search-tool summary of the abstract, because the publisher and ResearchGate pages were blocked). Experiment(s), participants not retrieved.
  - Kolers, P. A., & Gonzalez, E. (1980). Memory for words, synonyms, and translations. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Learning and Memory, 6(1), 53-65. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.6.1.53 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, ERIC). Experiments, word memory (population not stated in the abstract).
  - Graesser, McNamara, & Kulikowich (2011), see F3.
  - Morris, C. D., Bransford, J. D., & Franks, J. J. (1977). Levels of processing versus transfer appropriate processing. Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 16(5), 519-533. https://doi.org/10.1016/s0022-5371(77)80016-9 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, ERIC; it shows only that the best study-time processing depended on the type of test, which is the principle I lean on, not a result about terminology).
  - Chi, M. T. H., Feltovich, P. J., & Glaser, R. (1981). Categorization and representation of physics problems by experts and novices. Cognitive Science, 5(2), 121-152. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0502_2 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Four experiments, physics, experts versus novices.
- Strength: limited as direct evidence (one small experiment I could only read through a summary, a word-memory study, and theory). The practical rule is still low-risk because consistency costs nothing and the failure it prevents is exactly what the owner reported.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Synonym variation made texts more attractive, so there is a style cost to consistency, which does not matter for teaching. I could not retrieve the synonym experiment's materials or participants; none of the sources test technical terminology across a lesson, a questionnaire and a feedback message. A practitioner write-up on technical documentation (Pierrot, 2017, a guest post at https://idratherbewriting.com/2017/03/10/effects-of-terminology-consistency-guest-post/ , page read; a blog post, not research, listed only because it states the gap) says outright that its author could not find cognitive experiments on lexicon consistency for large bodies of technical content. Since the real world will use many words for one thing, the end goal is that the learner recognises the variants, which means variants have to be taught, not left to chance.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: This is the owner's "different language to explain and in the questionnaire" complaint. Fix one name per outcome and per diagnostic concept, and use it verbatim in: the lesson card where it is introduced, the key question and option text, the drill stems and options, the verdict text and the feedback. Do not vary it for style. Where a real-world case uses another word (for example a different word for the same scam type), teach the equivalence explicitly in the card ("also called ...") and again at the point of use (in the key or specimen feedback: "the case says X; that is the lesson's Y"). Specimen text may use varied wording on purpose, because transfer needs it, but the feedback should always map the varied wording back to the fixed term. A simple automated check (every term used in a key or drill must appear in a lesson card, and vice versa) would turn this from a convention into something the build can enforce; that is an implementation idea, not a research finding.

### F5. Define the technical terms the learner must use at the point they are first needed, in plain words with a context, and reuse them; but minimise incidental jargon, because a defined jargon word still slows reading and dents the feeling of understanding.
- What the research shows: A meta-analysis of vocabulary instruction in children found large effects on comprehension of passages containing the taught words (mean effect size 0.97) and a much smaller effect on global comprehension measures (0.30). The most effective methods combined definitional information with contextual information, engaged the learner in deeper processing, and gave more than one or two exposures. An experiment with 650 adults, reported in two papers, found that jargon impaired people's ability to process scientific information fluently, even when definitions for the jargon terms were provided, and that it affected their identification with the science community and, through that, their reported interest and perceived understanding; the same experiment linked the impaired processing to more resistance to persuasion and higher risk perceptions about an emerging technology.
- Sources:
  - Stahl, S. A., & Fairbanks, M. M. (1986). The effects of vocabulary instruction: A model-based meta-analysis. Review of Educational Research, 56(1), 72-110. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543056001072 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Meta-analysis, children.
  - Shulman, H. C., Dixon, G. N., Bullock, O. M., & Colón Amill, D. (2020). The effects of jargon on processing fluency, self-perceptions, and scientific engagement. Journal of Language and Social Psychology, 39(5-6), 579-597. https://doi.org/10.1177/0261927x20902177 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Experiment, N = 650 (same N as the next paper, likely the same dataset).
  - Bullock, O. M., Colón Amill, D., Shulman, H. C., & Dixon, G. N. (2019). Jargon as a barrier to effective science communication: Evidence from metacognition. Public Understanding of Science, 28(7), 845-853. https://doi.org/10.1177/0963662519865687 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, Europe PMC). Experiment, N = 650 (same N as the previous paper, likely the same dataset).
- Strength: moderate for "definition plus context plus repeated exposure" (a meta-analysis, though from 1986 and with children); limited for the jargon-fluency effect (online adult samples, one exposure to science-news-style items, outcomes were engagement and perceived understanding, not learning).
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: In this app the technical terms are the learning target (the outcome names and the diagnostic vocabulary), so they cannot be removed, which is not the situation in the jargon experiments. The vocabulary meta-analysis found only a small effect on general comprehension (0.30), so defining a word does not by itself make a passage easier to read; its big effect is on the taught words.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Split vocabulary into two lists. Target terms (outcome names, diagnostic features): define in everyday words in the card where first needed, give a concrete context, use again in later cards and in key and drill text. Incidental terms (anything the classification does not need): replace with ordinary words or cut, and spell out abbreviations. Because defined jargon still costs fluency, the cheaper the vocabulary list, the better; introduce target terms one or two at a time rather than a glossary dump at the start of a unit.

### F6. People who know a topic misjudge how hard it is for novices and skip steps they no longer notice, so a lesson written by an expert, or by a system that "knows" the topic, must be tested on someone who does not.
- What the research shows: In two studies, people with more expertise were worse at predicting how long novices would take on a complex task, and debiasing techniques meant to correct this did not help; people with intermediate expertise were more accurate. In a study of 48 preservice mathematics teachers, those with more advanced mathematics education were more likely to judge symbolic reasoning to be a prerequisite for solving word problems, contrary to how students actually performed, which the authors call "expert blind spot".
- Sources:
  - Hinds, P. J. (1999). The curse of expertise: The effects of expertise and debiasing methods on prediction of novice performance. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 5(2), 205-221. https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898x.5.2.205 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Two experiments.
  - Nathan, M. J., & Petrosino, A. J. (2003). Expert blind spot among preservice teachers. American Educational Research Journal, 40(4), 905-928. https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312040004905 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). N = 48.
- Strength: strong that the bias exists in judgments of novice difficulty; limited evidence on how to fix it (Hinds found debiasing did not work in her studies).
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Both studies measure predictions of novice performance, not the quality of text an expert writes. Applying the result to lesson drafting is an inference, though a close one. Intermediate users predicted best, which suggests the reviewer should be someone who learned the material recently, not the subject-matter expert.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: The owner's complaint ("it doesn't breed understanding") is the typical symptom. A unit should not count as finished until a person with no prior knowledge has read it cold and can say, in their own words, what the previous card said before moving on, and has attempted the drill without outside help. Reviews by the author are the least informative check. Where that is not possible, a think-aloud by a near-novice (someone who learned the subject recently) is the next best.

## B. How much to say

### F7. Removing interesting-but-irrelevant material improves learning; that is a rule about relevance to the learning goal, not a rule about being short.
- What the research shows: In four experiments with 357 undergraduates, readers of expository passages with interesting but irrelevant added details remembered fewer main ideas and produced fewer problem-solving transfer solutions; the authors suggest the details prime schemas that organise the passage around the wrong ideas. An earlier study found the same harm to recall of main ideas in 20 adults, and in seventh graders. Two meta-analyses converge: Rey (2012; 39 experimental effects) found a significant seductive-detail effect, small to medium for retention and medium for transfer, and Sundararajan and Adesope (2020) concluded that including seductive details can hinder learning, with effect sizes moderated by image type, delivery format, subject, learner pacing and other design features; secondary reports of that paper give a small-to-moderate overall effect (about g = -0.33) across 58 studies and more than 7,500 students, with larger harm for static visual details and in paper-based formats. A 2021 experiment with 194 students in a long chemistry unit found harm for students who were not told that the details were irrelevant, including late in the session, mediated by reduced positive affect.
- Sources:
  - Harp, S. F., & Mayer, R. E. (1998). How seductive details do their damage: A theory of cognitive interest in science learning. Journal of Educational Psychology, 90(3), 414-434. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.90.3.414 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, ERIC). Four experiments.
  - Garner, R., Gillingham, M. G., & White, C. S. (1989). Effects of "seductive details" on macroprocessing and microprocessing in adults and children. Cognition and Instruction, 6(1), 41-57. https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0601_2 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Two studies.
  - Rey, G. D. (2012). A review of research and a meta-analysis of the seductive detail effect. Educational Research Review, 7(3), 216-237. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2012.05.003 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, ERIC). Review and meta-analysis. Its abstract also notes that the data support, but also call into question, each of the four proposed explanations.
  - Sundararajan, N., & Adesope, O. (2020). Keep it coherent: A meta-analysis of the seductive details effect. Educational Psychology Review, 32(3), 707-734. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, ERIC, for the direction and the moderators; the count of 58 studies and the g value come from secondary reports, a university news release and a search summary, because the abstract gives no numbers). Meta-analysis.
  - Bender, L., Renkl, A., & Eitel, A. (2021). Seductive details do their damage also in longer learning sessions: When the details are perceived as relevant. Journal of Computer Assisted Learning, 37(5), 1248-1262. https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12560 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, ERIC). Experiment, N = 194.
- Strength: strong that irrelevant interesting material harms recall and transfer (two meta-analyses plus many experiments); the Sundararajan and Adesope effect size is secondary-sourced.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: "Seductive details" means interesting content that is irrelevant to the learning goal. Whether a detail counts depends on the learning goal: an example, a worked case or a definition that serves the goal is not one. The studies measure recall and transfer on short or medium lessons, not whether learners keep going; an engaging aside might keep someone studying. The 2021 study suggests that telling learners an aside is irrelevant changes its effect, though its abstract reports the harm for the uninformed group and does not spell out the informed group's result, so I do not claim the aside is then harmless.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Give every sentence a job: define, explain why or how, connect to an earlier card or to the key, illustrate, or separate from the nearest neighbour. Colourful history, trivia, name-dropping and tangents that do not help with the diagnosis are the cut candidates, regardless of how few words that saves. Relevant elaboration is not a candidate for cutting. If an aside is kept for interest, mark it as optional so the learner can skip it.

### F8. "Summaries beat full text" results are real, but they are narrow, and they do not show that thin explanations are better for novices who must learn a skill.
- What the research shows: Reder and Anderson found an advantage for studying summaries of textbook chapters over reading the original prose, and a follow-up (1982) tested two explanations: that summaries allow spaced rereading of main points, and that details distract attention from the main points. In the other direction, when learners had to acquire a skill (using a personal computer) from an instructional text, elaborations written by the author and elaborations generated by learners each improved skill performance, in contrast to earlier declarative-memory experiments in which author-written elaborations had hurt; the elaborations that illustrated command syntax helped both novice and experienced users. A set of three experiments by Mayer and colleagues found that a short summary of captions with simple illustrations of the main steps in how lightning forms led to recall and transfer as good as or better than the full text; removing the illustrations or the captions removed the advantage, and adding text to the summary reduced it. The authors draw the lesson that conciseness has to come with coherence and coordination between words and pictures. In an older sentence-memory line of work, elaborations that were precise and relevant helped memory while irrelevant ones hurt it.
- Sources:
  - Reder, L. M., & Anderson, J. R. (1980). A comparison of texts and their summaries: Memorial consequences. Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 19(2), 121-134. https://doi.org/10.1016/s0022-5371(80)90122-x . VERIFIED-ONLINE (citation; findings secondary: the CMU lab publication list and a search summary of how later work describes the result; I could not retrieve its abstract). Experiments.
  - Reder, L. M., & Anderson, J. R. (1982). Effects of spacing and embellishment on memory for the main points of a text. Memory & Cognition, 10(2), 97-102. https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03209210 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (citation; findings secondary: a search summary; abstract not retrievable). Three studies.
  - Reder, L. M., Charney, D. H., & Morgan, K. I. (1986). The role of elaborations in learning a skill from an instructional text. Memory & Cognition, 14(1), 64-78. https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03209230 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (citation; findings secondary: a search summary of the abstract as reproduced in a technical-report listing; publisher abstract not retrievable). Two experiments.
  - Mayer, R. E., Bove, W., Bryman, A., Mars, R., & Tapangco, L. (1996). When less is more: Meaningful learning from visual and verbal summaries of science textbook lessons. Journal of Educational Psychology, 88(1), 64-73. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.88.1.64 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Three experiments, college students.
  - Stein, B. S., Morris, C. D., & Bransford, J. D. (1978). Constraints on effective elaboration. Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 17(6), 707-714. https://doi.org/10.1016/s0022-5371(78)90423-1 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (citation; findings secondary: a book-style summary of the design, since the abstract was not retrievable; I rely only on the direction, precise versus irrelevant elaboration). Experiments with sentences.
- Strength: contested. The summary advantage is old, measured on memory for main points, and the explanation is unsettled; elaboration results run both ways depending on the kind of elaboration and the kind of learning.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Declarative memory for main points is not the same as being able to classify a new case. The Mayer summary was a text-plus-pictures sequence about one cause-and-effect chain, tested right after reading, in college students. In the Reder skill study the elaborations were relevant to the actual task. Across these papers the dividing line is relevance and precision of the added material (also F7), not word count.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Keep the teaching text complete, and add a short recap at the end of each unit ("what to carry into the drill") written in exactly the wording the key and drill use. The recap is an addition that supports main-point retention, not a replacement for the explanation. That recap idea is my inference from the summary results; no source tested it in a classification course.

### F9. How much explanation is right depends on what the learner already knows: material that is essential to a novice can be redundant, and slow or even hurt, a more experienced learner (expertise reversal).
- What the research shows: A review of the literature on the interaction between instructional techniques and learner experience concludes that techniques that are highly effective for inexperienced learners can lose their advantage, and even have negative effects, for experienced ones. The explanation offered is working-memory load: schemas in long-term memory let experienced learners process information efficiently, so extra explanation that helps a novice becomes redundant processing for them. McNamara's reverse-cohesion work (F1) and the segmenting meta-analysis (F10) show the same dependence on knowledge from other angles. See `instruction.md` F4 for the fuller account.
- Sources:
  - Kalyuga, S., Ayres, P., Chandler, P., & Sweller, J. (2003). The expertise reversal effect. Educational Psychologist, 38(1), 23-31. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep3801_4 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Review.
- Strength: strong as a general pattern (a review of many experiments), mostly from mathematics, engineering and technical training, with experts defined within a task domain.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Expertise is a continuum and changes within a session; the review does not say where to switch. The segmenting meta-analysis found, surprisingly, that high-prior-knowledge learners benefited more from segmentation in retention, so "small chunks are for novices" is too simple.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Full explanation belongs in the lesson cards, where learners are novices. Later encounters (the Mixed drill, repeat specimens) can carry shorter explanations that point back to the card's wording, ideally with an optional expand for the full reasoning. Treat this as a design inference: nothing here tested it on this app's learners.

### F10. Chunk the material into learner-paced segments and page rather than scroll; the total amount of content can be as large as the explanation needs.
- What the research shows: A meta-analysis of 56 investigations (88 comparisons) found a significant segmenting effect, small to medium for retention and transfer: people learn better when multimedia instruction comes in meaningful learner-paced segments than as one continuous unit; segmentation reduced overall cognitive load and increased learning time. Learners with high prior knowledge gained more in retention than those with low prior knowledge. In an experiment with Web-page text, a scrolling format reduced understanding of complex topics, especially for readers with lower working-memory capacity. Two meta-analyses of reading from paper versus screens found a screen disadvantage for comprehension (g = -0.21 across 54 studies and 171,055 participants; g = -0.25 across 33 studies), concentrated in informational or expository text (g = -0.32 in the second), larger under time pressure, and with no reliable difference in reading time in the second.
- Sources:
  - Rey, G. D., Beege, M., Nebel, S., Wirzberger, M., Schmitt, T. H., & Schneider, S. (2019). A meta-analysis of the segmenting effect. Educational Psychology Review, 31(2), 389-419. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, ERIC). Meta-analysis, mostly multimedia learning.
  - Sanchez, C. A., & Wiley, J. (2009). To scroll or not to scroll: Scrolling, working memory capacity, and comprehending complex texts. Human Factors, 51(5), 730-738. https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720809352788 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, Europe PMC). Two experiments.
  - Delgado, P., Vargas, C., Ackerman, R., & Salmerón, L. (2018). Don't throw away your printed books: A meta-analysis on the effects of reading media on reading comprehension. Educational Research Review, 25, 23-38. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2018.09.003 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Meta-analysis.
  - Clinton, V. (2019). Reading from paper compared to screens: A systematic review and meta-analysis. Journal of Research in Reading, 42(2), 288-325. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9817.12269 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract; the retrieved abstract was cut off before its last sentence). Meta-analysis.
- Strength: moderate for segmenting, which rests on a meta-analysis; limited for phone-specific claims.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Segmenting evidence is mostly from multimedia instruction, not plain text cards. The screen-reading disadvantage is smaller when reading is self-paced (Delgado) and the experiments are not phone studies; Sanchez and Wiley used Web pages on a desktop. I saw a search summary of a phone-versus-laptop comparison that reported lower comprehension on phones, and another study with no learning difference by screen size, but I could not verify either, so I do not rely on them.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: The app's own structure (one card at a time, self-paced) is the right shape. Put one idea in each card, and when an explanation needs more room, add another card at an idea boundary rather than a longer scrolling card. No source gives a word count per card; "fits a phone screen without scrolling" is a defensible working rule from the scrolling result, not a validated threshold. No timers or auto-advance, since the paper-screen gap grows under time pressure.

### F11. Headings and overviews help readers hold on to a text's structure when that structure is complex, they do nothing when it is simple, and signaling only some of the content hurts the rest.
- What the research shows: In three experiments with college students, headings, overviews and summaries had no effect on free recall when the text's topic structure was simple; with a complex structure they changed what was recalled and how it was organised. When only half the topics were signaled, the unsignaled topics were recalled worse than in a text with no signals at all. A headings study found that signaled topics were more likely to appear in readers' summaries, more so when only half the topics were signaled, and unsignaled topics less likely than in a text without headings (college students and sixth and eighth graders). A meta-analysis of signaling in multimedia learning (44 effect sizes from 29 experiments, 2,726 participants) found a positive effect on learning outcomes (d = 0.38) that varied with study features.
- Sources:
  - Lorch, R. F., Jr., & Lorch, E. P. (1996). Effects of organizational signals on free recall of expository text. Journal of Educational Psychology, 88(1), 38-48. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.88.1.38 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Three experiments.
  - Lorch, R. F., Jr., Lorch, E. P., Ritchey, K., McGovern, L., & Coleman, D. (2001). Effects of headings on text summarization. Contemporary Educational Psychology, 26(2), 171-191. https://doi.org/10.1006/ceps.1999.1037 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, Europe PMC). Experiments.
  - Alpizar, D., Adesope, O. O., & Wong, R. M. (2020). A meta-analysis of signaling principle in multimedia learning environments. Educational Technology Research and Development, 68, 2095-2119. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11423-020-09748-7 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, ERIC). Meta-analysis.
- Strength: moderate. Replicated by the same lab and a meta-analysis, but effects are on recall and summary content, not on diagnostic skill.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Signaling directs attention toward signaled content and away from unsignaled content. The meta-analysis is on multimedia signals (arrows, colours), only loosely related to headings in text. See `instruction.md` F12.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Give every card a heading that names the idea in the same term used later in the key and drills, and keep a parallel layout across cards. Do not signal only the "important" content: anything that is taught and later tested should be signalled in the same way, or learners will under-attend to it.

## C. Examples and analogies

### F12. A definition alone is a weak way to teach a category; add examples chosen to show the defining features, plus near-misses, arranged on purpose, and expect concrete versus abstract to be a trade-off.
- What the research shows: A review of research on concept teaching proposes a four-step design: first determine the taxonomic structure of the content; second, write the definition in terms of the critical attributes and select examples on the basis of both critical and variable attributes; third, arrange the examples in rational sets by manipulating the attributes; fourth, order the sets by how much the examples differ from one another and by difficulty. A meta-analysis of 57 experiments (336 tests) found that comparing cases beat studying them one at a time, studying a single case, or traditional instruction (d = 0.50); asking learners to find similarities, giving the principle after the comparison, and perceptual content were associated with larger effects. A systematic review of "concreteness fading" recommends starting with concrete materials and fading explicitly to abstract ones, to get the benefits of both. Against that, a study with undergraduates found a transfer advantage for learning mathematics through one abstract representation rather than several concrete examples. In text, four experiments with 221 college students found that concreteness (ease of imagery) was the variable most strongly related to comprehensibility and recall, and a follow-up across four text types reported that concrete texts were recalled better than abstract ones, with the size of the advantage varying by text type.
- Sources:
  - Tennyson, R. D., & Park, O. (1980). The teaching of concepts: A review of instructional design research literature. Review of Educational Research, 50(1), 55-70. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543050001055 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Review.
  - Alfieri, L., Nokes-Malach, T. J., & Schunn, C. D. (2013). Learning through case comparisons: A meta-analytic review. Educational Psychologist, 48(2), 87-113. https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2013.775712 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Meta-analysis.
  - Fyfe, E. R., McNeil, N. M., Son, J. Y., & Goldstone, R. L. (2014). Concreteness fading in mathematics and science instruction: A systematic review. Educational Psychology Review, 26(1), 9-25. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-014-9249-3 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, ERIC). Systematic review. Its argument is partly theoretical: it lists four theoretical benefits of fading and recommends the approach.
  - Kokkonen, T., & Schalk, L. (2021). One instructional sequence fits all? A conceptual analysis of the applicability of concreteness fading in mathematics, physics, chemistry, and biology education. Educational Psychology Review, 33(3), 797-821. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09581-7 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, ERIC). Theoretical analysis; it concludes that concreteness fading may not be as generalizable as has been suggested, because representations and instructional goals differ by domain.
  - Kaminski, J. A., Sloutsky, V. M., & Heckler, A. F. (2008). The advantage of abstract examples in learning math. Science, 320(5875), 454-455. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1154659 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract; only a one-sentence summary is available, so the detail beyond it is not reported here). Experiments, undergraduates.
  - Sadoski, M., Goetz, E. T., & Fritz, J. B. (1993). Impact of concreteness on comprehensibility, interest, and memory for text: Implications for dual coding theory and text design. Journal of Educational Psychology, 85(2), 291-304. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.85.2.291 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, ERIC). Four experiments, 221 college students.
  - Sadoski, M., Goetz, E. T., & Rodriguez, M. (2000). Engaging texts: Effects of concreteness on comprehensibility, interest, and recall in four text types. Journal of Educational Psychology, 92(1), 85-95. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.92.1.85 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, ERIC). Undergraduates.
  - Chi, Feltovich, & Glaser (1981), see F4.
- Strength: moderate. The concept-teaching review is a design synthesis rather than an effect size, the case-comparison meta-analysis is strong but covers many kinds of materials, and the concrete-versus-abstract question is open.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Kaminski et al. caution that concrete detail can hide the structure the learner is supposed to extract. Chi et al. show novices tend to latch onto literal features. Comparison effects were larger with immediate tests than delayed ones. Most of this research is mathematics and science concepts, not diagnosis of real-world cases, and my search for classification-specific text research found nothing.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: For each outcome, write the definition in terms of the same features the key's questions test, then give one concrete case that shows those features, and a near-miss case that shows why the neighbouring outcome does not fit, in the same wording as the key. Vary the surface of examples (setting, wording, people) so the surface does not become the cue, and say in words which feature carries the decision. Put the comparison in plain sight ("this one differs from that one in exactly this respect"). The structure of the worked case itself is covered in `instruction.md` F1 to F3 and F19.

### F13. Analogies help novices draw inferences but cost precision on basic details, and they need an explicit mapping and an explicit statement of where they break.
- What the research shows: In four experiments, learners comprehended scientific concepts presented either literally or through an analogy. Basic-level questions were answered best after literal presentation; inferential questions were answered best after analogical presentation. The pattern was strongest in science novices. A review of analogy in education concludes that analogies require explicit support so that learners notice the relevance of relational thinking, have the processing resources to hold the relations in mind, and recognise both similarities and differences. A chapter on multiple analogies argues, as described in secondary summaries, that a single analogy that helps novices form a first grasp of a hard concept may later become an obstacle to fuller understanding, and that several analogies can counter this.
- Sources:
  - Donnelly, C. M., & McDaniel, M. A. (1993). Use of analogy in learning scientific concepts. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 19(4), 975-987. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.19.4.975 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, PubMed). Four experiments.
  - Richland, L. E., & Simms, N. (2015). Analogy, higher order thinking, and education. WIREs Cognitive Science, 6(2), 177-192. https://doi.org/10.1002/wcs.1336 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, Europe PMC). Review.
  - Spiro, R. J., Feltovich, P. J., Coulson, R. L., & Anderson, D. K. (1989). Multiple analogies for complex concepts: Antidotes for analogy-induced misconception in advanced knowledge acquisition. In S. Vosniadou & A. Ortony (Eds.), Similarity and Analogical Reasoning (pp. 498-531). Cambridge University Press. https://doi.org/10.1017/cbo9780511529863.023 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (citation; findings secondary: search summaries of the chapter's argument; this is a theoretical chapter, not an experiment).
- Strength: moderate for the basic-versus-inferential trade-off (one four-experiment paper, multiple-choice measures), limited for the misconception risk.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Undergraduates, science concepts, tests right after reading. The misconception risk is argued for advanced knowledge in complex domains and is not shown for beginner lessons.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Use an analogy to give the shape of a mechanism, then state the literal rule in plain terms, and say where the analogy breaks. Do not let an analogy stand in for the literal definition, because the details are what the key later tests. Keep analogies out of key questions and options, where precise wording matters.

## D. Feedback

### F14. Telling learners why an answer is right or wrong beats telling them only whether they were right, and feedback that carries more task-relevant information tends to do more.
- What the research shows: A meta-analysis of item-based feedback in computer-based learning (40 studies, 70 effect sizes) found larger effects for elaborated feedback such as an explanation (0.49) than for knowledge of the correct response (0.32) or correct/incorrect only (0.05). Elaborated feedback was especially better for higher-order outcomes. Effects were larger in mathematics than in social sciences, science and languages, and were lower with delayed feedback and in primary and high school samples. A larger meta-analysis (435 studies, 994 effects, more than 61,000 learners) found a medium overall effect (d = 0.48) with large heterogeneity; by type of feedback, reinforcement or punishment had d = 0.24, corrective feedback d = 0.46 and high-information feedback d = 0.99. An earlier meta-analysis of feedback in test-like events (58 effect sizes from 40 reports) found effects could be large under optimal conditions and appeared when the learner received the feedback mindfully, and varied with whether learners could see answers before attempting. A review of the field says effectiveness depends on the type of feedback and how it is given.
- Sources:
  - Van der Kleij, F. M., Feskens, R. C. W., & Eggen, T. J. H. M. (2015). Effects of feedback in a computer-based learning environment on students' learning outcomes: A meta-analysis. Review of Educational Research, 85(4), 475-511. https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314564881 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Meta-analysis.
  - Wisniewski, B., Zierer, K., & Hattie, J. (2020). The power of feedback revisited: A meta-analysis of educational feedback research. Frontiers in Psychology, 10, 3087. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract; effect sizes by feedback type read from the open-access full text). Meta-analysis.
  - Bangert-Drowns, R. L., Kulik, C.-L. C., Kulik, J. A., & Morgan, M. (1991). The instructional effect of feedback in test-like events. Review of Educational Research, 61(2), 213-238. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543061002213 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Meta-analysis.
  - Hattie, J., & Timperley, H. (2007). The power of feedback. Review of Educational Research, 77(1), 81-112. https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Review and conceptual model.
  - Shute, V. J. (2008). Focus on formative feedback. Review of Educational Research, 78(1), 153-189. https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654307313795 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract and full text). Review. Its guideline tables recommend elaborated feedback that describes the what, how and why of a problem, and keeping it specific and clear.
- Strength: strong that explanatory feedback beats bare verification; moderate on size, because the meta-analyses are heterogeneous and feedback "information content" is confounded with other differences between studies.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The advantage is largest for higher-order outcomes and smaller outside mathematics. Elaborated feedback can be skipped by learners and can overload them (F15). Most of the studies are in classrooms and lab tasks, not self-study classification. Not every experiment shows an advantage for explanation over simpler feedback: in a conference-paper study of 143 middle-school students solving algebra problems on a computer, any feedback (correct answer, try again, or explanation) gave higher post-test scores than none, regardless of feedback type (Fyfe, 2016, North American Chapter of the International Group for the Psychology of Mathematics Education, 2016, abstract read in ERIC; no DOI retrieved).
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Never end a wrong-answer message at "Incorrect". Give the right answer, the reason in the lesson's own words, and the reason the chosen option does not fit. For the key, say which answer to which question the case actually supports and point to the cue in the case text. Because the app scores name and route separately, the reason should address the route.

### F15. More feedback is not always better: very long or complex feedback can lower efficiency, and many learners skip it, so put the decisive explanation first and keep each piece manageable.
- What the research shows: Shute's review reports that a meta-analysis of 15 computer-based and programmed-instruction studies found no significant relationship between the amount of information in feedback and its effects, that reteaching the content worked as well as feedback built on expensive error analysis in algebra studies, and that in one study with undergraduates (a 2,400-word passage, 16 multiple-choice questions), more complex feedback was associated with less error correction and lower learning efficiency than simple correct-answer feedback. The review concludes that the nature and quality of the content may matter more than its complexity and recommends presenting elaborated feedback in manageable units, because long feedback can be ignored or dilute the message. Three studies of attention to feedback in a computer-based assessment found that about half of respondents attended to feedback on incorrect answers only and about another quarter paid no attention to feedback at all, depending on task difficulty and test length. A higher-education experiment found no significant difference in post-test achievement between feedback conditions (immediate correct response plus elaboration, delayed correct response plus elaboration, or delayed correct/incorrect only), with more attention paid to immediate feedback. A large online study (2,445 participants) found that adding an explanation to the correct answer raised later performance after incorrect first responses but not after correct ones.
- Sources:
  - Shute (2008), see F14 (full text read; the meta-analysis, the algebra studies and the undergraduate study are as reported in that review, and I did not retrieve the originals).
  - Timmers, C., & Veldkamp, B. (2011). Attention paid to feedback provided by a computer-based assessment for learning on information literacy. Computers & Education, 56(3), 923-930. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2010.11.007 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, ERIC). Three studies.
  - Van der Kleij, F. M., Eggen, T. J. H. M., Timmers, C. F., & Veldkamp, B. P. (2012). Effects of feedback in a computer-based assessment for learning. Computers & Education, 58(1), 263-272. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2011.07.020 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, ERIC; citation confirmed in Crossref). Experiment, higher education.
  - Attali, Y., & van der Kleij, F. (2017). Effects of feedback elaboration and feedback timing during computer-based practice in mathematics problem solving. Computers & Education, 110, 154-169. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2017.03.012 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (citation in Crossref; findings secondary: an institutional repository abstract). Randomized experiment, N = 2,445.
- Strength: moderate, and mixed: the evidence is about efficiency and attention, and it does not fix a length.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: This pulls against F14, which shows explanations beat bare verification on average. The reconciliation is in content and ordering: relevant, response-specific explanation helps; padded or multi-part feedback is skipped or dilutes. The attention results come from assessment-for-learning tasks in higher education and from an online math site.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: There is no cap on how much explanation a wrong answer may need, but structure it: first line, the verdict and the right answer; second, the reason tied to the cue in the case; then, if useful, why the other options fail, in short separate pieces or behind an expand control. After a correct answer, one short reason is enough unless the route was wrong (F18). Because many learners will read only the first lines, the decisive sentence goes first.

### F16. Feedback should be about the task and the reasoning, not about the person; praise adds little, and attention drawn to the self can reduce the benefit of feedback.
- What the research shows: A meta-analysis of 607 effect sizes (23,663 observations) found that feedback interventions improved performance on average (d = 0.41) but that more than a third of them decreased performance; effectiveness fell as feedback moved attention up toward the self and away from the task. A later large meta-analysis found the lowest effect for reinforcement and punishment (d = 0.24) and weaker effects of feedback on motivational than on cognitive outcomes (d = 0.33 against d = 0.51). Shute's guidelines, drawing on these results, say to focus feedback on the task, use praise sparingly if at all, and avoid comparisons with others and overall grades. Hattie and Timperley distinguish feedback at the level of the task, the process, self-regulation and the self, and secondary summaries of the model say the first three levels are the most beneficial for learning; I read the levels in those summaries because the abstract does not list them.
- Sources:
  - Kluger, A. N., & DeNisi, A. (1996). The effects of feedback interventions on performance: A historical review, a meta-analysis, and a preliminary feedback intervention theory. Psychological Bulletin, 119(2), 254-284. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Meta-analysis.
  - Wisniewski et al. (2020), Shute (2008), Hattie & Timperley (2007), see F14. The levels are VERIFIED-ONLINE (citation; findings secondary: educational-authority summaries of the model).
- Strength: strong. A large meta-analysis and later replication agree, and the guideline is consistent across reviews.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Much of the evidence is from workplace, laboratory and school tasks. Feedback is less effective for motivational outcomes in the later meta-analysis, so affirming messages may have a role in keeping people studying that these learning-outcome studies cannot see.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Write feedback as information ("Right: the case shows X, which only Y has"), not evaluation ("Great job!" or "Not quite"). Keep any status mark neutral. Do not compare the learner with others. A short neutral confirmation is fine as long as the content follows it.

### F17. Feedback timing is contested: immediate feedback supports novices and hard material, delayed feedback sometimes improves retention and transfer, and the best-supported compromise for new material is immediate feedback with a later review.
- What the research shows: A meta-analysis of 53 studies found a mixed picture: applied studies with real classroom quizzes mostly favoured immediate feedback; experimental studies of acquiring test content mostly favoured delay; list-learning studies varied systematically with study features. A two-experiment study with prose passages of 280 to 300 words found that delayed feedback produced better performance on a later cued-recall test than immediate feedback, the benefit being attributed to the spacing of the information, and that the type of feedback (showing the answer versus answering until correct) made no difference. In an engineering course, feedback one week late led to better exam performance on new problems about the same concepts, even though students thought immediate feedback helped them more. The computer-based meta-analysis in F14 found delayed feedback to be negatively related to effects overall, with no significant interaction by outcome type. In the 2,445-participant study, immediate feedback followed by a delayed review produced higher later performance than delayed feedback after incorrect first responses (and lower after correct first responses), and immediate feedback without the review gave lower performance in both cases (per the repository abstract as retrieved). Shute's review recommends immediate feedback for difficult tasks and low-achieving learners, and delayed feedback for simple tasks, high achievers and when transfer is the goal.
- Sources:
  - Kulik, J. A., & Kulik, C.-L. C. (1988). Timing of feedback and verbal learning. Review of Educational Research, 58(1), 79-97. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543058001079 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Meta-analysis.
  - Butler, A. C., Karpicke, J. D., & Roediger, H. L. (2007). The effect of type and timing of feedback on learning from multiple-choice tests. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 13(4), 273-281. https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898x.13.4.273 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, Europe PMC). Two experiments (population not stated in the abstract).
  - Mullet, H. G., Butler, A. C., Verdin, B., von Borries, R., & Marsh, E. J. (2014). Delaying feedback promotes transfer of knowledge despite student preferences to receive feedback immediately. Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition, 3(3), 222-229. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jarmac.2014.05.001 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Two experiments in an upper-level college course.
  - Van der Kleij et al. (2015), Attali & van der Kleij (2017), Shute (2008), see F14 and F15.
- Strength: contested. Different task types and delays give opposite results, and the delay advantage is plausibly a spacing effect rather than a feedback effect.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The delay studies mostly used undergraduates (not complete novices) and delays of a day to a week. The immediate-feedback advantage is strongest for difficult new material and for lower-order outcomes. Students prefer immediate feedback and judge it more helpful even when delay leads to better transfer, so learner preference is a poor guide.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: In the unit drills, where the learner is a novice on material just taught, give feedback immediately; the evidence for withholding it is weakest exactly there. Get the benefits of delay by bringing the item back later (a recurring item in the Mixed drill, with the explanation shown again), not by holding the first explanation back. Spacing itself belongs to another cluster.

### F18. Feedback after correct answers still matters, especially when the learner was unsure; a correct answer can hide a wrong route or a lucky guess.
- What the research shows: In two experiments, participants answered general-knowledge multiple-choice questions and rated their confidence, and feedback on correct answers doubled retention of the answers they had given with low confidence, compared with no feedback. The authors conclude that the function of feedback is to correct metacognitive errors as well as memory errors. Against that, a large study found that adding an explanation to the correct answer helped after incorrect first responses but not after correct ones, and a study of attention to feedback found that learners often skip feedback on correct answers.
- Sources:
  - Butler, A. C., Karpicke, J. D., & Roediger, H. L. (2008). Correcting a metacognitive error: Feedback increases retention of low-confidence correct responses. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 34(4), 918-928. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.34.4.918 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, Europe PMC). Two experiments (population not stated in the abstract).
  - Attali & van der Kleij (2017) and Timmers & Veldkamp (2011), see F15.
- Strength: moderate. The low-confidence result is clean but comes from general-knowledge facts; it is in tension with the finding that explanations after correct answers add little.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The result was about simple confirmation of a correct answer, not an explanation; the elaboration result is for mathematics problems. No source tested classification routes.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Because name and route are scored separately, "right name, wrong route" is exactly the hidden-error case. After a correct answer, show a one-line recap of the route or cue that makes it right, and give the fuller route explanation when the route was wrong even if the name was right. An optional one-tap "how sure were you?" rating would let the app give extra feedback to low-confidence correct answers; that is a design option inspired by the finding, not something tested in this app.

### F19. Errors made with high confidence are the most likely to be corrected when feedback arrives, but the correct answer often fades and the original error can come back, so one corrective message is not the end of it.
- What the research shows: Participants most often corrected the errors they had made with high confidence on a later retest, contrary to the researchers' own expectations, which suggests people can tell what is correct once they see it. A review of learning from errors concludes that errorful learning followed by corrective feedback is beneficial and that corrective feedback including an analysis of the reasoning that led to the mistake is crucial. A one-week follow-up found that the hypercorrection effect held on both immediate and delayed tests, but overall error correction decreased on the delayed test, and when learners failed to correct an error after a week they were more likely to repeat it if it had been a high-confidence error. A set of six experiments using questions participants could not answer found that unsuccessful attempts to retrieve the answer before seeing it enhanced later learning compared with reading the question and answer together.
- Sources:
  - Butterfield, B., & Metcalfe, J. (2001). Errors committed with high confidence are hypercorrected. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 27(6), 1491-1494. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.27.6.1491 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, Europe PMC). Experiments.
  - Metcalfe, J. (2017). Learning from errors. Annual Review of Psychology, 68, 465-489. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010416-044022 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Review.
  - Butler, A. C., Fazio, L. K., & Marsh, E. J. (2011). The hypercorrection effect persists over a week, but high-confidence errors return. Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 18(6), 1238-1244. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-011-0173-y . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, Europe PMC). Experiment.
  - Kornell, N., Hays, M. J., & Bjork, R. A. (2009). Unsuccessful retrieval attempts enhance subsequent learning. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 35(4), 989-998. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015729 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, Europe PMC). Six experiments.
- Strength: strong for the immediate hypercorrection effect, moderate for lasting correction.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: General-knowledge facts and word associations; no study of misclassified real-world cases. The persistence of old errors after a week shows that one explanation does not finish the job.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Treat a confident wrong answer as the best moment to teach; show the reasoning that leads from the learner's choice to the right answer, not only the answer. Make sure the item returns later in the Mixed drill. Spacing and retrieval practice are covered by another cluster.

### F20. In multiple-choice drills the wrong options can teach wrong things unless the feedback addresses them, and plausible alternatives plus an explanation of why each is wrong turn the drill into a learning event.
- What the research shows: Taking a multiple-choice test boosted later performance, including on questions tapping higher-level concepts, but also led students to learn some false facts from the lures, which persisted on later tests; the positive effects outweighed the cost. Reading more lures decreased the testing benefit and increased the production of lure answers. When feedback was given after the test, whether immediately or later, it increased the proportion of correct answers and reduced the proportion of lure intrusions on a delayed test. When the alternatives were plausible enough for test takers to retrieve both why the correct answer was right and why the others were wrong, multiple-choice testing also facilitated recall of information about the incorrect alternatives, which cued-recall tests did not.
- Sources:
  - Roediger, H. L., & Marsh, E. J. (2005). The positive and negative consequences of multiple-choice testing. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 31(5), 1155-1159. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.31.5.1155 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, Europe PMC). Experiments.
  - Marsh, E. J., Roediger, H. L., Bjork, R. A., & Bjork, E. L. (2007). The memorial consequences of multiple-choice testing. Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 14(2), 194-199. https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03194051 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, Europe PMC). Experiments.
  - Butler, A. C., & Roediger, H. L. (2008). Feedback enhances the positive effects and reduces the negative effects of multiple-choice testing. Memory & Cognition, 36(3), 604-616. https://doi.org/10.3758/mc.36.3.604 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, Europe PMC). Experiment (population not stated in the abstract).
  - Little, J. L., Bjork, E. L., Bjork, R. A., & Angello, G. (2012). Multiple-choice tests exonerated, at least of some charges: Fostering test-induced learning and avoiding test-induced forgetting. Psychological Science, 23(11), 1337-1344. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797612443370 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, Europe PMC). Two experiments.
- Strength: strong. Consistent results from one research programme across several labs' replications of the same paradigm.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Prose passages and general-knowledge questions; one-off tests. Whether the lure effect appears with diagnostic categories that recur across a course is untested.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: The quickDrills' shared option list (the same outcome names as options for every item) makes the alternatives plausible competitors, which is what Little et al. recommend. The weak point is the explanation: each `w` should say why the right answer is right and which feature rules out the nearest wrong option, and any item answered wrongly should show that explanation. The errDrill, where learners meet faulty claims, should put the corrected claim in the feedback so the faulty wording is not the last thing read.

### F21. Feedback helps and hurts depending on what the learner already knows: novices benefit from corrective feedback and directive support, while learners who already hold the right strategy can be hurt by minimal feedback or find it redundant.
- What the research shows: In two experiments with elementary schoolchildren (N = 108 and N = 101), feedback helped children who had not been taught a correct strategy but had negative effects for children with induced knowledge of a correct strategy, including minimal immediate or summative correct-answer feedback relative to no feedback. Shute's guidelines map this onto learner characteristics: for low-achieving learners, use immediate, directive feedback with scaffolding and the correct response plus some elaboration; for high-achieving learners, consider delayed, facilitative feedback and note that verification alone may suffice.
- Sources:
  - Fyfe, E. R., & Rittle-Johnson, B. (2016). Feedback both helps and hinders learning: The causal role of prior knowledge. Journal of Educational Psychology, 108(1), 82-97. https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000053 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Two experiments, children.
  - Shute (2008), see F14.
- Strength: moderate for the direction, but the experiments are with children and in mathematics; Shute's learner-characteristic table is a set of recommendations drawn from several studies.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: A companion study with 75 second graders (Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson, 2016, Journal of Experimental Child Psychology, 147, 140-151, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2016.03.009 , VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, ERIC)) found that computer feedback helped children with low prior knowledge and that immediate feedback also helped mastery for children with both low and high prior knowledge, which complicates a simple "experts do not need it" reading.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: First-time unit drills get the full explanation; later encounters with the same material (Mixed drill, repeated specimens) can use shorter feedback with an expand option. This is the feedback counterpart of F9.

## E. Delivery

### F22. Evidence that "bite-sized" mobile delivery by itself improves learning is thin; the solid findings are about segmenting and self-paced reading, not about making content short.
- What the research shows: A systematic review of 40 microlearning articles from 2020 to 2024 reported consistently positive effects across cognitive, behavioural and affective outcomes, but its own caveats are that most studies were in medical fields and in the USA, that the optimal duration is subjective, and that studies used durations of about one to fifteen minutes with ongoing debate about the ideal length. A meta-analysis of 110 studies of mobile devices in education reported a moderate average effect (0.523) for the use of mobile devices as such, which is not evidence about text length. The reading-medium meta-analyses in F10 are the nearest solid evidence for phones, and they concern screens in general.
- Sources:
  - Monib, W. K., Qazi, A., & Apong, R. A. (2025). Microlearning beyond boundaries: A systematic review and a novel framework for improving learning outcomes. Heliyon, 11(2), e41413. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heliyon.2024.e41413 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (citation in Crossref; findings secondary: the open-access full text on PubMed Central as summarised by the fetch tool). Systematic review.
  - Sung, Y.-T., Chang, K.-E., & Liu, T.-C. (2016). The effects of integrating mobile devices with teaching and learning on students' learning performance: A meta-analysis and research synthesis. Computers & Education, 94, 252-275. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2015.11.008 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Meta-analysis.
  - Delgado et al. (2018), Clinton (2019), Rey et al. (2019), see F10.
- Strength: limited. Heterogeneous reviews, short follow-ups, and no length-specific experiments.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Microlearning reviews pool many definitions of "micro". Another microlearning meta-analysis surfaced in my search, but it was on a preprint server and I did not verify it, so it is not used.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Do not defend thin explanations by citing "microlearning". Use the evidence for what it supports: self-paced pacing, one idea per card, no scrolling cliffs, and a screen-sized unit for feedback.

## F. Procedure, principle and the feeling of understanding

### F23. The strongest evidence for "shorter is better" comes from minimalist software manuals, where a briefer task-first manual beat a conventional one for adults learning a procedure; its brevity came with added error-recovery help and real tasks, so it is not evidence for thin explanation in general.
- What the research shows: The Minimal Manual was designed as a briefer self-instruction manual that helps learners coordinate attention between the system and the manual, trains them to recognise and recover from errors, and supports later reference use. In two experiments it afforded more efficient learning progress than an otherwise comparable commercially developed manual and was superior in the areas its design predicted. In a later experiment comparing a minimal manual with a conventional one, minimal-manual users completed about 50% more tasks successfully on a performance test and showed more self-reliance (more self-initiated error recoveries, fewer manual consultations). The minimalist approach, as its advocates describe it, goes almost directly to procedural skill development instead of building a conceptual model first, uses authentic tasks instead of mock exercises, and tries not to discuss everything the learner should see or know.
- Sources:
  - Carroll, J. M., Smith-Kerker, P. L., Ford, J. R., & Mazur-Rimetz, S. A. (1987). The Minimal Manual. Human-Computer Interaction, 3(2), 123-153. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327051hci0302_2 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Two experiments, adults learning a computing system.
  - van der Meij, H., & Lazonder, A. W. (1993). Assessment of the minimalist approach to computer user documentation. Interacting with Computers, 5(4), 355-370. https://doi.org/10.1016/0953-5438(93)90001-a . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Experiment.
- Strength: moderate within its domain (replicated in more than one experiment, adults), but narrow: learning to operate software.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The tasks were procedures the learner could try on the real system straight away, so practice carried the learning that explanation would otherwise carry. The advantage came from three design moves together (brevity, authentic task focus, explicit error recovery), not from brevity alone. The goal was skill at a defined procedure, not the ability to classify new cases, and the minimalist authors themselves note the trade-off in F24.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: If someone argues for very short cards by pointing to minimalism, the accurate reading is that the short manuals were task-first, put the learner in front of real cases early, and gave explicit help for recognising and recovering from wrong turns. In this app that translates to: get to a real case quickly, keep each card on one job, and give explicit error-recovery feedback. It does not translate to cutting the reasoning behind the key. The minimalist approach also does not settle how a conceptual skill such as classification should be explained.

### F24. Step-by-step instructions improve initial performance, while explaining the principles behind the steps improves learning and transfer, so a key without its rationale lets learners follow it without understanding it.
- What the research shows: A review of research on procedural instructions, principles and examples concludes that there is a trade-off between usability and learnability: specific instructions help initial performance, while more general instructions that require problem solving help learning and transfer. Learning from instructions takes cognitive effort, and learners typically opt for low effort. Both goals can be met with methods such as fading and combining different types of instruction. Separately, four experiments found that adding a label for a group of steps in worked examples helped participants form subgoals, assessed by problem-solving performance and talk-aloud protocols, and that abstract labels led to subgoals with fewer ties to surface features than superficial labels, which supported solving novel problems that needed changed steps.
- Sources:
  - Eiriksdottir, E., & Catrambone, R. (2011). Procedural instructions, principles, and examples: How to structure instructions for procedural tasks to enhance performance, learning, and transfer. Human Factors, 53(6), 749-770. https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720811419154 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, Europe PMC). Literature review.
  - Catrambone, R. (1998). The subgoal learning model: Creating better examples so that students can solve novel problems. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 127(4), 355-376. https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.127.4.355 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract). Four experiments.
- Strength: moderate. A review of a mixed literature plus a set of experiments by one researcher; the task types were procedures and problem solving.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: If the aim is only to get someone through a procedure today, the explanation is dead weight (F23). Learners prefer low effort, so rationale that is not required by any later task is likely to be skipped. The subgoal-label experiments used example solutions in problem-solving tasks, which differs from classification.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: The determination key is a procedure. A learner can follow it by recognising surface cues and still not know why each question is asked. Every key question (the gate question and the two follow-ups per branch) needs a lesson-card explanation of what the question separates and why that distinction decides between the possible outcomes, in the same wording the key uses. Give each group of questions an abstract purpose label tied to the outcome definitions (what they sort, not what they mention in the example case). Then use fading: full rationale in early specimens, reduced rationale later.

### F25. Short, fluent explanations create an illusion of understanding: people overestimate how well they grasp how things work until they are asked to explain, so a learner's feeling that a text is clear is not evidence that it taught.
- What the research shows: Across twelve studies, people felt they understood complex phenomena with far more precision, coherence and depth than they really did, an illusion the authors call the illusion of explanatory depth. It was far stronger for explanatory knowledge (how things work) than for facts, procedures or narratives. It was strongest where the environment supports real-time explanations with visible mechanisms.
- Sources:
  - Rozenblit, L., & Keil, F. (2002). The misunderstood limits of folk science: An illusion of explanatory depth. Cognitive Science, 26(5), 521-562. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog2605_1 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract, Europe PMC). Twelve studies.
- Strength: moderate. Replicated within the paper across studies; the topics were everyday mechanisms (devices, natural phenomena), not classification skills.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The paper concerns self-ratings of understanding, not the effect of text length on learning. The link to lessons is an inference: a thin explanation can feel adequate, and a clear-feeling card does not show the learner could use it.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Do not judge a card by whether it reads smoothly or whether the learner says it makes sense. Check by making the learner use it: a drill item or a short "say it back" question that cannot be answered by recognising words. The owner's report that the lessons were confusing is itself a high-value signal, because confusion is what thin text produces in readers who do notice the gaps.

## Tensions inside this cluster

1. **Explicit connections for novices versus gaps that force inference (F1, F2).** The "reverse cohesion" results say knowledgeable readers learn more deeply from text that leaves gaps. The literature resolves this by learner knowledge and skill: low-knowledge readers gain from explicit text; the gap benefit is limited to readers with high knowledge (and in one study only those with weaker comprehension skill) and to deeper-understanding measures. In this app the learners are novices to each subject, so lesson text should be explicit. Inference practice belongs in drills, where it can be checked.

2. **Elaboration helps versus extra material hurts (F7, F8, F2).** The coherence and seductive-details work says removing material improves learning; elaboration work says adding explanation improves learning. Both are consistent once the dividing line is relevance to the learning goal and precision: relevant, precise explanation helps (also in Reder's skill-learning study and in the STEM text meta-analysis); interesting-but-irrelevant material hurts. Length is not the variable.

3. **Summaries beat full text versus thin text fails novices (F8, F1).** The summary advantage is for memory of main points, with materials built around one coherent chain, tested soon after reading. It supports adding a recap, not replacing explanation.

4. **Elaborated feedback beats bare verification versus feedback that is too complex gets skipped or lowers efficiency (F14, F15, F18).** The literature resolves this through content and structure, not length: response-specific explanation beats bare verification, but complex multi-part feedback can reduce efficiency, many learners read only feedback on wrong answers, and explanation after a correct answer adds little in at least one large study. The lesson is explanation after errors, a short reason after correct answers, decisive sentence first.

5. **Immediate versus delayed feedback (F17).** Classroom and computer-based evidence mostly favours immediate feedback, especially for difficult material and weaker learners; laboratory and a few classroom studies find delay better for retention and transfer, probably through spacing. The best-supported compromise is immediate feedback plus later review, which is also what a recurring Mixed drill gives.

6. **Define jargon versus avoid jargon (F5).** Definitions with context and repeated exposure improve vocabulary learning, yet defined jargon still disrupts fluent reading. The resolution is to divide terms into target terms (the learner must learn them; define and reuse) and incidental terms (avoid).

7. **Consistent wording versus varied examples for transfer (F4, F12).** Consistency of names across lesson, key and drill avoids confusion; variation across cases prevents the surface from becoming the cue. These do not conflict if the fixed terms are fixed everywhere and the variation is confined to the case text, with feedback that maps the varied wording back to the fixed term.

8. **Concrete versus abstract examples (F12).** Evidence is split (concreteness fading and imagery results against the abstract-example advantage). The compromise supported by the reviews is concrete first with explicit movement to the abstract rule, and varied surface features.

9. **Small chunks are for novices versus chunking helped high-knowledge learners more (F9, F10).** The segmenting meta-analysis found larger retention gains for high-knowledge learners, against the usual story. Expertise reversal suggests novices need more support, not necessarily smaller pieces. The safe reading is that segmentation is not harmful for novices and that "novices need small chunks" is not a clean finding.

10. **Brevity in minimalist manuals versus length for conceptual learning (F23, F24).** Minimalism won for procedural tasks the learner could practise on the real system; principle-based instruction wins for learning and transfer. A classification skill needs transfer to new cases, so principles are required, while the minimalist lessons about getting to real cases early and supporting error recovery still apply.

## What the research does not tell us

- **How long an explanation should be.** I found no study that sets or tests an optimal word count, paragraph count or card length for an explanation. Everything above is about function (what a sentence does) and chunking (what is shown at once), not about a number. Any specific length rule in the app would be a design choice, not a finding.
- **Terminology consistency across a lesson, a key and a quiz.** Direct evidence is a small synonym-versus-repetition experiment I could read only through a summary, a word-memory study, and theory. One practitioner write-up states that the author could not find experiments on this. The recommendation (one name per concept, mapped variants) is low-risk and well motivated, but not empirically established for multi-part instruction.
- **Classification-specific instruction.** I found no study of how explanatory text or feedback should be written for diagnostic classification of real-world cases with route and name scored separately. Everything is extrapolated from fact learning, science and mathematics text, procedures and multiple-choice testing.
- **Feedback about the route.** No experiments retrieved test feedback that corrects the reasoning route when the final answer was right. F18 and F24 argue for it from related findings.
- **The learners in this app.** Most of the cited participants are university students or school children. Adults with mixed literacy, studying alone, on a phone, across many sessions are largely absent. Phone-specific evidence in what I could verify is essentially nil; the screen results are about screens generally.
- **Long-term retention.** Most experiments test minutes to about a week later. The feedback-timing and hypercorrection results show that short-term and longer-term effects can differ, and I have little evidence about months.
- **Motivation and drop-off.** The seductive-details and feedback meta-analyses measure learning, not whether people keep studying. Whether short, cheerful cards keep adults coming back more than complete explanations is untested in what I found, and it could cut against the recommendations above.
- **Pictures and dual coding.** I did not review the evidence on illustrations and diagrams in depth; this app is text-first. The little I verified (relevant, coordinated pictures help; decorative ones can hurt) is in F7 and F8 and is thin for this app.
- **Sources I could not read in full.** Reder and Anderson (1980, 1982), Reder, Charney and Morgan (1986), Stein, Morris and Bransford (1978), Oversteegen and van Wijk (2003), Spiro et al. (1989) and Attali and van der Kleij (2017) were verified as citations but their findings come from secondary reports, as marked. The synthesizer should treat them as one notch weaker than the abstract-verified sources.
- **Items that fell outside this cluster or could not be verified.** Pre-training of component names (Mayer, Mathias and Wetzell, 2002), the full account of worked examples and ordering, and self-explanation are handled in `instruction.md` or other notes. A "microlearning meta-analysis" surfaced in search only on a preprint server and was not used.
