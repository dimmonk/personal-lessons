# Telling similar categories apart and carrying it into real cases: research notes for Fieldcraft

Cluster: learning to classify cases and transfer to real situations (interleaving versus blocking, contrasting cases and comparison, example variety, concreteness, self-explanation, diagnostic-classification teaching, limits of transfer, applying learning to one's own life).
Researcher notes for the synthesizer. Written 2026-10-04. Scope: adults doing self-study on a phone, learning to read an unlabelled real-world case, answer two or three narrowing questions, and name the outcome.

## Short answer to the cluster question

How do you teach someone to tell similar categories apart by rule and also recognise them in messy real cases? The research in this cluster supports a sequence of moves, not a single technique.

1. Let the learner meet each outcome on its own first: what it is in general, its characteristic features, and one clean concrete case (F3, F11, F12, F13).
2. Put each outcome next to its closest look-alike and make the learner compare the two on the specific feature the key tests, then state the difference in plain words (F2, F6, F7).
3. Practise mixed across the confusable outcomes, not blocked by unit, so that the learner has to choose the category or method from the case itself (F1, F5), and tell the learner that mixed practice feels harder than blocked practice but works better (F4).
4. Teach two ways of reaching an answer and say that both are meant: the feature-by-feature check (the key's route) and the overall sense of resemblance to cases already seen, with the check used to confirm or overturn the impression (F19). Do not rely on "slow down" or "watch for bias" reminders (F21).
5. Move the cases from clean to messy, and vary the story while the decisive feature stays fixed, so the idea can travel (F9, F10, F12).
6. Ask for the reason, "why this and not that", before the model reason is shown, and teach exceptions explicitly, because explaining pushes learners toward the general rule (F15 to F18).
7. Aim for near-to-moderate transfer on purpose: specimens in the format real life uses, rules taught with examples from several areas of life, a planned revisit, and where the subject calls for action, an optional if-then plan (F22 to F27).

Nearly every one of these adds material (a second case in each pair, contrast text, an exceptions card, a mixed drill, a plan card). None supports shortening a lesson, and the research here sets no ceiling on how long a lesson may be; what it argues against is padding and unexplained steps.

The evidence is strongest for interleaving and case comparison with picture and mathematics material, moderate for explanation prompts and combined diagnostic strategies, and thinnest for exactly what this app teaches: verbal, partly contested categories studied by adults alone on a phone. See "What the research does not tell us".

## Implications at a glance (for the synthesizer)

Each line is a design inference from the cited findings, not a tested result for this app. The app parts are the ones named in the task description.

- **Lesson cards.** One outcome at a time at first, with characteristic features and a concrete case that is then stripped back to the decisive feature (F11, F13). Worked cases in pairs, with an instruction naming what to compare (F6, F7). A named structural feature, separated from surface detail (F9). Exceptions and hybrids taught explicitly (F17). The rule stated after the comparison, in the same words the key and the drill use (F7).
- **Unit drill.** From Unit 2 on, include items whose answer depends on an earlier unit and do not label them by unit (F1, F5). Feedback names what separates the right answer from the tempting neighbour (F2, F18). Ask for a short reason before showing the model one (F15, F16).
- **Mixed drill across subjects.** The discrimination benefit grows with similarity between categories (F1, F2), and categories from different subjects are dissimilar, so cross-subject mixing probably serves retrieval and spacing more than discrimination; confusable outcomes inside one subject are where mixing pays (inference, not tested).
- **Determination key on specimens.** Alternate confusable outcomes in the specimen order; order clean to messy; vary the story while the decisive feature stays fixed (F1, F10, F12). Contrastive "why" and "fals" feedback (F2, F18). Teach the route and the overall impression as two checks on each other (F19).
- **Caveats and verdicts.** Hybrids and borderline cases are the "novel distinctions" classification-only learners miss; teach them, do not append them (F11, F17).
- **Practice design and tone.** Do not let learners choose a blocked-only mode as the default, say that mixed practice is meant to feel harder, and show real accuracy (F4). Avoid cards that tell learners to be careful (F21).
- **Transfer to the learner's own life.** End-of-unit "where would you meet this" step, real-format cases, a revisit after a delay, optional if-then plan card with example cues, and legitimate as well as fraudulent cases in the Scams subject (F22 to F26).


## Index of findings

**A. Telling similar categories apart: interleaving and blocking**
- F1. Mixing the confusable categories during practice, rather than finishing one category before the next, helps people learn to tell them apart and to classify new cases.
- F2. The benefit comes from putting different categories next to each other so their differences show, not from spacing as such.
- F3. Blocking can win: when categories are dissimilar or easy to tell apart, when study is passive, for some verbal material, and possibly for a learner's first look at a category.
- F4. Learners misjudge this: they feel blocked practice works better, even after their own results show the opposite.
- F5. When the skill is "choose the method or type from the case itself", interleaved practice beats blocked practice in real classes, including a randomised trial.

**B. Comparison and contrasting cases**
- F6. Comparing two cases side by side teaches the shared structure better than studying the same cases one after the other.
- F7. Comparison only works when the learner is pointed at the right thing, and the best kind of comparison depends on what the learner already knows.
- F8. Contrasting cases can prepare a learner to get more out of an explanation, but "work it out before being told" is contested and is risky for novices studying alone.

**C. What the learner ends up knowing: structure versus surface, variety, concreteness**
- F9. Novices sort cases by their surface features and experts by the underlying principle; examples that emphasise structure move novices toward the expert pattern.
- F10. Varied examples help people carry an idea to new situations, but only once they can see the structure; people with little knowledge do better first with examples that look alike.
- F11. The task you practise shapes what you learn: practice that asks only "which category is this?" narrows attention to the features that separate the categories, at a cost to knowing what each category is like.
- F12. For recognising messy real cases, starting from clean, unambiguous cases can beat training on a representative mix, and remembering specific past cases helps novices with atypical ones.
- F13. Starting with a concrete case and then explicitly fading to the generic form tends to carry ideas further than either alone.
- F14. Abstract-only teaching is not supported for the app's goal, because what transfers is tied to the form in which it was learned.

**D. Explaining: self-explanation, explanation prompts and "why this and not that"**
- F15. Prompting learners to explain material to themselves improves learning and transfer, on average by a medium amount.
- F16. How self-explanation is prompted matters: content-focused prompts work, on-demand explanations and multiple-choice prompts may undercut it, and it takes time.
- F17. Explaining pushes learners to look for a general pattern, which helps discovery of the rule and hurts learning when the category has exceptions.
- F18. "Why would this be true of this one and not of the neighbouring one?" is a well-studied prompt, and it works better when the learner already knows something about the topic.

**E. Teaching diagnostic classification: rules, pattern recognition and mixed practice**
- F19. Teaching novices to use both a feature-by-feature check and an overall sense of resemblance to known cases beats teaching either alone.
- F20. How practice is arranged matters more than how the lesson is organised: mixed practice beat blocked practice for ECG diagnosis, features-first and category-first teaching tied, and short mixed classification trials with feedback speed up pattern recognition.
- F21. Telling people to slow down or to watch out for their biases does not improve diagnosis; organised knowledge and structured comparison do.

**F. Carrying it into real situations and one's own life**
- F22. Transfer across very different situations is rare and depends on specific conditions; nobody can promise "general critical thinking" from a lesson.
- F23. Short formal training in a reasoning rule, taught with varied examples, can improve reasoning about everyday problems, including in domains not taught, though the untrained-domain effect fades faster.
- F24. Debiasing training that combines instruction with practice and personalised feedback can reduce biases for months and in one field case, but the evidence is concentrated and partly mixed.
- F25. "If this situation comes up, then I will do this" plans help people turn a goal into action, with a medium-to-large average effect, but only when they have a goal and a clear cue, and the effect varies.
- F26. Short education can reduce susceptibility to fraud without making people suspicious of everything, but the effect fades unless there is a reminder.
- F27. To be able to apply a definition to new cases, practise with examples of it, not only the definition; and provided examples have beaten learner-generated ones in the tests I found.

## A. Telling similar categories apart: interleaving and blocking

### F1. Mixing the confusable categories during practice, rather than finishing one category before the next, helps people learn to tell them apart and to classify new cases.
- What the research shows: In the study that started this line of work, UCLA undergraduates studied 10 paintings by each of 12 artists, with the paintings of each artist shown together (blocked) or mixed with the other artists (interleaved). They then had to name the artist of new paintings by the same artists. Mixed study won clearly: about 61% against 35% correct in one experiment (d about 1.0) and 59% against 36% in a second (d about 1.3). Kang and Pashler found the same pattern with painting styles. A 2019 meta-analysis of 59 studies (238 effect sizes, 158 samples) found a moderate overall advantage for interleaving (Hedges g = 0.42), largest for paintings (g = 0.67), smaller for naturalistic photographs (g = 0.35) and for mathematics tasks (g = 0.34), not reliably different from zero for expository texts, and reversed for studies that used words (g = -0.39, blocking better). Whether the test used old or new items made no reliable difference, and the length of the delay before the test did not moderate it. A 2021 systematic review of 26 studies (17 in a meta-analysis) found benefits up to about g = 0.65 for recognising studied items and about g = 0.66 for classifying new ones, larger when the differences between items were subtle, extending to both art and science material and to delayed tests.
- Sources:
  - Kornell, N., & Bjork, R. A. (2008). Learning concepts and categories: Is spacing the "enemy of induction"? Psychological Science, 19(6), 585-592. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02127.x . VERIFIED-ONLINE (full text read; Crossref check passed). Experiments, undergraduates.
  - Kang, S., & Pashler, H. (2012). Learning painting styles: Spacing is advantageous when it promotes discriminative contrast. Applied Cognitive Psychology, 26(1), 97-103. https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1801 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Crossref; Crossref lists 2011 as the online date). Experiments.
  - Brunmair, M., & Richter, T. (2019). Similarity matters: A meta-analysis of interleaved learning and its moderators. Psychological Bulletin, 145(11), 1029-1052. https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (full text of the accepted manuscript read; Crossref check passed). Meta-analysis.
  - Firth, J., Rivers, I., & Boyle, J. M. E. (2021). A systematic review of interleaving as a concept learning strategy. Review of Education, 9(2), 642-684. https://doi.org/10.1002/rev3.3266 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via OpenAlex; Crossref check passed). Systematic review with meta-analysis.
- Strength: strong for picture-based categories and for choosing between problem types (F5); moderate and thinner for verbal or conceptual categories, which is most of what this app teaches, because the bulk of the studies use images and the meta-analysis found no clear benefit for expository text and a reversal for words.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Heterogeneity was large (I-squared about 77%), so the average hides a lot. Both reviews say the literature is dominated by laboratory studies of university students, and the 2019 authors note that this limits generalising to adults beyond college age. In that meta-analysis the benefit was larger in younger samples, although within university-only samples the age slope was not reliable and the authors warn that the age pattern comes from comparing studies, not people. See F3 for the conditions where blocking wins.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Any practice whose job is "tell A from B" has to put A items and B items next to each other, in an order the learner cannot predict. Two places in the learner path are blocked by construction. (1) A unit drill that follows a lesson on one idea tells the learner which idea to use before they read the item, so it trains carrying out the idea, not choosing it. From Unit 2 on, a drill should mix in a share of items whose right answer depends on an earlier unit, and should say so. (2) The specimens at the end should be ordered so that confusable outcomes alternate rather than cluster. This is a design inference from the studies, not a tested result for this exact app.

### F2. The benefit comes from putting different categories next to each other so their differences show, not from spacing as such.
- What the research shows: Kang and Pashler held the order blocked but added time between items (spacing without mixing) and got no better results than plain blocking, both worse than interleaving; showing paintings by different artists at the same time did as well as interleaving. Birnbaum and colleagues, using photographs of butterflies and birds, found that time gaps hurt when they broke up the side-by-side comparison of two categories, even when the total spacing was held equal, and helped only when they did not interrupt it. In the 2019 meta-analysis, the 17 comparisons that included both conditions showed a reliable interleaving benefit only when items followed each other immediately (g = 0.73), not when they were separated in time (g = 0.22, interval including zero). The same meta-analysis found bigger benefits when the categories were more similar to each other and when items within one category were more varied. Carvalho and Goldstone's experiments show the mechanism: with interleaving, attention goes to the properties that differ between successive items, so the properties that discriminate get encoded and remembered; with blocking, attention goes to what successive items share, so characteristic features get encoded.
- Sources:
  - Kang, S., & Pashler, H. (2012), as in F1.
  - Birnbaum, M. S., Kornell, N., Bjork, E. L., & Bjork, R. A. (2013). Why interleaving enhances inductive learning: The roles of discrimination and retrieval. Memory & Cognition, 41(3), 392-402. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-012-0272-7 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC; Crossref check passed; Crossref lists 2012 as the online year). Three experiments.
  - Brunmair, M., & Richter, T. (2019), as in F1 (temporal-spacing and material-similarity analyses read in the full text).
  - Carvalho, P. F., & Goldstone, R. L. (2017). The sequence of study changes what information is attended to, encoded, and remembered during category learning. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 43(11), 1699-1719. https://doi.org/10.1037/xlm0000406 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Three experiments.
- Strength: moderate to strong. The mechanism is supported by several independent labs and a meta-analytic moderator test, though the process evidence comes from artificial and visual categories.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The 2019 meta-analysis found no reliable difference between showing items together and showing them one after another (g = 0.44 against 0.29, difference not significant), so the comparison does not need a split screen. The temporal-spacing comparison rests on only 17 comparisons. The mechanism implies that the benefit is specific to the discrimination goal: Carvalho and Goldstone state that which sequence is better depends on whether the test needs the discriminating features or the characteristic ones.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Put the look-alike outcome directly after the one being taught, and name the difference in words in the same breath ("the first case turned on X; this one turns on Y"). Do not insert an unrelated explanation between two cases that are meant to be compared. Feedback on an interleaved item should say what separates the right answer from its most tempting neighbour, because that difference is the thing the interleaving brings into view. The determination key is itself an explicit discrimination procedure, so each question should be taught as "the feature that differs between these outcomes", in the same words the question uses.

### F3. Blocking can win: when categories are dissimilar or easy to tell apart, when study is passive, for some verbal material, and possibly for a learner's first look at a category.
- What the research shows: In three experiments, interleaved study gave better generalisation for categories whose members were similar to each other and to the other category, while blocked study gave better generalisation for categories low in both kinds of similarity. A second set of experiments found that whether blocking or interleaving did better depended on whether participants had to work out the category themselves (active) or were given the label with each item (passive), consistent with passive study and blocking focusing attention on what members share and active study and interleaving focusing it on what differs. A crossover study found that blocking helped categories learned through explicit hypothesis-testing ("rule-based") while interleaving helped categories learned through implicit, procedural integration of information. In a verbal-category experiment, participants whose first exposure was blocked did better on both implicit and explicit tests than interleaved participants, and the paper also tested a gradual blocked-to-interleaved transition. A separate experiment found interleaving helped when the categories were hard to discriminate and massing helped when they were easy to discriminate. The 2019 meta-analysis found the reversal for words reported in F1.
- Sources:
  - Carvalho, P. F., & Goldstone, R. L. (2014). Putting category learning in order: Category structure and temporal arrangement affect the benefit of interleaved over blocked study. Memory & Cognition, 42(3), 481-495. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-013-0371-0 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Three experiments.
  - Carvalho, P. F., & Goldstone, R. L. (2015). The benefits of interleaved and blocked study: Different tasks benefit from different schedules of study. Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 22(1), 281-288. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-014-0676-4 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Two experiments.
  - Noh, S. M., Yan, V. X., Bjork, R. A., & Maddox, W. T. (2016). Optimal sequencing during category learning: Testing a dual-learning systems perspective. Cognition, 155, 23-29. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2016.06.007 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Experiments.
  - Sorensen, L. J., & Woltz, D. J. (2016). Blocking as a friend of induction in verbal category learning. Memory & Cognition, 44(7), 1000-1013. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-016-0615-x . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Experiment. The abstract reports that blocked-first groups did better; I did not read how the gradual-transition group fared.
  - Zulkiply, N., & Burt, J. S. (2013). The exemplar interleaving effect in inductive learning: Moderation by the difficulty of category discriminations. Memory & Cognition, 41(1), 16-27. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-012-0238-9 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Two experiments.
  - Brunmair, M., & Richter, T. (2019), as in F1.
- Strength: moderate. Each moderator has been shown in at least one careful study, but the conditions come from laboratory categories (artificial or natural images, word sets), and the verbal-category result is a single study.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: These results do not say blocking beats interleaving for a learner meeting a confusable pair for the first time. "Rule-based" in the crossover study is defined by which learning system is doing the work, in laboratory categories (I read only the abstract, not the stimuli); a multi-question key over real cases is a different thing, so mapping that result onto this app's rule-like key is a stretch. All of this sits against F1's larger and more consistent evidence for confusable categories.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Do not interleave everything. For outcomes that look different, a short stretch focused on one outcome is fine and cheaper. For first exposure, let the card introduce each outcome on its own with the features that are characteristic of it (blocked study encodes those, F2), then move to mixed practice among the outcomes that are easy to confuse. Reserve the mixed drills for the confusable pairs rather than the whole outcome list. The order "one outcome at a time, then contrast, then mix" is a design inference that fits Carvalho and Goldstone's mechanism and the verbal-category result, not something I found tested end to end on adults with abstract categories.

### F4. Learners misjudge this: they feel blocked practice works better, even after their own results show the opposite.
- What the research shows: In Kornell and Bjork's experiments, 78% of participants did better with mixed study than with blocked study, and 78% said blocked study was as good or better; across two experiments, 85% did at least as well in the mixed condition and 83% rated blocked as equally or more effective. Yan, Bjork and Bjork traced the illusion to a feeling of fluent learning during blocked study plus a prior belief that grouping by topic is better. Explaining the illusion and pointing out the link between schedule and score had only modest effects; most learners changed their minds only when their test performance under each schedule was shown separately and both experience and explanation were given. In a university physics course, students who did interleaved homework for eight weeks gave more correct solutions on two surprise tests (observed median improvements of 50% and 125%), yet rated the technique as harder and believed they had learned less.
- Sources:
  - Kornell, N., & Bjork, R. A. (2008), as in F1.
  - Yan, V. X., Bjork, E. L., & Bjork, R. A. (2016). On the difficulty of mending metacognitive illusions: A priori theories, fluency effects, and misattributions of the interleaving benefit. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 145(7), 918-933. https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000177 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Six experiments (participants not described in the abstract).
  - Samani, J., & Pan, S. C. (2021). Interleaved practice enhances memory and problem-solving ability in undergraduate physics. npj Science of Learning, 6, Article 32. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-021-00110-x . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Field experiment in two lecture sections.
- Strength: strong for the illusion. Weak for how to fix it, because the only successful remedy in Yan et al. was an elaborate disambiguation procedure.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The illusion was shown mostly in lab and course settings with undergraduates. It says nothing direct about whether learners quit an app that feels harder.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Do not treat a learner's sense that a drill felt easy, or their confidence rating, as evidence that the drill is working, and do not let people opt into blocked-only practice by default. Mixed drills will feel harder than unit drills, so the card that introduces them should say that this is intended and why, and the result screen should show the learner's actual accuracy on mixed items. A single honest sentence is cheap, but the evidence says it will not by itself change the feeling.

### F5. When the skill is "choose the method or type from the case itself", interleaved practice beats blocked practice in real classes, including a randomised trial.
- What the research shows: Rohrer and colleagues give 7th-grade classes the same practice problems arranged in blocks or interleaved so that no two consecutive problems use the same strategy. Interleaving makes students pick a strategy from the problem itself, as an exam does, instead of knowing the strategy from the preceding lesson. In a 2015 experiment with 126 students over three months, interleaved practice beat blocked practice on tests one day later (d = 0.42) and 30 days later (d = 0.79). A preregistered cluster randomised trial of 54 classes over four months gave 61% against 38% on an unannounced test one month later (d = 0.83), with teachers using it without training. University physics (F4) and the mathematics subgroup of the 2019 meta-analysis (g = 0.34) point the same way.
- Sources:
  - Rohrer, D., Dedrick, R. F., & Stershic, S. (2015). Interleaved practice improves mathematics learning. Journal of Educational Psychology, 107(3), 900-908. https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000001 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via OpenAlex). Experiment, seventh graders.
  - Rohrer, D., Dedrick, R. F., Hartwig, M. K., & Cheung, C.-N. (2020). A randomized controlled trial of interleaved mathematics practice. Journal of Educational Psychology, 112(1), 40-52. https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000367 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via OpenAlex). Cluster randomised controlled trial, seventh graders.
  - Samani and Pan (2021) and Brunmair and Richter (2019), as above.
- Strength: moderate to strong for school mathematics and physics, from a research group that champions the technique; adult self-study on a phone has not been tested.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Mathematics is the one non-visual material with a reliable benefit in the 2019 meta-analysis and its effect was smaller than for paintings. The children's results may not transfer to adults, and a typical blocked problem set is about carrying out a procedure, whereas choosing the method is the thing being rehearsed in these interleaved sets.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: This is the closest published match to the app's central move: the first step in the key (the gate question) is the choice the learner must make unaided in real life. For Basic Math and Statistical Claims especially, practice sets must include items where the learner first has to decide which kind of problem or claim this is. A set where every item is "use the idea from the card you just read" practises the wrong skill.

## B. Comparison and contrasting cases

### F6. Comparing two cases side by side teaches the shared structure better than studying the same cases one after the other.
- What the research shows: Gentner, Loewenstein and Thompson ran three studies with novices learning negotiation strategies. Studying cases helped relative to no cases, comparing two cases helped markedly more than studying the same two separately, and giving more support for the comparison raised the rate at which the strategy was used later in a live, face-to-face negotiation. In a companion study, graduate management students who drew an analogy between two cases were nearly three times more likely to use the trained strategy in their own negotiations than students who had the same two cases separately. A meta-analysis of 57 experiments (336 tests) found that case-comparison activities led to better learning than sequential study, single cases, non-analogous cases, traditional instruction or no instruction (d = 0.50, 95% CI 0.44 to 0.56). The classic demonstration: when people read one story that contained a solution to a problem, instructions to summarise it, a statement of the principle, or a diagram did not get them to see the analogy in a new problem, but the same statement or diagram helped a lot when it was paired with two stories, and the quality of the schema people formed predicted how well they transferred.
- Sources:
  - Gentner, D., Loewenstein, J., & Thompson, L. (2003). Learning and transfer: A general role for analogical encoding. Journal of Educational Psychology, 95(2), 393-408. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.95.2.393 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via OpenAlex; Crossref check passed). Three experiments, novices (the abstract gives no further description).
  - Loewenstein, J., Thompson, L., & Gentner, D. (1999). Analogical encoding facilitates knowledge transfer in negotiation. Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 6(4), 586-597. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03212967 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Two experiments, undergraduates and graduate management students.
  - Alfieri, L., Nokes-Malach, T. J., & Schunn, C. D. (2013). Learning through case comparisons: A meta-analytic review. Educational Psychologist, 48(2), 87-113. https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2013.775712 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via OpenAlex; Crossref check passed). Meta-analysis.
  - Gick, M. L., & Holyoak, K. J. (1983). Schema induction and analogical transfer. Cognitive Psychology, 15(1), 1-38. https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(83)90002-6 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in the University of Michigan repository record, reached through web search; Crossref check passed). Experiments (participants not described in the excerpt I read).
- Strength: strong. A meta-analysis and several independent experiments agree, including experiments with adult novices.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The meta-analysis found that the benefit depends on how the comparison is run (F7), and larger effects went with immediate rather than delayed tests, so durability is the weaker part. Most of the adult studies are in negotiation, problem-solving analogies and school mathematics, not in classifying real-world political, psychological or fraud cases.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: The worked cases in the cards should come in pairs, not singly, and the learner should be told to compare them. One pair shows two different-looking cases that belong to the same outcome; another shows two look-alike cases that belong to different outcomes. A principle or definition stated about a single case, with no second case to compare, is exactly the single-story condition that failed in the classic study.

### F7. Comparison only works when the learner is pointed at the right thing, and the best kind of comparison depends on what the learner already knows.
- What the research shows: The meta-analysis of case comparison identified four moderators out of fifteen: the objective of the comparison, whether a principle was presented, the content, and the lag before testing. Asking learners to find similarities, giving the principle after the comparison, using perceptual content and testing immediately were each associated with larger effects. In an experiment with 162 seventh and eighth graders learning to solve equations, comparing different solution methods on the same problem built the most conceptual knowledge and flexibility, and comparing different problem types helped somewhat less. A later experiment with 236 students found that the order flipped for students who did not already use algebra: they learned more from studying examples one after another or comparing problem types, and only students who had some algebra learned more from comparing methods. In a radiology experiment with 84 medical students, comparing two cases of the same disease and comparing cases of different diseases both gave the most efficient learning, yet eye tracking showed the students compared normal anatomy in 45.8% of trials and made few comparisons between abnormalities.
- Sources:
  - Alfieri, L., Nokes-Malach, T. J., & Schunn, C. D. (2013), as in F6.
  - Rittle-Johnson, B., & Star, J. R. (2009). Compared with what? The effects of different comparisons on conceptual knowledge and procedural flexibility for equation solving. Journal of Educational Psychology, 101(3), 529-544. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0014224 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via OpenAlex). Experiment, 162 seventh and eighth graders.
  - Rittle-Johnson, B., Star, J. R., & Durkin, K. (2009). The importance of prior knowledge when comparing examples: Influences on conceptual and procedural knowledge of equation solving. Journal of Educational Psychology, 101(4), 836-852. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016026 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via OpenAlex). Experiment, 236 seventh and eighth graders.
  - Kok, E. M., de Bruin, A. B. H., Leppink, J., van Merrienboer, J. J. G., & Robben, S. G. F. (2015). Case comparisons: An efficient way of learning radiology. Academic Radiology, 22(10), 1226-1235. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.acra.2015.04.012 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Randomised experiment, medical students.
- Strength: moderate. The four meta-analytic moderators are cross-study contrasts and can be confounded; the prior-knowledge result and the radiology result each come from one study.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: "Find the similarities" is a moderator in the case-comparison meta-analysis, whereas the interleaving literature (F2) says the benefit comes from seeing the differences between categories. These do not conflict if similarity-finding is applied to pairs from the same category and difference-finding to pairs from neighbouring categories (see "Tensions"). "Principle after comparison" is a cross-study moderator, not a head-to-head test against stating it first; the problem-first versus instruction-first question is handled in the presenting-new-material notes, not here. The prior-knowledge result is an expertise-reversal pattern: comparing methods needs some foundation.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Name the thing to compare. A pair of cases with a heading like "compare these two" and no instruction wastes the benefit; the card should say which feature to look at (the one the key tests) and ask one question about it. For a true beginner, put a same-outcome pair (different surface, same decisive feature) before a look-alike pair that belongs to different outcomes. This is an inference by analogy: in the equation-solving experiments, low-knowledge students did better comparing problem types that share a method than comparing methods, and nobody has tested the analogous order on classification. State the rule in plain words after the comparison, using the same words the key and the drill use.

### F8. Contrasting cases can prepare a learner to get more out of an explanation, but "work it out before being told" is contested and is risky for novices studying alone.
- What the research shows: Schwartz and Bransford had students analyse contrasting cases (simplified data sets from classic psychology experiments, which they were asked to turn into graphs); these students learned and transferred more from a later lecture than students who first summarised a chapter and then heard the same lecture. The authors' reading is that telling works well once learners have differentiated knowledge to attach it to, and badly when they have not. In two experiments with adolescents learning physics (N = 128 and N = 120), students who invented formulas from contrasting cases before being told did as well as tell-and-practice students at applying the formulas, but understood the ratio structure better and transferred more often to unrelated topics with the same structure, and this held for lower and higher achievers. A large meta-analysis of discovery learning found that unassisted discovery was worse than explicit instruction (d = -0.38 over 580 comparisons) whereas enhanced discovery, with feedback, worked examples, scaffolding or elicited explanations, was better than other instruction (d = 0.30 over 360 comparisons). A 2021 meta-analysis of problem-solving-before-instruction designs (53 studies) found a moderate advantage (g = 0.36) but a reversal for younger learners and for domain-general skills.
- Sources:
  - Schwartz, D. L., & Bransford, J. D. (1998). A time for telling. Cognition and Instruction, 16(4), 475-522. https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1604_4 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in a search-result excerpt from repository pages; Crossref check passed; weaker than the others). Experiments with students (the excerpt does not say more).
  - Schwartz, D. L., Chase, C. C., Oppezzo, M. A., & Chin, D. B. (2011). Practicing versus inventing with contrasting cases: The effects of telling first on learning and transfer. Journal of Educational Psychology, 103(4), 759-775. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025140 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via OpenAlex). Two experiments, adolescents.
  - Alfieri, L., Brooks, P. J., Aldrich, N. J., & Tenenbaum, H. R. (2011). Does discovery-based instruction enhance learning? Journal of Educational Psychology, 103(1), 1-18. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021017 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in the paper's text; Crossref check passed). Meta-analysis.
  - Sinha, T., & Kapur, M. (2021). When problem solving followed by instruction works: Evidence for productive failure. Review of Educational Research, 91(5), 761-798. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Crossref). Meta-analysis.
- Strength: contested. Contrasting cases as material is well supported (F6). Putting them before the explanation helps in some designs and loses to explicit instruction in others, and none of the studies I read about tested self-study.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The adolescent physics studies compared invention with tell-and-practice and found equal procedural performance, so the gain was in deep structure and transfer, not in getting answers right. Unassisted discovery lost across a very large set of comparisons. None of these studies used adults learning alone on a phone; as far as the abstracts show, they were run with classes or in lab sessions, where someone can consolidate afterwards.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Use contrasting cases as the material of the lesson, but follow the safer order for a phone: explain the idea and show it applied, then present contrasting cases with a specific question, then name the difference. A short "predict the outcome before you see it" step on a contrasting pair is defensible as a light version of the invention designs, as long as the next card spells out the answer. Do not make an unguided puzzle the opening of a unit.

## C. What the learner ends up knowing: structure versus surface, variety, concreteness

### F9. Novices sort cases by their surface features and experts by the underlying principle; examples that emphasise structure move novices toward the expert pattern.
- What the research shows: In the classic comparison of physics experts and novices, experts started from the principle that applied, while novices built their representation from the literal features of the problem. In statistics, college students who studied example problems grouped by solution type (t test, correlation, chi-square) were more likely to sort new problems by structure and less by cover story than students who studied no examples; the effect was strongest when the examples emphasised structure. After studying and practising four worked examples that emphasised structure, students were more likely to pick the right statistical test for new word problems than students who had studied examples emphasising surface; that held for lower-ability but not higher-ability students. A follow-up taught students to sort problems by structure rather than cover story, and a preliminary study showed students become more structure-aware over a first statistics course.
- Sources:
  - Chi, M. T. H., Feltovich, P. J., & Glaser, R. (1981). Categorization and representation of physics problems by experts and novices. Cognitive Science, 5(2), 121-152. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0502_2 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Crossref). Experiments, experts and novices.
  - Quilici, J. L., & Mayer, R. E. (1996). Role of examples in how students learn to categorize statistics word problems. Journal of Educational Psychology, 88(1), 144-161. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.88.1.144 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via OpenAlex). Three experiments, college students.
  - Quilici, J. L., & Mayer, R. E. (2002). Teaching students to recognize structural similarities between statistics word problems. Applied Cognitive Psychology, 16(3), 325-342. https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.796 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Crossref). Experiments, college students.
- Strength: moderate. Replicated pattern in physics and statistics, but a small number of studies and mainly sorting tasks and word problems rather than free recognition in the wild.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The Quilici and Mayer benefit was weak for higher-ability students in one experiment. Sorting is a proxy for classifying; it is not the same as naming a case under time pressure.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: This is the nearest published match to the app's skill. Specimens and drill cases should vary in topic and cover story while the decisive structural feature stays fixed, and the "why" text should name that feature, not retell the story. The lesson should teach, in words, what counts as the structural feature and what counts as surface for this subject (for example, in Statistical Claims, what is being compared with what, as against whether the claim is about health or money). A learner who is never told which kind of feature to look at will default to the topic.

### F10. Varied examples help people carry an idea to new situations, but only once they can see the structure; people with little knowledge do better first with examples that look alike.
- What the research shows: In two experiments on a mathematical concept, learners with less prior knowledge learned better from superficially similar examples, whereas learners with more prior knowledge learned better from varied ones; when participants first received brief training that drew attention to the underlying relations, they too benefited from varied examples. In technical problem-solving training, students who studied worked examples gained most from high-variability examples, spent less time and mental effort in practice, and transferred better than students who first attempted problems. A 2022 review across many domains concludes that more variability tends to make early learning harder but eventually produces more general and robust performance, and it separates variability on dimensions that matter to the task from variability on dimensions that do not.
- Sources:
  - Braithwaite, D. W., & Goldstone, R. L. (2015). Effects of variation and prior knowledge on abstract concept learning. Cognition and Instruction, 33(3), 226-256. https://doi.org/10.1080/07370008.2015.1067215 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via OpenAlex). Two experiments (participants not described in the abstract).
  - Paas, F. G. W. C., & Van Merrienboer, J. J. G. (1994). Variability of worked examples and transfer of geometrical problem-solving skills: A cognitive-load approach. Journal of Educational Psychology, 86(1), 122-133. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.86.1.122 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via OpenAlex). Experiment, technical training.
  - Raviv, L., Lupyan, G., & Green, S. C. (2022). How variability shapes learning and generalization. Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 26(6), 462-483. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2022.03.007 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Review.
- Strength: moderate. The direction (variety helps eventually, hurts early for low-knowledge learners) is consistent, and the review ties together a large literature, but the key study of the novice reversal uses a mathematical concept in a lab.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: A learner who cannot yet tell which dimension carries the structure experiences variety as noise (the novice reversal above), and the review says variability makes initial learning harder, so it should be introduced at a point the learner can handle. The conditions under which pre-training attention to structure is enough to unlock variety have been tested in one lab.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Sequence the cases inside a unit: first, a case or two where the decisive feature is clear and the story is simple; then the same feature in a very different story; only later, the cases where the story misleads. Do not open a unit with the widest possible spread of cases for a novice, and do not stop at the first, tidy version either, because variety on topic and context is what lets the idea travel. Tell the learner which dimension is meant to stay constant (the decisive feature) and which is meant to change (the topic, the people, the setting).

### F11. The task you practise shapes what you learn: practice that asks only "which category is this?" narrows attention to the features that separate the categories, at a cost to knowing what each category is like.
- What the research shows: Markman and Ross reviewed experiments comparing classification learning with a formally equivalent task of predicting a missing feature from the category, and found that the two produce very different learning. Hoffman and Rehder found that people who learned by classification focused on diagnostic features, whereas people who learned by inferring missing features acquired more information about what is typical within the category; in two experiments classification learners were worse at drawing a distinction that was not part of their training, because their attention had narrowed and they were slow to shift it to newly relevant information. Carvalho and Goldstone's sequence experiments (F2) point to the same trade-off from another angle: interleaved study favours encoding of discriminating properties and blocked study favours encoding of characteristic ones.
- Sources:
  - Markman, A. B., & Ross, B. H. (2003). Category use and category learning. Psychological Bulletin, 129(4), 592-613. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.129.4.592 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Review.
  - Hoffman, A. B., & Rehder, B. (2010). The costs of supervised classification: The effect of learning task on conceptual flexibility. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 139(2), 319-340. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019042 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Two experiments with eye tracking.
  - Carvalho, P. F., & Goldstone, R. L. (2017), as in F2.
- Strength: moderate. A review plus several laboratory experiments with artificial categories; I found no test in real-world conceptual domains.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Most of this comes from laboratory categories with artificial features. In everyday domains with rich prior knowledge the narrowing may be less severe, and I did not find a study that tests it.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: The app's core practice is classification through diagnostic questions, which is the task that narrows attention. Cards need to teach what each outcome is in general (its typical features, its logic, what it looks like in the wild) so that the learner is not left with only the separating features. A few tasks should run the other way: given the outcome, what would you expect to see in a case, or which of these details does not fit? Real cases that fall outside the key (hybrids, mixtures) are the "novel distinctions" this research predicts classification-only learners will miss, so the caveats section earns its place and should be taught, not appended.

### F12. For recognising messy real cases, starting from clean, unambiguous cases can beat training on a representative mix, and remembering specific past cases helps novices with atypical ones.
- What the research shows: Hornsby and Love trained people to classify mammograms as normal or tumorous. One group trained only on cases that a norming study had rated unambiguous, the other on a representative range. The idealised group was more accurate when tested on a range of cases, though it struggled with test cases very unlike its training cases; the authors attribute the benefit to reduced noise in what gets retrieved from memory. In a small study of preclinical medical students diagnosing skin conditions, adopting a similarity-based strategy (reminding oneself of previously seen cases) improved performance, especially on atypical cases, whereas residents improved by adopting an analytic strategy, which suggests the best strategy shifts with expertise.
- Sources:
  - Hornsby, A. N., & Love, B. C. (2014). Improved classification of mammograms following idealized training. Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition, 3(2), 72-76. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jarmac.2014.04.009 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Experiment.
  - Kulatunga-Moruzi, C., Brooks, L. R., & Norman, G. R. (2001). Coordination of analytic and similarity-based processing strategies and expertise in dermatological diagnosis. Teaching and Learning in Medicine, 13(2), 110-116. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15328015tlm1302_6 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Experiment, 12 preclinical students plus residents.
- Strength: limited. One experiment with one kind of image and one very small study, though they agree with the structure-first ordering in F10.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The idealisation advantage failed for very dissimilar test cases, so clean training does not remove the need to meet messy cases. It was tested on a binary visual task, not on multi-way classification of conceptual cases. The preclinical result has 12 participants.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Within each unit, order the specimens from clean to messy, and teach the clean case as the anchor ("this is what X looks like when nothing gets in the way"). Do not leave out the messy ones: they are the point of the app, and they should be taught as named, remembered cases ("this is the one that looks like Y but is X, because...") so the learner has something specific to be reminded of. The balance between clean and messy has been tested in only one domain, so treat the ordering as a starting design to check in use.

### F13. Starting with a concrete case and then explicitly fading to the generic form tends to carry ideas further than either alone.
- What the research shows: In two experiments, participants interacted with simulations of a scientific principle whose elements stayed concrete, stayed idealised, or switched midway. Transfer to a second simulation governed by the same principle was better when the appearance switched, and best when originally concrete elements became idealised. A systematic review in mathematics and science instruction recommends beginning with concrete material and then explicitly, gradually fading to the abstract, listing four reasons: concrete objects make abstract symbols interpretable, they ground thinking in perceptual experience, they leave memorable images to fall back on, and the fade strips away the irrelevant concrete detail. In a children's mathematics experiment (second and third graders), the fading condition did well on early problems and on the transfer test, while concrete-only did worst on transfer.
- Sources:
  - Goldstone, R. L., & Son, J. Y. (2005). The transfer of scientific principles using concrete and idealized simulations. Journal of the Learning Sciences, 14(1), 69-110. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327809jls1401_4 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via OpenAlex). Two experiments (participants not described in the abstract).
  - Fyfe, E. R., McNeil, N. M., Son, J. Y., & Goldstone, R. L. (2014). Concreteness fading in mathematics and science instruction: A systematic review. Educational Psychology Review, 26(1), 9-25. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-014-9249-3 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in a search-result excerpt of a repository page; Crossref check passed). Systematic review.
  - Fyfe, E. R., McNeil, N. M., & Borjas, S. (2015). Benefits of "concreteness fading" for children's mathematics understanding. Learning and Instruction, 35, 104-120. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2014.10.004 . VERIFIED-ONLINE for existence (Crossref); findings read only in a secondary summary of the paper from a search result. Experiment, children.
  - Jaakkola, T., & Veermans, K. (2018). Exploring the effects of concreteness fading across grades in elementary school science education. Instructional Science, 46(2), 185-207. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-017-9428-y . VERIFIED-ONLINE for existence (Crossref); the counter-finding below was read only in a search-result excerpt of the abstract. Experiments, elementary-school pupils.
- Strength: moderate. A consistent theoretical story and several experiments, mostly in mathematics or simulations, with a counter-example below.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The elementary school science study found that learning with concrete representations throughout either took less time or produced better learning than fading, and that the concrete condition did at least as well on transfer, so the authors call for caution with young learners. Everything here is about mathematics or science representations; I found no study of fading with real-world conceptual cases like ideologies or scams.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Within a unit, the order a novice meets an idea should be: a concrete, memorable real case; then the same case with its story stripped back to the decisive features; then the rule stated in plain words; then a new case in a different story for the learner to classify. The stripped-back form is the fading step and the new case is the transfer test. A bare definition as the first thing on a card is the order this research argues against for beginners.

### F14. Abstract-only teaching is not supported for the app's goal, because what transfers is tied to the form in which it was learned.
- What the research shows: Kaminski, Sloutsky and Heckler reported in Science that undergraduates may benefit more from learning a piece of mathematics through one abstract symbolic representation than through several concrete examples. A partial replication with an elaboration found that the results held, but its qualitative data raised serious questions about what the students had actually learned from the abstract version; students who learned from the abstract version transferred to a similar abstract context, and students who learned from concrete examples transferred to a similar concrete context. The original authors' later account argues that extraneous detail in concrete versions can distract from structure and hinder transfer, while generic notation such as standard mathematical symbols can be learned by children and adults and does transfer.
- Sources:
  - Kaminski, J. A., Sloutsky, V. M., & Heckler, A. F. (2008). The advantage of abstract examples in learning math. Science, 320(5875), 454-455. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1154659 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (one-sentence summary read via OpenAlex; Crossref check passed; the paper itself not read). Experiments, undergraduates.
  - De Bock, D., Deprez, J., Van Dooren, W., Roelens, M., & Verschaffel, L. (2011). Abstract or concrete examples in learning mathematics? A replication and elaboration of Kaminski, Sloutsky, and Heckler's study. Journal for Research in Mathematics Education, 42(2), 109-126. https://doi.org/10.5951/jresematheduc.42.2.0109 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Crossref). Replication and extension.
  - Kaminski, J. A., Sloutsky, V. M., & Heckler, A. F. (2009). Transfer of mathematical knowledge: The portability of generic instantiations. Child Development Perspectives, 3(3), 151-155. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1750-8606.2009.00096.x . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Crossref). Review.
- Strength: contested. The original result replicated but its interpretation was challenged, and it concerns a small piece of formal mathematics.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Both sides agree that what was learned from an abstract form transferred to a similar abstract form. That is a statement about the match between learning and use, not about abstract being better in general. The result does not address recognising non-formal real-world cases at all.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: The target of transfer in this app is concrete: untidy real cases that arrive without labels. So the lesson cannot be abstract alone, and the abstract statement of the decisive feature ("it turns on X") has to be paired with several concrete cases of different kinds so that it can travel. Where the two research positions agree is that generic wording is portable and concrete wording is grounding; the fading sequence in F13 uses both.

## D. Explaining: self-explanation, explanation prompts and "why this and not that"

### F15. Prompting learners to explain material to themselves improves learning and transfer, on average by a medium amount.
- What the research shows: A meta-analysis of 64 reports (69 effect sizes) on prompting learners to self-explain while studying or solving problems found a mean effect of g = 0.55, with benefits across learning tasks (solving problems, studying worked examples, studying text), subjects and outcome types. By education level the mean effects were about 0.48 for elementary pupils, 0.43 for high school, 0.61 for undergraduates and 0.68 for professional programmes. Outcome-type averages were close to each other: about 0.50 for recall, 0.45 for problem solving and 0.53 for transfer. Self-explanation also beat receiving an instructor's explanation by about g = 0.35, but the benefit over no extra explanation (g = 0.67) means a good share of the gain is also available from well-written explanations. The early experiments: students who explained worked examples of mechanics problems as they studied them understood the principles better, and eighth graders prompted to explain after each line of a text on the circulatory system gained more than students who read it twice (14 prompted students against 10 controls, a small study).
- Sources:
  - Bisra, K., Liu, Q., Nesbit, J. C., Salimi, F., & Winne, P. H. (2018). Inducing self-explanation: A meta-analysis. Educational Psychology Review, 30(3), 703-725. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x . VERIFIED-ONLINE (full text read; Crossref check passed). Meta-analysis.
  - Chi, M. T. H., Bassok, M., Lewis, M. W., Reimann, P., & Glaser, R. (1989). Self-explanations: How students study and use examples in learning to solve problems. Cognitive Science, 13(2), 145-182. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1302_1 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Crossref). Observational comparison of good and poor students.
  - Chi, M. T. H., de Leeuw, N., Chiu, M.-H., & LaVancher, C. (1994). Eliciting self-explanations improves understanding. Cognitive Science, 18(3), 439-477. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1803_3 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Crossref). Experiment, eighth graders.
- Strength: strong for the average effect; the meta-analysis tested for publication bias with two methods and judged it not a plausible threat.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Dunlosky and colleagues rate self-explanation as moderate utility because it has not been tested enough in real educational settings. The meta-analysis found no benefit that was reliably different from zero for comprehension outcomes (three effect sizes) or for essay-format tests, and prompts in studies conducted in East Asia produced much larger effects than elsewhere (probably a mix of design features). Most studies are lab or class experiments. The Chi 1989 comparison of good and poor students is correlational: the good students explained more, and it does not alone show that explaining caused the difference.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: After a worked case, ask the learner for their reasoning before showing the explanation ("what in the case points to X rather than Y?"), because explanation the learner generates is worth more than the same explanation they only read. Put the question on the step that carries the idea, not at the end of every card.

### F16. How self-explanation is prompted matters: content-focused prompts work, on-demand explanations and multiple-choice prompts may undercut it, and it takes time.
- What the research shows: In the meta-analysis, prompts asking learners to explain concepts, justify choices or explain parts of the material all produced reliable benefits; prompts about the learner's own planning or performance (metacognitive prompts) did not, and conceptual prompts did better than metacognitive ones. The two studies that used multiple-choice prompts averaged g = 0.24, not reliably different from zero, though the authors urge caution with so few studies; they suggest strongly cued prompts may suit beginners and less cued ones later. The same paper reports (from Schworm and Renkl) that learners who were prompted to explain did better when an instructor's explanation was not available on request, because availability reduced the effort put into explaining. Self-explanation usually takes longer. The effect was bigger when it took more time (g = 0.72) than when time was similar (g = 0.41), but the difference was not statistically reliable and most studies did not report time. A time-matched comparison with 69 children in grades 2 to 4 found self-explanation beat a control but gave only modest and patchy gains over solving extra practice problems. A synthesis of the mathematics literature proposes four constraints: it promotes attention to particular types of information, explaining one's own solution method or choice can sometimes reduce learning, prompts pull effort from other material, and other techniques may beat it. In medical education, students who first explained cases themselves and then listened to residents' explanations of the same kind of case, with prompts, did better on different cases one week later than a control (54 third-year students). In a study of worked examples, prompts to identify the principle behind each step, combined with gradually removing steps, produced medium to large effects on near and far transfer without extra time.
- Sources:
  - Bisra et al. (2018), as in F15.
  - Rittle-Johnson, B., & Loehr, A. M. (2017). Eliciting explanations: Constraints on when self-explanation aids learning. Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 24(5), 1501-1510. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-016-1079-5 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Review.
  - McEldoon, K. L., Durkin, K. L., & Rittle-Johnson, B. (2013). Is self-explanation worth the time? A comparison to additional practice. British Journal of Educational Psychology, 83(4), 615-632. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8279.2012.02083.x . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Experiment, 69 children.
  - Chamberland, M., Mamede, S., St-Onge, C., Setrakian, J., Bergeron, L., & Schmidt, H. (2015). Self-explanation in learning clinical reasoning: The added value of examples and prompts. Medical Education, 49(2), 193-202. https://doi.org/10.1111/medu.12623 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Randomised experiment, third-year medical students.
  - Atkinson, R. K., Renkl, A., & Merrill, M. M. (2003). Transitioning from studying examples to solving problems: Effects of self-explanation prompts and fading worked-out steps. Journal of Educational Psychology, 95(4), 774-783. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.95.4.774 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via OpenAlex). Two experiments.
- Strength: moderate. The design details come from smaller studies, and the multiple-choice and on-demand-explanation results rest on very few.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The time-cost comparison I could read most closely involved children in a short session. The medical and example-fading studies involve guided examples, not a bare prompt. No study here tested typing an explanation on a phone, which has an effort cost the lab studies did not face.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Prompts should target the content ("why does this case go to X and not to Y?"), not the learner's feelings about their understanding ("how confident are you?"). Show the model explanation after the learner has committed to an answer or written a reason, not as an optional hint they can open first. Since multiple-choice explanation prompts are unproven, a short free reply is the better-supported format, with the cost on a phone kept low by asking for one sentence. Pairing the learner's own reasoning with an expert's worked reasoning, as in the medical study, is supported.

### F17. Explaining pushes learners to look for a general pattern, which helps discovery of the rule and hurts learning when the category has exceptions.
- What the research shows: In experiments with artificial categories, learners prompted to explain why items belonged to their categories were more likely to discover a broad regularity underlying category membership than learners who described items, thought aloud or studied freely, whereas describing helped more with memory for the details of individual items. Explaining anomalous observations may play a special part in revising beliefs. In two further category-learning experiments, across artifact and social domains, the same property of explanation led learners to overgeneralise when the pattern had exceptions, so they learned the exceptions worse.
- Sources:
  - Williams, J. J., & Lombrozo, T. (2010). The role of explanation in discovery and generalization: Evidence from category learning. Cognitive Science, 34(5), 776-806. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1551-6709.2010.01113.x . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Three experiments.
  - Williams, J. J., Lombrozo, T., & Rehder, B. (2013). The hazards of explanation: Overgeneralization in the face of exceptions. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 142(4), 1006-1014. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030996 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Two experiments.
- Strength: moderate. Two sets of controlled experiments from one research group, using constructed categories.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The same property is a benefit or a hazard depending on the structure of what is being learned. The experiments used artificial categories, in artifact and social domains, not politics or fraud, and the populations were not described in the abstracts I read.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Explanation prompts will strengthen the learner's grasp of the decisive rule, which is what the key needs. For the outcomes that have well-known exceptions, hybrids or near-miss cases, a prompt to explain will tend to flatten them into the rule, so the lesson must teach the exceptions explicitly and prompt explanation of them separately ("why is this one X even though it has the feature that usually means Y?"). Where the aim is to remember details, such as what a specific outcome's typical claims are, ask the learner to describe or list them rather than to explain.

### F18. "Why would this be true of this one and not of the neighbouring one?" is a well-studied prompt, and it works better when the learner already knows something about the topic.
- What the research shows: Dunlosky and colleagues' review rates elaborative interrogation (answering "why" about a stated fact) as moderate utility. The prompt in most studies has the form "why would this fact be true of this X and not some other X?", which invites noticing both similarities and differences between related entities. Benefits appeared across a wide age range, but the effect generally grew with prior knowledge: students who answered questions about their own country's regions gained about 24% against about 12% for the region they knew less about. It was larger when the learner's elaborations were precise and self-generated than when they were provided, and the benefit for low-knowledge learners was described as less certain. Whether the effect depends on the quality of the reasons produced is still open. The review is explicit that the technique has been applied, and may be applicable, only to discrete units of factual information, that its effects are firmly established for memory of explicitly stated facts after short delays, and that only three studies measured comprehension or application.
- Sources:
  - Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques: Promising directions from cognitive and educational psychology. Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14(1), 4-58. https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (full text of the elaborative-interrogation section read; Crossref check passed). Review.
  - Woloshyn, V. E., Willoughby, T., Wood, E., & Pressley, M. (1990). Elaborative interrogation facilitates adult learning of factual paragraphs. Journal of Educational Psychology, 82(3), 513-524. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.82.3.513 . VERIFIED-ONLINE for existence and the first sentences of the abstract only (university students, factual paragraphs about five universities); no results read.
- Strength: moderate. A well-replicated technique with a modest utility rating, largely tested on facts.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The prior-knowledge dependence means a lesson that has not taught the facts first gives the prompt nothing to work with. The evidence is about memory for facts, not classification of cases, so the claim for this app is by analogy: the "this and not that" wording invites the same comparison that the discrimination studies (F2) say produces the benefit.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: The "why this and not that" form is the natural shape of the feedback that follows a specimen, and the fals text the app already has is a version of it. Make that wording a repeated, recognisable question in the app (same words each time), put it after the cards have supplied the facts, and have the learner attempt the reason before reading the model one.

## E. Teaching diagnostic classification: rules, pattern recognition and mixed practice

### F19. Teaching novices to use both a feature-by-feature check and an overall sense of resemblance to known cases beats teaching either alone.
- What the research shows: In a study of 48 novices learning to read electrocardiograms, learners who were told to compare each diagnosis with a confusable one (contrastive learning) and learners who were told to identify all features carefully while also trusting a feeling of familiarity (a combined strategy) both reached higher diagnostic accuracy than learners given no instructions, with no interaction between the two, and the benefits held after a week. In two further studies with psychology undergraduates trained on ten cardiac conditions, the combined instruction gave higher accuracy overall and especially so when the cases were made misleading, either by mentioning a wrong feature or by suggesting a wrong diagnosis. In dermatology, preclinical students did better by leaning on resemblance to past cases, especially for atypical cases, whereas residents did better with an analytic strategy (F12). An older line of experiments argues that earlier specific cases keep an important role in applying a rule to new material, even after the learner is no longer aware of them.
- Sources:
  - Ark, T. K., Brooks, L. R., & Eva, K. W. (2007). The benefits of flexibility: The pedagogical value of instructions to adopt multifaceted diagnostic reasoning strategies. Medical Education, 41(3), 281-287. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2929.2007.02688.x . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Experiment, 48 naive students.
  - Eva, K. W., Hatala, R. M., LeBlanc, V. R., & Brooks, L. R. (2007). Teaching from the clinical reasoning literature: Combined reasoning strategies help novice diagnosticians overcome misleading information. Medical Education, 41(12), 1152-1158. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2923.2007.02923.x . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Two studies, undergraduates.
  - Kulatunga-Moruzi, C., Brooks, L. R., & Norman, G. R. (2001), as in F12.
  - Allen, S. W., & Brooks, L. R. (1991). Specializing the operation of an explicit rule. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 120(1), 3-19. https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.120.1.3 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (opening of the abstract read via OpenAlex; I did not read the results). Experiments.
- Strength: moderate. Several experiments from the same research tradition, using ECGs and undergraduates or trainees; I found no replication in non-medical categories.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The studies used a visual diagnostic task in which a feature check and an overall impression are both available. For text cases the "overall impression" may be less reliable. The dermatology result shows that the best mix depends on expertise.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Teach both and say so: run the key's questions on the features, and also notice whether the case resembles ones you have seen; when the two disagree, recheck the feature that decided it. This answers the question the cluster asks, how to teach someone to tell categories apart by rule and recognise them in messy cases: neither alone is best. The app's scoring of name and route separately is consistent with this, since the route is the feature check and the name is the overall judgement; the research supports telling the learner that an overall impression is a useful signal, to be checked by the route and not ignored or trusted blindly.

### F20. How practice is arranged matters more than how the lesson is organised: mixed practice beat blocked practice for ECG diagnosis, features-first and category-first teaching tied, and short mixed classification trials with feedback speed up pattern recognition.
- What the research shows: In two experiments with medical students, instruction organised around features (such as voltage) and instruction organised around diagnostic categories (such as bundle branch blocks) gave no significant difference in accuracy on new ECGs. In the second experiment, with instruction held constant, practice that mixed examples from different categories gave 46% diagnostic accuracy against 30% for practice in single-category blocks. Perceptual and adaptive learning modules, which give short, speeded classification trials across many categories with feedback and adaptive repetition of items a learner finds hard, were reported to produce large and durable gains in medical trainees: in histopathology, 261 images, accuracy and speed improved from pre-test to post-test, and gains held at a 6 to 7 week test on unseen cases despite some decline in first-year students. A review by the research group that builds these modules reports large and durable gains from short interventions in medical trainees, and a mathematics version improved equation-solving speed and transfer in middle and high school.
- Sources:
  - Hatala, R. M., Brooks, L. R., & Norman, G. R. (2003). Practice makes perfect: The critical role of mixed practice in the acquisition of ECG interpretation skills. Advances in Health Sciences Education, 8(1), 17-26. https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1022687404380 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Two randomised experiments, medical students.
  - Krasne, S., Hillman, J. D., Kellman, P. J., & Drake, T. A. (2013). Applying perceptual and adaptive learning techniques for teaching introductory histopathology. Journal of Pathology Informatics, 4, 34. https://doi.org/10.4103/2153-3539.123991 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Pre-post study with delayed test, no control group in the abstract.
  - Kellman, P. J., & Krasne, S. (2018). Accelerating expertise: Perceptual and adaptive learning technology in medical learning. Medical Teacher, 40(8), 797-802. https://doi.org/10.1080/0142159X.2018.1484897 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Review by the developers.
  - Kellman, P. J., Massey, C. M., & Son, J. Y. (2010). Perceptual learning modules in mathematics: Enhancing students' pattern recognition, structure extraction, and fluency. Topics in Cognitive Science, 2(2), 285-305. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1756-8765.2009.01053.x . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Experiments, middle and high school students.
- Strength: moderate for the mixed-practice result and the features-versus-categories null (one report with two experiments); limited for the perceptual-learning-module claims because the evidence I could read is a pre-post design and the developers' own review.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The perceptual-learning evidence is for visual or structural pattern recognition (histology slides, equation structure). It does not show that the same trial format works for conceptual categories read from text. The mixed-practice result is from a single group and does not say how many mixed items per category are enough.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Do not spend effort arguing about whether a unit should be built around the key's questions or around the outcomes, because organising the explanation either way showed no difference in the one test I found; spend it on the practice schedule. The quickDrills format (many short items with a shared list of options) suits short mixed classification trials, and a learner's confusions (which two outcomes keep getting swapped) are the best candidates to bring back mixed together soon. The strongest claims about adaptive timing come from the developers, so use them as a design idea, not a proven effect.

### F21. Telling people to slow down or to watch out for their biases does not improve diagnosis; organised knowledge and structured comparison do.
- What the research shows: In a trial with 191 medical students, training in cognitive forcing strategies (named techniques meant to force a second look) did not change the rate of finding a second correct diagnosis or an uncommon one, with no significant differences between trained and untrained students. In a study of second-year residents tested in two cohorts, instructing participants to be quick or to be careful and reflective produced the same accuracy (44.5% against 45.0%), though the careful group took about 20 seconds longer per case. A narrative review concluded that teaching recognition of biases has not reduced diagnostic errors, whereas strategies that reorganise knowledge have small but consistent benefits. A systematic review of 28 studies of interventions for diagnostic reasoning found guided reflection to be the most consistently successful approach (five studies), with cognitive forcing strategies improving accuracy and confidence in some studies, and flagged heterogeneous measures and a sample mostly of early-career doctors.
- Sources:
  - Sherbino, J., Kulasegaram, K., Howey, E., & Norman, G. (2014). Ineffectiveness of cognitive forcing strategies to reduce biases in diagnostic reasoning: A controlled trial. CJEM, 16(1), 34-40. https://doi.org/10.2310/8000.2013.130860 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Controlled trial, medical students.
  - Norman, G. R., Sherbino, J., Dore, K., Wood, T., Young, M., Gaissmaier, W., et al. (2014). The etiology of diagnostic errors: A controlled trial of System 1 versus System 2 reasoning. Academic Medicine, 89(2), 277-284. https://doi.org/10.1097/ACM.0000000000000105 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Two cohorts of residents tested in successive years, not one randomised experiment.
  - Norman, G. R., Monteiro, S. D., Sherbino, J., Ilgen, J. S., Schmidt, H. G., & Mamede, S. (2017). The causes of errors in clinical reasoning: Cognitive biases, knowledge deficits, and dual process thinking. Academic Medicine, 92(1), 23-30. https://doi.org/10.1097/ACM.0000000000001421 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Narrative review.
  - Lambe, K. A., O'Reilly, G., Kelly, B. D., & Curristan, S. (2016). Dual-process cognitive interventions to enhance diagnostic reasoning: A systematic review. BMJ Quality & Safety, 25(10), 808-820. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2015-004417 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Systematic review.
- Strength: moderate. Direct tests of "slow down" and cognitive forcing are consistent, but the reviews disagree on how pessimistic to be.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The Lambe review found some positive results for cognitive forcing and calls the field promising, while the Sherbino trial and the two Norman papers found none. The mixed picture is explained by the difference between a generic reminder (no effect) and a structured step such as guided reflection (some effect). The two-cohort residents study is not randomised.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Do not rely on cards that say "slow down" or "beware confirmation bias" to produce better classifications. The route the key forces (check each narrowing question against the case) is a structured step, which is the kind the evidence leaves room for. Put the effort into teaching what each outcome is and how it differs from its neighbours (organised knowledge) and into practice that compares them.

## F. Carrying it into real situations and one's own life

### F22. Transfer across very different situations is rare and depends on specific conditions; nobody can promise "general critical thinking" from a lesson.
- What the research shows: Barnett and Ceci argued that a century of arguments over whether far transfer happens made no progress because studies differ along many dimensions; they set out a framework of nine, placed the literature in it, and concluded that evidence for transfer under some conditions is substantial, that a single effect size for far transfer is misguided, and that critical conditions for many key questions have not been tested. Sala and Gobet's meta-analyses of chess, music and working-memory training for children's cognitive and academic skills found small to moderate effects that shrank as the quality of the experimental design rose (for example, with active control groups), and concluded that far transfer of learning rarely occurs. Perkins and Salomon describe transfer in everyday life as often easy but in formal learning as much rarer than educators hope, and model it as three separate bridges, detecting a possible link to prior learning, choosing to pursue it, and working out the connection, with motivation and disposition driving each. Their 1988 distinction between "hugging" (make the learning resemble the target situation) and "bridging" (help learners abstract for far transfer) appears in teaching-resource pages I found by search; I did not read the 1988 paper itself.
- Sources:
  - Barnett, S. M., & Ceci, S. J. (2002). When and where do we apply what we learn? A taxonomy for far transfer. Psychological Bulletin, 128(4), 612-637. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.128.4.612 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Review.
  - Sala, G., & Gobet, F. (2017). Does far transfer exist? Negative evidence from chess, music, and working memory training. Current Directions in Psychological Science, 26(6), 515-520. https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721417712760 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Meta-analyses, children.
  - Perkins, D. N., & Salomon, G. (2012). Knowledge to go: A motivational and dispositional view of transfer. Educational Psychologist, 47(3), 248-258. https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2012.693354 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via OpenAlex). Review.
  - Perkins, D. N., & Salomon, G. (1988). Teaching for transfer. Educational Leadership, 46(1), 22-32. SECONDARY MENTION ONLY: citation and the "hugging and bridging" concepts seen in teaching-resource pages found by search; the paper itself was not read and not checked in Crossref. Nothing in the findings relies on it.
- Strength: strong that far transfer is hard and conditional. The cited negative evidence concerns training thinking skills in children through chess, music or working-memory exercises, which differs from teaching adults to classify cases; it supports the caution, not a specific number.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Barnett and Ceci stress that the same literature contains substantial transfer under some conditions (F23, F26). The Sala and Gobet studies are about children and general cognitive skills.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: Aim for near-to-moderate transfer on purpose and say so. The target is: the same kind of case, in a different story, setting and wording, in the format real life uses (a message, a headline, an offer, a statement). So the specimens should look like the real thing, in the learner's own world, and not only like tidy textbook cases; the words the learner will meet in real life should be taught alongside the lesson's own vocabulary; and the lessons should help the learner notice the occasion ("where would you meet this?"), because detecting the occasion is a separate step from knowing the idea. Do not claim a unit makes people better thinkers in general.

### F23. Short formal training in a reasoning rule, taught with varied examples, can improve reasoning about everyday problems, including in domains not taught, though the untrained-domain effect fades faster.
- What the research shows: Fong, Krantz and Nisbett taught people the law of large numbers in brief laboratory sessions, either as a formal rule or through examples in particular domains ("guided induction"), and found more and better statistical reasoning about a wide variety of everyday problems; example training produced as much improvement in untrained domains as in the trained one, people with years of statistics training reasoned better than those with less, and training effects appeared even when people were tested outside the context of training. Fong and Nisbett trained the rule using example problems in one domain and tested on the same or another domain immediately or after two weeks: transfer to another domain was strong when testing was immediate, performance in the trained domain did not decline over two weeks, and in the untrained domain it declined but stayed better than for controls; memory measures suggested what was retained was the rule system, not details of the examples. A review concluded that even brief formal training in inferential rules may improve reasoning about everyday events. A meta-analysis of 341 effect sizes on teaching critical thinking found a modest overall effect (g = 0.30), with generic and content-specific approaches both effective, and with dialogue, authentic or situated problems and examples, and mentoring helping.
- Sources:
  - Fong, G. T., Krantz, D. H., & Nisbett, R. E. (1986). The effects of statistical training on thinking about everyday problems. Cognitive Psychology, 18(3), 253-292. https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(86)90001-0 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in a search-result excerpt of the University of Michigan repository record; Crossref check passed). Four experiments.
  - Fong, G. T., & Nisbett, R. E. (1991). Immediate and delayed transfer of training effects in statistical reasoning. Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 120(1), 34-45. https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.120.1.34 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via OpenAlex). Experiment.
  - Nisbett, R. E., Fong, G. T., Lehman, D. R., & Cheng, P. W. (1987). Teaching reasoning. Science, 238(4827), 625-631. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.3672116 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Review.
  - Abrami, P. C., Bernard, R. M., Borokhovski, E., Waddington, D. I., Wade, C. A., & Persson, T. (2015). Strategies for teaching students to think critically: A meta-analysis. Review of Educational Research, 85(2), 275-314. https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314551063 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Crossref). Meta-analysis.
- Strength: moderate. These are carefully run experiments from one research tradition with a reasonable meta-analytic backdrop, but the rule taught is mainly the law of large numbers and the test problems resemble the training problems in kind.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The result sits in tension with F22. The reconciling difference is that the rule is formal and narrow, was taught with varied examples from different domains, and the test problems were the same kind of problem in a new setting, which is moderate, not far, transfer. The untrained-domain benefit faded over two weeks. The abstracts I read do not describe the participants beyond "subjects" and do not report effect sizes in a form I could quote.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: For subjects built on rules (Statistical Claims, Basic Math, Scams), teach each rule with examples from at least two different areas of life and then test it in a third. Re-test after a delay, since the untrained-domain benefit decays first. Expect the benefit to be strongest for new cases of the same kind and weaker for situations that do not obviously look like the lesson.

### F24. Debiasing training that combines instruction with practice and personalised feedback can reduce biases for months and in one field case, but the evidence is concentrated and partly mixed.
- What the research shows: Morewedge and colleagues ran two longitudinal experiments in which a single training intervention, either a computer game or an instructional video, addressed biases relevant to intelligence analysis. Both reduced bias immediately (games by at least about 32%, videos by at least about 19%) and the reductions persisted at least two months (games by at least about 24%, videos by at least about 19%); games with personalised feedback and practice did better than videos, and the effects appeared on problems in contexts and formats that had not been taught. In a quasi-field study, 290 graduate students in three professional programmes who had received a one-shot training intervention against confirmation bias were 19% less likely to choose the inferior, hypothesis-confirming answer to an unannounced business case modelled on the Challenger launch decision than students who had not yet been trained (the schedule varied naturally). A study of analogical training against ten biases (154 participants, tested four weeks later) found improvement only on tasks that measure violations of statistical principles. A systematic review of 68 articles (87 studies) of debiasing in health-related judgments found most interventions were at least partly successful, but named transfer to real life and weak conceptual rigour as the main challenges.
- Sources:
  - Morewedge, C. K., Yoon, H., Scopelliti, I., Symborski, C. W., Korris, J. H., & Kassam, K. S. (2015). Debiasing decisions: Improved decision making with a single training intervention. Policy Insights from the Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 2(1), 129-140. https://doi.org/10.1177/2372732215600886 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Crossref). Two longitudinal experiments.
  - Sellier, A.-L., Scopelliti, I., & Morewedge, C. K. (2019). Debiasing training improves decision making in the field. Psychological Science, 30(9), 1371-1379. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797619861429 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Quasi-experimental field study, graduate students.
  - Aczel, B., Bago, B., Szollosi, A., Foldes, A., & Lukacs, B. (2015). Is it time for studying real-life debiasing? Evaluation of the effectiveness of an analogical intervention technique. Frontiers in Psychology, 6, 1120. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01120 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Experiment, 154 participants.
  - Ludolph, R., & Schulz, P. J. (2018). Debiasing health-related judgments and decision making: A systematic review. Medical Decision Making, 38(1), 3-13. https://doi.org/10.1177/0272989X17716672 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Systematic review.
- Strength: moderate. Several lines of results, but the strongest come from one research team and its collaborators, and the abstracts do not give the participants' ages or backgrounds (except graduate students in the field study).
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Aczel and colleagues' narrow result and the review's call for real-life transfer evidence temper the headline numbers. The quasi-field study did not randomise who was trained when. The diagnostic-reasoning trials (F21) find no benefit of generic reminders, which fits the pattern that practice with feedback matters and exhortation does not.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: For the Psychology and Statistical Claims subjects, where biases and statistical fallacies are the content, build the learning around instances: several cases of each error, with feedback, mixed with cases where the error is not present. Do not rely on a card that describes a bias. If a bias is taught, the lesson should include the learner doing something with an example of it in a new setting and being told how they did.

### F25. "If this situation comes up, then I will do this" plans help people turn a goal into action, with a medium-to-large average effect, but only when they have a goal and a clear cue, and the effect varies.
- What the research shows: Gollwitzer's account is that a plan of the form "whenever situation X arises, I will do Y" links a cue to a response so that the situation triggers the response with little deliberate control. A meta-analysis of 94 independent tests found a positive effect of medium-to-large size (d = 0.65) on goal attainment, through starting goal-directed action, shielding it from distraction, disengaging from failing courses of action and saving capability for later, and it supported the proposed process that such plans make the specified opportunity more accessible and automate the response. An analysis of experiments in which intentions were strengthened found that a medium-to-large change in intention (d = 0.66) led to only a small-to-medium change in behaviour (d = 0.36), which is the kind of gap that planning research targets. The plans benefited goal attainment when the underlying goal intention was strong but not when it was weak. In eating, plans helped promote healthy foods (d = 0.51) but less so reduce unhealthy eating (d = 0.29), and some studies of promotion may have overstated the effect because of weak control conditions. A health-contexts review found considerable heterogeneity and few registered randomised trials with objective behaviour measures, and recommended if-then plans that name salient cues, give examples of cues, are guided rather than wholly user-defined, and include boosters.
- Sources:
  - Gollwitzer, P. M. (1999). Implementation intentions: Strong effects of simple plans. American Psychologist, 54(7), 493-503. https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.54.7.493 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via OpenAlex). Review of a research programme.
  - Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69-119. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read in a search-result excerpt of the University of Konstanz repository record; Crossref check passed). Meta-analysis.
  - Webb, T. L., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Does changing behavioral intentions engender behavior change? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence. Psychological Bulletin, 132(2), 249-268. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.2.249 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Meta-analysis.
  - Sheeran, P., Webb, T. L., & Gollwitzer, P. M. (2005). The interplay between goal intentions and implementation intentions. Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 31(1), 87-98. https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167204271308 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Two studies.
  - Adriaanse, M. A., Vinkers, C. D. W., De Ridder, D. T. D., Hox, J. J., & De Wit, J. B. F. (2011). Do implementation intentions help to eat a healthy diet? A systematic review and meta-analysis of the empirical evidence. Appetite, 56(1), 183-193. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.appet.2010.10.012 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Systematic review and meta-analysis.
  - Hagger, M. S., & Luszczynska, A. (2014). Implementation intention and action planning interventions in health contexts: State of the research and proposals for the way forward. Applied Psychology: Health and Well-Being, 6(1), 1-47. https://doi.org/10.1111/aphw.12017 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC; Crossref lists 2013 as the online year). Review.
- Strength: strong for simple behaviours with a clear cue and a goal the person holds; moderate for stopping an unwanted habit, where the effects found were smaller.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: Plans did not help when the goal intention was weak. Effects shrink for diminishing unwanted behaviour and in better-controlled studies, and heterogeneity is large. I did not search exhaustively, but I found no study of if-then plans as a way to carry a classification skill into everyday life; the evidence I read is for behaviours such as eating and goal pursuit, not for noticing a category.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: At the end of units whose subject calls for action in the learner's own life (spotting a scam, handling money, reading a claim), offer a short plan card: name the cue in the learner's own world, with two or three example cues supplied (guided, not blank), and write one "if I see X, then I will do Y". Offer it only to learners who want that goal, because plans do little without it, and plan a later reminder. Treat this as a transfer aid for behaviour, an inference from the planning literature that the app has not yet tested, and not as a substitute for the discrimination practice.

### F26. Short education can reduce susceptibility to fraud without making people suspicious of everything, but the effect fades unless there is a reminder.
- What the research shows: A preregistered randomised experiment with a nationally representative panel of US adults (1,976 completed the baseline) compared a few minutes of online video or text education about five techniques fraudsters use (promising huge returns, posing as experts, claiming that many others have joined, creating urgency, and offering freebies to create a sense of obligation) with a control. Right afterwards, people were less likely to say they would invest in adapted real-world fraudulent solicitations and put more probability on losing money, while the effects on legitimate offers were much more muted, so people had become better at telling them apart and not more distrustful. Without a reminder the effect had gone by six months (no difference from control); with a reminder at three months it persisted at six. The benefit was concentrated among people more financially sophisticated and more likely to invest; for people with lower cognitive ability and financial literacy, the text version alone was associated with a worse outcome in one measure, and no such pattern appeared for the video. In a field experiment with older consumers who had been scam victims before, a warning about a scam given two or four weeks before a mock telemarketing pitch reduced acceptance of the pitch; warnings about the same scam produced more outright refusals but lost effect over time, while warnings about a different scam did not lose effect over the same period.
- Sources:
  - Burke, J., Kieffer, C., Mottola, G., & Perez-Arce, F. (2022). Can educational interventions reduce susceptibility to financial fraud? Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization, 198, 250-266. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2022.03.028 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (full text read of the February 2021 working-paper version; the journal version was not read; Crossref check passed for the journal version). Preregistered randomised experiment.
  - Scheibe, S., Notthoff, N., Menkin, J., Ross, L., Shadel, D., Deevy, M., & Carstensen, L. L. (2014). Forewarning reduces fraud susceptibility in vulnerable consumers. Basic and Applied Social Psychology, 36(3), 272-279. https://doi.org/10.1080/01973533.2014.903844 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Field experiment.
- Strength: moderate. A large preregistered trial and a small field experiment agree, but the main outcome in the large trial is stated willingness to invest, answered "assuming you had the money", in adapted offers; it is not behaviour with real money.
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The result concerns investment fraud; I did not find a comparable test for scams in other settings. The text-only caution in the working paper is a subgroup result, and the authors describe it in terms of a specific measure, not a general finding. A separate line of research (psychological inoculation against misinformation) is a related approach that I did not review here.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: In the Scams subject, test the learner on legitimate cases as well as fraudulent ones, so what is learned is discrimination, not suspicion of everything. Build in a revisit at roughly the point where the benefit would otherwise fade, since the evidence says a reminder is what keeps it. Since the only text-versus-video comparison suggests text can be less helpful to less sophisticated readers, the plainness of the language and the use of worked cases matter more, not less, in a text-only app.

### F27. To be able to apply a definition to new cases, practise with examples of it, not only the definition; and provided examples have beaten learner-generated ones in the tests I found.
- What the research shows: In two experiments, participants read a textbook passage with the definition and one example for each of ten declarative concepts (the abstract's example is from psychology) and then practised recalling either the definition or the example of each; application of the concepts to new scenarios was better after practising the examples. In experiments on social-psychology concepts, studying provided examples gave better long-term learning and efficiency than generating one's own examples, or doing both, according to the search excerpt of the abstract I could read.
- Sources:
  - Wissman, K. T., Zamary, A., Rawson, K. A., & Dunlosky, J. (2023). Enhancing declarative concept application: The utility of examples as primary targets of learning. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied, 29(2), 341-357. https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000432 . VERIFIED-ONLINE (abstract read via Europe PMC). Two experiments.
  - Zamary, A., & Rawson, K. A. (2018). Which technique is most effective for learning declarative concepts: Provided examples, generated examples, or both? Educational Psychology Review, 30(1), 275-301. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-016-9396-9 . VERIFIED-ONLINE for existence (Crossref; Crossref lists 2016 as the online year); the finding was read only in a search-result excerpt of the abstract, so treat it as weaker. Experiments.
- Strength: limited. Few experiments, short-term outcomes, concepts from introductory psychology, undergraduate samples (not stated in the abstract I read, but typical of this lab).
- Boundary conditions and counter-evidence: The generated-example result concerns the quality and efficiency of learning, and says nothing about whether asking learners to think of an example from their own life helps motivation or relevance, which I did not find tested. The comparison is for declarative concepts with definitions, a bit different from a multi-question key.
- What it implies for a lesson in this app: An outcome's name and one-line definition are not enough to be applied to a new case; each needs worked examples, and the learner's practice should centre on applying the idea to a new example, not on repeating the definition. An invitation to think of a personal example may suit the end-of-unit transfer step (F25), but the evidence found does not support replacing provided examples with learner-generated ones.

## Tensions inside this cluster

1. **Interleave or block (F1, F2, F3, F11).** Interleaving wins for confusable categories and when the test needs the learner to separate them; blocking wins for dissimilar categories, for passive study, for some verbal material, and for encoding what a category is typically like. The literature resolves this with one mechanism: a sequence changes what the learner attends to (what differs between neighbours when mixed, what is shared within a category when blocked), so the right sequence depends on what has to be known at test (Carvalho and Goldstone 2017). For this app the test has both halves: tell look-alike outcomes apart, and recognise one outcome in a messy case. The reconciliation is a sequence: meet each outcome on its own first (what it is, characteristic features, a clean case), then contrast it with its closest neighbours, then practise mixed. That order is an inference; each of its three parts is supported, and I found no study that tests the whole sequence on adults with conceptual categories.
2. **Compare to find similarities, or contrast to find differences (F6, F7 against F2).** The case-comparison meta-analysis found larger effects when learners were asked to find similarities; the interleaving literature says the gain comes from differences between categories. Both are right for different pairings: similarity-seeking across two different-looking cases from the same category builds the schema of the category, and difference-seeking across look-alike cases from neighbouring categories builds the boundary. The contrastive instruction in the ECG study (compare the category being learned with a confusable one, F19) used the second kind and helped.
3. **Varied examples against clean, idealised ones (F10 against F12, with F9).** Variety eventually gives broader transfer, yet for low-knowledge learners similar examples worked better and clean cases beat a representative mix in one study. The consistent reading is sequencing by what the learner can already see: clean and similar first, then varied, then deliberately misleading. Where the studies stop is how fast to move; nobody gives a schedule.
4. **Concrete or abstract (F13 against F14).** Fading from concrete to generic beat either alone in several experiments; abstract-only learning transferred to abstract tests and concrete-only to concrete ones. The target decides: this app's target is concrete real cases, so concrete cases are required, with the decisive feature also stated generically so it can travel.
5. **Learners explaining against instructors explaining (F15, F16, F17, F18).** Self-explanation beat instructional explanation on average (g about 0.35) yet a substantial part of its benefit is also available from a good instructor explanation, an on-demand explanation can reduce the effort learners put in, and explaining can flatten exceptions. The resolution that fits all of these is to have the learner commit to a reason first, then show the model reason, and to teach exceptions explicitly and separately.
6. **Rule or resemblance (F19 against F11).** Classification practice narrows attention to the features that discriminate, and combined instruction (check features and use resemblance) beat either alone. Together: teach the rule-like check and the pattern sense, and say that both are meant.
7. **Transfer is rare, yet reasoning rules and debiasing training transferred (F22 against F23 and F24).** The positive results are narrow, formal rules or specific biases, taught with several examples and practice, tested on the same kind of problem in a different setting. The negative results are about general cognitive skills trained through unrelated activities. These do not conflict: near-to-moderate transfer of a specific skill is achievable; far transfer of "thinking" is not shown. Telling people to be careful or to watch for bias (F21) belongs with the negative side.
8. **What feels better against what works (F4).** Learners judge blocked practice better even when mixed practice gave them better results. A design that lets learners choose their practice format, or that judges drills by how easy they felt, will tend to remove the beneficial difficulty.
9. **Generation against provision (F15 against F27).** Generating explanations helps, but generating examples did not beat studying provided ones in the experiments I found. The consistent reading is that the useful generation is the one that processes structure (explaining why), not the one that replaces the model case.

## What the research does not tell us

- **Abstract, verbal and contested categories.** Almost all the discrimination evidence uses paintings, birds and butterflies, X-rays, ECGs, artificial images or school mathematics. The 2019 meta-analysis found no clear interleaving benefit for expository text and a reversal for words. Political ideologies, scam types, psychological effects and civics concepts are verbal, partly overlapping and sometimes disputed; I found no study of teaching fuzzy or contested categories like these.
- **The determination key itself.** A short fixed sequence of narrowing questions with a gate question first is not a format I found studied. The nearest evidence (F20) shows that whether the lesson is organised around features or around categories made no measurable difference to ECG accuracy, and the practice schedule did. Nothing tells us whether scoring the route and the name separately improves learning, or whether the key should be taught before or after the outcomes.
- **Adults studying alone on a phone.** The strongest results come from classroom or laboratory studies with children and university students, with teachers, peers or an experimenter present. I found no study of interleaved classification practice in short phone sessions, of typing effort for explanation prompts, or of whether learners stay with a drill that feels harder (F4). The age pattern in the 2019 meta-analysis (smaller effects in older samples, though not reliable within university samples) and its authors' remark about adults beyond college age leave open how big the benefit is for this audience.
- **How much, how many, how soon.** Not established: how many confusable outcomes can be mixed in one drill, how many cases per outcome are enough, the best ratio of clean to messy cases, how long to block before mixing (one verbal study favoured blocked-first, and I did not read the result for its gradual-transition group), and what spacing keeps recognition alive.
- **Durability and real-life outcomes.** Most tests were minutes to weeks after learning. Debiasing effects were followed for two months, fraud education for six months (with a reminder), and the field evidence on real behaviour is thin. Almost nothing measures whether a person later recognises the category in their own life unprompted.
- **Own-life application.** Implementation-intention evidence is about behaviour change for goals people hold; I did not find evidence on using if-then plans, own-life examples or reflection prompts to carry a classification skill into daily life, and the one study on learner-generated examples found no advantage over provided ones.
- **Evidence quality.** Many findings rest on single laboratories or research groups promoting their own method (interleaved mathematics practice, perceptual learning modules, debiasing games). Heterogeneity is large (the 2019 interleaving meta-analysis had I-squared of about 77%), and I found few direct comparisons of laboratory and real-world effect sizes.
- **Amount of content.** None of this research sets a limit on how long a lesson should be. Nearly every implication above adds material (second cases in each pair, contrast text, an exceptions card, a mixed drill, a plan card); none supports shortening. The research supports small steps and no padding, and it does not support cutting an explanation that the learner needs in order to get through the next step.
