# Adversarial review: claims, boundaries and applicability

Reviewer lens: are the strength ratings earned, do the REQUIRES lines follow for adults doing phone self-study on diagnostic classification, are the tension resolutions and the explanation-length answer honest, and what is missing. Target: `docs/learning-science.md` (sections 1-7 read in full on 2026-10-04; the section 8 reference list was checked programmatically against Crossref rather than read line by line). Status: complete (23 problems: 9 SERIOUS, 14 MINOR; ends with the line "END OF REVIEW").

Tags: SERIOUS = changes what a lesson should do, or is a false/fabricated citation. MINOR = wording, rating or labelling fix.

## Problems

**1. [P2 requires 1, P3 requires 1-3, P3 forbids; section 4.3 step 0] SERIOUS. "Every unit assumes nothing about the subject" ignores that the learner is no longer a beginner by Unit 3, and the document's own strongest evidence says to thin support as knowledge grows.**
What is wrong: P2 requires that "the default path of every unit is the full-beginner version: it assumes nothing about the subject beyond everyday life" and requires 3 that support shrinks "not by thinning the explanation". P3 then forbids "leaving a gap" and "trimming connective reasoning" with no exception. But each subject is 5 to 7 units long (docs/lesson-rebuild-todo.md), so a learner in Unit 5 has studied Units 1-4 of the same subject. The expertise-reversal literature the document cites for P2 is precisely about explanatory support: Tetzlaff et al. (2025) found d = 0.505 for high-assistance instruction in low-prior-knowledge learners and d = -0.428 for the same instruction in high-prior-knowledge learners (60 studies, 176 effect sizes), and the original reversal studies (Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller 2003) are about redundant explanation hurting people who no longer need it. P3's own boundary note says gaps help readers who can fill them. The document therefore cites expertise reversal as strong evidence and then writes a rule (no thinning, ever) that the evidence contradicts for every unit after the first.
Evidence: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142 ; https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4 ; docs/learning-science.md lines 121-124, 141-150.
Correction: In P2 requires 1 replace "assumes nothing about the subject beyond everyday life" with "assumes nothing beyond everyday life plus the earlier units of the same subject, whose terms and routes it may use without re-teaching (it restates a definition in one line, per P3 requires 2, but does not re-explain)". Replace requires 3 with "Support shrinks across units as well as inside a unit: connective explanation of a link the learner has already been taught and has answered correctly (P24 record) may be shortened to a one-line restatement; a link that is new to the learner is always written out." Add to P3 FORBIDS: "Re-explaining, at full length, a link the same learner has already been taught in an earlier unit." Mark the unit-1 beginner path as the only place where "assume nothing" holds.

**2. [P11 requires 1, P12 forbids, P10 requires 2, P8 forbids] SERIOUS. Three normative lines cannot all be satisfied by one lesson.**
What is wrong: (a) P11 requires introducing every idea as case, then the same case stripped to its decisive features, then the rule, and only then (step d) a second case. P12 FORBIDS "a principle stated about a single case with no second case". Steps (a)-(c) of P11 state the rule after a single case, so a lesson that follows P11 literally breaks P12. The reason P12 exists is Gick & Holyoak (1983): a principle or diagram alone did not produce the analogy from one story, but did with two. (b) P8 FORBIDS "splitting a rule from its case across cards", while P10 requires 2 allows the rule beside the case "on the same card or on consecutive cards". A complete worked case (P10 requires 1 lists about ten components) plus the rule plus the marked cue cannot fit one phone screen, and P7 requires 3 says a card that must scroll should be split. A builder is forced to violate one of P7, P8 or P10 on every worked case.
Evidence: https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(83)90002-6 (P12's own source). Sanchez & Wiley (2009), https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720809352788 (scrolling cost, cited in P7), Ginns (2006), https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2006.10.001 (contiguity, cited in P8).
Correction: (a) Reword P11 requires 1(c) to "the rule is stated provisionally in plain words, and is confirmed as general only after a second, differently-storied case shows the same decisive feature (this is P11 requires 3 and P12)", and reword the P12 FORBIDS to "A principle presented as general after a single case with no second case". (b) Define "worked case" in P10 as a sequence of consecutive cards, and change the P8 FORBIDS to "Splitting a rule from its case without restating the case text and the rule in a pinned header (or first two lines) of each following card in the sequence". State explicitly that a worked-case sequence is exempt from the "fit one screen" judgement call in P7 requires 3 because splitting at an idea boundary inside a single worked case is not possible without the restatement.

**3. [P22 strength line, P22 requires 2/forbids, section 4.1 tension 2] SERIOUS. The rating "moderate for verbal and conceptual categories" is not earned by the evidence listed, and the FORBIDS turns it into an absolute rule.**
What is wrong: the document labels interleaving "strong (pictures, maths); moderate (verbal, this app)" and then FORBIDS "one-outcome-per-block drills after the outcomes have been introduced" and "learner-selectable blocked-only practice as the default". But the verbal-category evidence the document itself lists points the other way or is null: Brunmair & Richter (2019) find g = -0.39 for words (13 effect sizes), g = 0.21 with a 95% CI of -0.06 to 0.47 for expository text, and their own discussion says interleaving "should be used with caution" for expository text and words; Sorensen & Woltz (2016, verbal categories) favoured blocked-first. No study listed in P22 shows an interleaving benefit on verbal categories. The meta-analysis also shows the benefit only for immediate succession (g = 0.73 versus 0.22 when separated), which the document handles, but it means the benefit is fragile exactly where the app's real conditions (a phone, interruptions, short sessions) put gaps between items. The only directly relevant positive study I found that the document does not cite is Sana, Yan & Kim (2017): interleaving problems of different cognitive concepts improved classification of new problems, and adding temporal spacing hurt the interleaved schedule. It supports the direction but is one study of undergraduates.
Evidence: https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209 (Brunmair & Richter; I checked the accepted manuscript: the table gives words k = 13, g = -0.39; expository texts k = 25, g = 0.21, CI -0.06 to 0.47); https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000119 (Sana, Yan & Kim 2017); https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-016-0615-x (Sorensen & Woltz).
Correction: Change the strength line to "strong (pictures, maths); limited (verbal and conceptual categories, which is most of this app; one supportive study of cognitive concepts, one meta-analytic null for expository text, one reversal for words)". Turn P22 FORBIDS items 1 and 2 into a judgement call: "Default is blocked introduction, then contrast, then mixed practice of confusable outcomes; the standard should log first-attempt accuracy for mixed versus blocked drill items so the default can be tested on real learners." Add Sana, Yan & Kim (2017) to the P22 evidence and remove "almost nothing on everyday verbal categories" from section 7 or soften to "almost nothing beyond one study of cognitive concepts".

**4. [P17 requires 5, P24 requires 5 and forbids, section 6 row "Practice to a high criterion"] SERIOUS. "Never gate the next unit on a mastery score" is built on one half of a meta-analysis whose main result is the opposite.**
What is wrong: the only evidence for the ban is Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990), cited as "positive effects, self-paced mastery reduced completion in college classes". The headline result of that meta-analysis is that mastery learning programmes (which are defined by requiring mastery before moving on) raised examination scores by an average of 0.52 standard deviations across 108 evaluations, with larger gains for weaker students. The completion problem is specific to self-paced, deadline-free college courses. The document inverts the finding into a hard FORBIDS without any evidence about what a soft gate does in a no-stakes phone app, in either direction. Section 6 repeats the one-sided summary.
Evidence: https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543060002265 (effect size 0.52 reported in the abstract; the research note docs/learning-science/practice.md line 298 also records the positive effect, so the synthesis dropped it).
Correction: Replace the FORBIDS "Gating the next unit on a score" with a judgement call: "No hard gate by default because of the completion risk in self-paced study (Kulik et al. 1990), but mastery learning is the better-supported design for learning itself (ES 0.52); when first-attempt accuracy on the prerequisite unit's items is low, the app shows a soft prompt ('review these first') and schedules the items, and the learner may continue. Tune on completion data." In section 6, change the right-hand cell to state both findings.

**5. [P23 requires 1 and forbids; section 2 learner description; gap list] SERIOUS. No principle governs motivation, persistence or autonomy, yet ten principles add friction for a learner the document says "will stop the moment practice feels punishing".**
What is wrong: section 2 describes an adult studying alone with "no teacher, no exam and no external stakes" who stops when practice is punishing. P15 (typed reasons), P16 (a check between cards), P17, P22 (practice that is meant to feel harder), P23, P24 (returns on later days) all add effort, and P23 forbids any learner control ("no 'I've got this' control", no opt-in to blocked practice). The only concession is "one plain sentence says it is meant to feel harder", and the document's own source for that remedy (Yan, Bjork & Bjork 2016) found explanation alone had only a modest effect. Engagement and dropout appear only as a GAP in section 7 ("it could cut against the recommendations"). A document that is the sole authority for lesson design cannot list the main threat to its own recommendations as an unexamined gap. There is well-established counter-evidence on choice: Patall, Cooper & Robinson (2008), 41 studies, found that providing choice raised intrinsic motivation, effort, task performance and perceived competence, though less so for instructionally relevant choices and for adults. The ban on item removal is well supported (Kornell & Bjork 2008b, Karpicke & Roediger 2008); the ban on all learner control is not.
Evidence: https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.134.2.270 ; https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000177 (Yan, Bjork & Bjork) ; docs/learning-science.md lines 41, 549-556, 778.
Correction: Add a principle P28 "Protect persistence": (1) the learner controls what the evidence says they can safely control (when to stop a session, which subject or unit to open, pace, whether to see the optional plan card, skipping an item for now without it counting as learned) while the app controls what returns and when (P23 stays for item removal); (2) every session has a visible, achievable end (a short set, then stop) and the app shows the learner their own results (P23 requires 3); whether brief affirming messages or streaks help persistence is untested (the P20 boundary note says the same), so test them rather than ban or assume them; (3) record session starts, completion and return rate as first-class data because they are the unmeasured cost of P15-P24. Narrow P23 requires 1 and FORBIDS to "no control that removes an item from review or declares a unit finished". Move the engagement gap from section 7 into the principles.

**6. [MISSING principle: misconceptions] SERIOUS. Learners are not blank slates in these seven subjects, and the document has no principle for the wrong ideas they arrive with.**
What is wrong: psychology, politics, money, statistics, scams and civics are exactly the areas where adults hold strong, wrong everyday beliefs ("correlation shows cause", "the president can do X", "this scam only fools gullible people"). P2 treats the learner as someone who "assumes nothing beyond everyday life", but everyday life is where the wrong ideas come from. The best-supported remedy is refutation text: state the common misconception, say it is wrong, and give the correct explanation. A 2022 meta-analysis of 44 independent comparisons (n = 3,869) found a moderate positive effect (g = 0.41) for refutation text compared with other conditions. P18 touches the idea only as a safety rule for wrong statements ("clearly marked wrong and the correction follows"), and P14 as "near-misses", not as a deliberate teaching move.
Evidence: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-021-09656-z (Schroeder & Kucera 2022; the numbers are from a search summary of the abstract because the publisher page was not retrievable, so tag it `sec` until read).
Correction: Add principle P29 "Name and refute the intuition the learner is likely to bring": for each outcome or key question whose wrong everyday reading is known, one card states the tempting idea in the learner's own words, marks it wrong, then gives the correct reasoning, and a later check item tests that exact contrast. Boundary to state: the meta-analysis is mostly science-concept misconceptions and school or undergraduate samples; it has not been tested on political, financial or scam categories. Add one line to P18 requires 3 pointing to P29.

Mark every principle with the unit kinds it governs.

**9. [P3 strength] MINOR. "Strong" fails the document's own definition of strong.**

**10. [P27 requires 1, "Equivalently"] SERIOUS. A coverage check by a simulated reader is declared equivalent to a cold read by a real novice, which contradicts the principle's own evidence.**
What is wrong: P27's whole argument is that the author (and the subject expert) cannot see the gaps, because of the expert blind spot (Hinds 1999; Nathan & Petrosino 2003). Requires 1 then says a unit is finished when a person with no prior knowledge has read it cold, "equivalently, every practice item is answerable from the lessons alone (the learner-simulation check in the rebuild worklist)". The simulation is run by the same model and author pipeline that wrote the lessons, and a model has the subject knowledge a real novice lacks, which is the blind spot in its purest form. The simulation is a useful mechanical coverage check (is every option wording taught in an earlier card; the same thing as the P5 two-way vocabulary check), but it measures whether a term appears, not whether a person understood it. Making the two "equivalent" lets a unit be signed off with no human novice, which P27 forbids elsewhere ("author review ... never sign a unit off").
Evidence: https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898x.5.2.205 ; https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312040004905 ; docs/lesson-rebuild-todo.md ("Learner simulation re-run on the rewritten units").
Correction: Split requires 1 into two lines. "(a) Coverage check (automatable, may be simulated): every option, question and outcome wording a drill uses appears in an earlier card of the same subject. Necessary, never sufficient. (b) Comprehension check (human): a person with no prior knowledge, or failing that a near-novice who learned the subject recently, reads the unit cold, says each card back in their own words, and attempts the drill unaided. Only (b) signs a unit off." Delete the word "Equivalently".

**13. [P17 title and requires 4; section 4.1 tension 1] MINOR. The accuracy target and the "worked case first" argument cite the wrong half of the evidence: Rowland's no-feedback bands do not apply, Rowland's feedback subset points the other way, and the source that does support a high-accuracy target (for transfer) is not cited in P17.**
What is wrong: Section 4.1 says "Retrieval with no feedback at or below 50% success does nothing (Rowland), so asking a beginner to retrieve a route they were never shown wastes the attempt." The app always gives feedback (P19), so the no-feedback bands (g = 0.03, 0.29, 0.56) do not apply. In the feedback subset of the same meta-analysis, low initial test performance went with larger testing effects on the same material (g = 0.99 under 50%, 0.68 for 51-75%, 0.40 above 75%; the author reads it as retrieval effort, and the subset is small). So for retention Rowland argues against a high-success target. For transfer, which is what this app wants (P25), Pan & Rickard (2018) found the opposite slope: transfer was predicted by initial test accuracy (with no response congruency, no elaboration and the lowest observed initial accuracy, 0.19, the estimated transfer effect was near zero, d = -0.05; with congruency, elaboration and the highest accuracy, 0.98, d = 0.90, bias-adjusted 0.58). The document cites Pan & Rickard in P5, P13 and P25 but not in P17, and P17's title ("with most first attempts succeeding") is justified in the text only by Rosenshine's fourth-grade 82% versus 73% observation and a model of idealised learners. The target may be right; the stated support is not.
Evidence: https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559 (Rowland; full text read: "Retrievability and reexposure" table and the feedback-only initial-performance analysis); https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151 (Pan & Rickard; author manuscript read).
Correction: Keep requires 4 but state both findings: "Rowland (2014): with feedback, low initial accuracy went with larger retention effects (g = 0.99 under 50%, small subset). Pan & Rickard (2018): transfer to new problems rose with initial accuracy. Because the target is transfer (P25), aim for most early-rung first attempts correct; low accuracy is a flag to investigate, not a proven harm, and the risk it protects against (dropout) is untested." Remove the Rowland sentence from section 4.1 tension 1 and replace with "A beginner who has not been shown the route cannot produce it, so the attempt yields only a wrong guess to correct (P10); this is a worked-example argument."

**15. [P26 strength line and evidence] MINOR. The if-then plan effect size is superseded by a larger update by the same authors, and "strong" should not stand on d = 0.65.**
What is wrong: P26 rates if-then plans "strong" and cites Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), 94 tests, d = 0.65. In 2025 Sheeran, Listrom and Gollwitzer published an update of 642 independent tests (European Review of Social Psychology, 36(1), 162-194). Its abstract reports effects of .27 <= d <= .66 across cognitive, affective and behavioural outcomes, larger when the plan had an if-then format, the goal was highly motivating and the plan was rehearsed. Secondary summaries of the paper (not the abstract) give a raw pooled d = 0.36 (CI 0.33 to 0.40), roughly half the 2006 figure; I could not confirm from the abstract that the authors attribute the drop to small-study or publication bias, so treat that reading as unverified. Either way, d = 0.65 is no longer the best single number to quote. The document already limits the plan card (optional, judgement call), so the practical impact is small; the label and the number should change.
Evidence: https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/handle/123456789/69905 (author repository record; abstract read; the pooled 0.36 is from secondary summaries and should be checked in the paper).
Correction: Change "strong for if-then plans" to "moderate (a 2025 update of 642 tests reports d = .27 to .66 by outcome; the 2006 figure of d = 0.65 came from 94 tests)". Keep the rule that plans help when the goal is held and rehearsed, and add "rehearsal" to the plan-card requirement: the later reminder (P26 requires 4) is what supplies it.

**16. [P4 strength, P4 requires 3] MINOR. The "moderate" rating rests on pre-training and jargon evidence that does not test the rule, and requires 3 contradicts itself.**

Evidence: https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-013-9228-0 (Ginns et al.; 35-minute boundary checked on a search summary of the abstract) ; docs/learning-science.md lines 155-165.
Correction: Change the strength to "limited to moderate". In requires 3 choose one design and say so: "Each unit opens with a card naming at most three target terms in plain words with one example each (the pre-training form); any further terms are introduced at the card that first needs them." Mark the order "ordinary words, then term" as a judgement call.

**18. [Section 5 "The short answer"] MINOR. "The owner is right, and the evidence says so" overstates; the evidence neither imposes nor licenses a length.**
What is wrong: the section is honest on the core finding: no study sets or tests a length (checked: docs/learning-science/text-feedback.md line 383 says so in terms). But the opening line turns an absence of studies into support for the owner's instruction. Absence of a tested cap is not evidence that no cap is right.
