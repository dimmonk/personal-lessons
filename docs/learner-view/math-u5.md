## Part 1 of 3: How many ways: separate lists, a group in order, a group in any order

### 1. Count the wrong way and you still get a number

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 1 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient-chance`]

Before you work out how many ways something can happen, or how likely it is, check which of five kinds of problem you have. Each kind has its own steps, and the wrong steps still give a neat number that looks right.

Say 4 people are to be picked from 9 for a quiz team. Count one way and you get 6,561. Count another way and you get 3,024. Count the right way and you get 126. All three look like sensible answers, and nothing in the number tells you which is wrong.

So the first job is not the sum. It is to ask what you are counting, or what chance you want, and only then to work it out with the steps for that kind.

Four words mean the same thing every time. A result is one complete way it can turn out, such as one phone cover or one team. A list is everything one choice can be, such as the 3 styles of cover. A pick is one thing taken from a list or a group. A chance is a number for how likely something is: 0 means it cannot happen, 1 means it is certain, and 0.2, the same as 20%, means 1 time in every 5.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“What is this problem about?”** Its answers:

- **“A missing number in a formula, rate or totals”**: give this answer when one or two numbers are left out, and you are given a formula, a rate such as so much for each thing, or totals that they have to fit.
- **“An amount changing over time”**: give this answer when one amount changes by the same number added or multiplied each month or year, or changes once, and you are asked what it will be or when it reaches a target.
- **“Counting ways and chances”**: give this answer when you are asked how many ways something can be chosen or ordered, how likely at least one of several things is, or how likely a test result is to be right. **This unit is about these problems.**
- **“Right triangles, or one shape at two sizes”**: give this answer when there is a right triangle, or two things of exactly the same shape at different sizes, and you are asked for a length, an area or a volume, or how they compare.

### 2. Multiplying the choices

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 2 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-multprin`]

The simplest counting there is: several choices, each made from its own list.

*The phone covers*

> A phone shop sells covers in 4 colors (black, red, blue, green) and 3 styles (plain, ridged, clear). ⟦A customer picks one color and one style⟧. How many different covers can the shop sell?

Write them out. Black comes in 3 covers: plain, ridged and clear. Red comes in the same 3, and so do blue and green. That is 4 colors with 3 covers each: 4 × 3 = 12 different covers.

You multiply, and do not add, because every color goes with every style. Whatever color the customer picks, all 3 styles are still there to choose from. Two numbers to multiply are not enough to spot this kind: the next two kinds multiply too.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the separate choices: a color and a style.** Each one is a decision of its own.
2. **Check each choice has its own full list: 4 colors and 3 styles.** Picking a color uses up none of the styles.
3. **Check the question asks for whole results: how many different covers.** You are counting finished covers, not single colors or styles.

This is **Multiplying the choices**. To count the results, multiply the sizes of the lists.

You may also hear this called “the multiplication principle” or “the counting principle”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Multiplying the choices**.

### 4. Worked: how many different sandwiches?

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 4 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `solved`, id `solved-multprin-1`]

The steps for this kind of problem, worked out on a sandwich shop.

**The problem**

> A sandwich shop lets a customer pick one of 3 breads, one of 5 fillings and one of 2 sauces. How many different sandwiches can the shop make?

**The working, step by step**

- Name each choice: bread; filling; sauce

Each is a separate decision with a list of its own. A sandwich is one result of all three.

- Count the full list for each choice: bread: 3; filling: 5; sauce: 2

Picking rye still leaves all 5 fillings and both sauces, so each list stays full.

- Multiply the counts: 3 × 5 × 2 = 30 (3 × 5 = 15, then 15 × 2 = 30). That is 30 sandwiches

**You are asked:** This step carries the idea. Every statement below is true of the problem. Before you read the reason, choose the one that explains why this step is done.

- Every bread goes with every filling, and each of those pairs goes with either sauce, so the counts multiply.
- There are 3 + 5 + 2 = 10 things on the shelf.
- The shop has 3 breads, 5 fillings and 2 sauces.

**Shown as soon as you answer**

- The one that explains it: Every bread goes with every filling, and each of those pairs goes with either sauce, so the counts multiply.
  - If you chose “There are 3 + 5 + 2 = 10 things on the shelf.”: True, but that counts single items, such as a bread, and never a whole sandwich. It does not say why to multiply.
  - If you chose “The shop has 3 breads, 5 fillings and 2 sauces.”: True, but those are only the counts. It does not say what to do with them.

Take rye. It goes with any of the 5 fillings, so 5 sandwiches start with rye. White and the third bread do the same, so the breads and fillings make 3 × 5 = 15 pairs.

Each pair can have either sauce, so every pair appears twice: 15 × 2 = 30. Adding would count the 10 things on the shelf, and a sandwich such as rye, ham and mustard would never be in your count.

**The result**

The shop can make 30 different sandwiches, each with one bread, one filling and one sauce.


### 5. Permutations

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 5 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-perm`]

Now the picks come out of one group, so each pick takes someone out.

*The four runners*

> Four friends, Ana, Ben, Cal and Dev, run a race on the school field. ⟦The first across the line gets a gold medal and the second gets a silver medal⟧. In how many different ways can the two medals be given out?

Sort the results by who wins gold. If Ana wins gold, silver goes to Ben, Cal or Dev: 3 results. Ben, Cal and Dev winning gold give 3 each. That is 4 gold winners with 3 silver winners each: 4 × 3 = 12.

The second number is 3, not 4, because the gold winner cannot also win silver. Each pick takes someone out of the group, so the counts fall by one each time: 4, then 3. With a third medal it would be 4 × 3 × 2. And the order counts: Ana with gold and Ben with silver is not the same as Ben with gold and Ana with silver.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the one group everyone is picked from: Ana, Ben, Cal and Dev.** There is a single list, not one list for each pick.
2. **Check the picks are different jobs, made one after another: gold, then silver.** Different jobs are what make the order matter.
3. **Check each pick uses someone up: the gold winner cannot win silver.** The list gets one shorter each time.

This is **Permutations**. Multiply counts that fall by one each time, and every different order is its own result.

You may also hear this called “arrangements”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Permutations**.

The steps for this kind of problem, worked out on a hiking club.

**The problem**

> A hiking club with 12 members elects a chair, a secretary and a treasurer. No member may hold more than one of the jobs. In how many different ways can the three jobs be filled?

**The working, step by step**

- Count the group and the picks: Group: 12 members. Picks: 3 (chair, secretary, treasurer)

The jobs are different, so the order matters: chair Ana with secretary Ben is not chair Ben with secretary Ana.

- Write how many can be picked each time: chair: 12; secretary: 11; treasurer: 10

**You are asked:** This step carries the idea. Every statement below is true of the problem. Before you read the reason, choose the one that explains why this step is done.

- No member may hold two jobs, so each job is picked from a group one smaller than the last.
- The club has 12 members, so the first job has 12 people to pick from.
- There are three jobs, so there are three counts to multiply.

**Shown as soon as you answer**

- The one that explains it: No member may hold two jobs, so each job is picked from a group one smaller than the last.
  - If you chose “The club has 12 members, so the first job has 12 people to pick from.”: True, but that only explains the first count. It does not say why the next counts are 11 and 10.
  - If you chose “There are three jobs, so there are three counts to multiply.”: True, but that only says how many counts there are. It does not say why they get smaller.

### 8. Multiplying the choices or Permutations: telling them apart

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 8 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-multprin-perm`]

Both multiply, and both can be about the same club and the same jobs. One sentence changes the kind.

**Problem A**

> A club of 6 members is to fill 3 jobs: president, secretary and treasurer. The same member may hold more than one job. In how many different ways can the jobs be filled?

**Problem B**

> A club of 6 members is to fill 3 jobs: president, secretary and treasurer. No member may hold more than one job. In how many different ways can the jobs be filled?

**What to compare.** Both problems are about the same club of 6 members and the same three jobs. Compare one thing: can one member hold more than one job?

**You are asked:** Which problem gives the answer **“Ways to pick from one group, where order matters”**? (Problem A / Problem B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Problem B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In A, one member can hold several jobs, so every job is picked from all 6 members. These are three separate choices from full lists: 6 × 6 × 6 = 216. That is **Multiplying the choices**.

In B, no member can hold two jobs. The first job takes someone out, so the second is picked from 5 and the third from 4: 6 × 5 × 4 = 120. That is **Permutations**.

**How to tell them apart**

After one pick, is the next one made from a list of the same length, or from what is left of the same group?


### 9. Combinations

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 9 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-comb`]

The same picks from the same group, but now the order does not matter.

*The ice at the picnic*

> Four friends, Ana, Ben, Cal and Dev, are at a picnic. ⟦Two of them are sent to fetch ice, and both do the same job⟧. In how many different ways can the pair be picked?

List the pairs: Ana and Ben, Ana and Cal, Ana and Dev, Ben and Cal, Ben and Dev, Cal and Dev. That is 6 pairs.

Compare the race. The same four friends and two picks gave 12 results there, because gold for Ana and silver for Ben is not gold for Ben and silver for Ana. Here “Ana and Ben” and “Ben and Ana” are one pair, so the 12 counted every pair twice: 12 ÷ 2 = 6.

So you count in two steps. First count the picks in order: 4 × 3 = 12. Then divide by the number of orders one pair can come in, 2, so each pair counts once.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the one group: Ana, Ben, Cal and Dev.** As in the race, the picks come out of a single group.
2. **Check the picks all do the same job: both fetch the ice.** Nobody is first or second.
3. **Ask whether the same people in another order are the same result: Ana and Ben is Ben and Ana.** If they are, you have to divide the extra orders away.

This is **Combinations**. Count the picks in order, then divide by the number of orders one group can come in.

You may also hear this called “selections” or “n choose r”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Combinations**.

The steps for this kind of problem, worked out on a quiz night.

**The problem**

> A quiz night needs a team of 4 players, and 9 people have put their names forward. Every player on the team has the same part, so the order of the names does not matter. How many different teams can be picked?

**The working, step by step**

- Count the group and the picks: Group: 9 people. Picked: 4

All four have the same part, so the same four people in another order are the same team.

- Count the picks as if the order mattered: 9 × 8 × 7 × 6 = 3,024

- Divide the first count by the second: 3,024 ÷ 24 = 126. That is 126 teams

- Every team is in the 3,024 once for each of its 24 orders, so dividing by 24 counts each team once.
- Dividing 3,024 by 24 gives 126, and 126 × 24 = 3,024.
- A team of 4 can be put in order in 24 ways.

**Shown as soon as you answer**

- The one that explains it: Every team is in the 3,024 once for each of its 24 orders, so dividing by 24 counts each team once.

  - If you chose “A team of 4 can be put in order in 24 ways.”: True, and that is where the 24 comes from. It does not say why you divide by it.

### 12. Permutations or Combinations: telling them apart

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 12 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-perm-comb`]

Both pick from one group and start from the same count. The test is whether the order counts.

**Problem A**

> A café owner has 6 pastries and puts 3 of them in a row in the window, from left to right. How many different rows can she make?

**Problem B**

> A café owner has 6 pastries and puts 3 of them in a box. The box is the same whichever pastry goes in first. How many different boxes can she make?

**What to compare.** Both problems are about the same café owner, the same 6 pastries and the same 3 picks. Compare one thing: does the order the pastries go in matter?

**You are asked:** Which problem gives the answer **“Ways to pick from one group, in any order”**? (Problem A / Problem B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Problem B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In A the pastries go in a row, left to right, so the same pastries in a different order are a different row. The order counts: 6 × 5 × 4 = 120. That is **Permutations**.

In B the pastries go in a box, and the box is the same whichever goes in first. Each box is in the 120 once for every order its three pastries can come in, 3 × 2 × 1 = 6, so 120 ÷ 6 = 20. That is **Combinations**.

**How to tell them apart**

Is the same group, picked in a different order, a different result or the same one?

## Part 2 of 3: How likely: at least one of several things, and trusting a test

### 13. Counting the opposite

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 13 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-complement`]

This one asks for a chance, not a count: how likely is it that something happens at least once?

*The coin game*

> A game is won if a fair coin lands heads ⟦at least once in 3 flips⟧. Each flip has a chance of 0.5 of landing heads, and one flip does not change the next. How likely is it that the game is won?

Write out every run of 3 flips: HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT. All 8 are equally likely, and every run except TTT has a head. So 7 of the 8 win: 7 ÷ 8 = 0.875, or 87.5%.

There is a quicker way, because only one run loses: TTT. Three tails in a row is 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.125. You either win or lose, so the two chances add up to 1, and the chance of winning is 1 − 0.125 = 0.875. The flips must be separate, so that the chances of tails can be multiplied.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the separate things that each have a chance: three flips, 0.5 each.** One flip does not change the next.
2. **Look for “at least one”: at least one head in 3 flips.** One head, two or three all win.
3. **Check the question asks how likely, not how many: how likely is it that the game is won?** The answer is a chance from 0 to 1, not a count.

This is **Counting the opposite**. Work out the chance that none of them happens, and take it away from 1.

You may also hear this called “the complement rule”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Counting the opposite**.

### 15. Worked: a bus that is late at least once in a week

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 15 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `solved`, id `solved-complement-1`]

The steps for this kind of problem, worked out on a commuter’s bus.

**The problem**

> A commuter’s bus is late on 20% of days, and one day’s lateness does not change the chance on another day. How likely is it that the bus is late at least once in a working week of 5 days?

**The working, step by step**

- Find the chance that each thing does not happen: Each day: 1 − 0.2 = 0.8

Each day the bus is late or it is not, so the two chances add up to 1. On time is 1 − 0.2 = 0.8, or 80 days in 100.

- Multiply those chances to get the chance that none happens: 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 = 0.32768

The bus must be on time on day 1 and day 2 and day 3 and day 4 and day 5. The days are separate, so the chances multiply: about 33 weeks in 100.

- Take that away from 1 to get the chance that at least one happens: 1 − 0.32768 = 0.67232, which is 67.2%

**You are asked:** This step carries the idea. Every statement below is true of the problem. Before you read the reason, choose the one that explains why this step is done.

- A week has either no late day or at least one, so the two chances add up to 1.
- The chance of a week with no late day is 0.32768.
- The bus is late on 20% of days, so a late day has a chance of 0.2.

**Shown as soon as you answer**

  - If you chose “The bus is late on 20% of days, so a late day has a chance of 0.2.”: True, but that is the chance for one day, not for the whole week.

### 16. Base rate

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 16 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-baserate`]

This one asks for a chance after the event: a test has already given its result, and you ask how far to trust it.

*The village clinic*

> In a village of 1,000 people, 1 person in 50 has a skin condition. ⟦A test shows the condition in 90% of the people who have it, and wrongly shows it in 5% of the people who do not⟧. A person’s test shows the condition. How likely is it that the person has it?

It is tempting to say 90%. But 90% is how often the test is positive for someone who has the condition. The question runs the other way: how often does someone with a positive test have it?

Chances are hard to mix in your head, so count people instead. Of 1,000 villagers, 20 have the condition and 980 do not. The test is positive for 18 of the 20 (90%), and wrongly for 49 of the 980 (5%). That is 67 positive tests, and only 18 of them are real: 18 ÷ 67, about 27%.

The condition is rare, so the 980 healthy people make far more wrong positives than the 20 sick people make right ones. If the condition were common, most positive tests would be right.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the result that has already come in: this person’s test shows the condition.** The test has been done, and you are reading it.
2. **Find how rare the thing is: 1 person in 50 has it.** This is the number people forget, and the answer depends on it.
3. **Find how often the test is wrong: it flags 5% of the people who do not have it.** Healthy people are the majority, so even a small error rate is a lot of people.
4. **Check the question asks how far to trust the result: how likely is it that the person has it?** It starts from the positive test, not from the people who have the condition.

This is **Base rate**. The name is how common the thing is before anyone is tested: here 1 in 50.

You may also hear this called “the base rate fallacy”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Base rate**.

### 18. Worked: how far to trust a positive test

The steps for this kind of problem, worked out on a quick test for an infection.

**The problem**

> In a town, 1 person in 100 has a particular infection. A quick test finds the infection in 90% of the people who have it, and wrongly shows it in 5% of the people who do not. A person’s test is positive. How likely is it that the person has the infection?

**The working, step by step**

- Imagine a big group and split it into people who have it and people who do not: Imagine 10,000 people. 1 in 100 have it: 100 have it and 9,900 do not

Counting imagined people is easier than mixing chances. 10,000 is used so that 1 in 100, 90% and 5% all give whole numbers of people.

- Count the positive results among those who have it: 90% of 100 = 90

The other 10 who have the infection test negative, because the test misses them.

- Count the positive results among those who do not have it: 5% of 9,900 = 495

**You are asked:** This step carries the idea. Every statement below is true of the problem. Before you read the reason, choose the one that explains why this step is done.

- People without the infection far outnumber people with it, so a 5% error rate still gives many wrong positives.
- The sum is 5% of 9,900, which is 495.
- The test finds the infection in 90% of the people who have it.

**Shown as soon as you answer**

- The one that explains it: People without the infection far outnumber people with it, so a 5% error rate still gives many wrong positives.
  - If you chose “The sum is 5% of 9,900, which is 495.”: True, but that is only the sum. It does not say why this step is needed.

A positive test means the person has the infection about 15 times in 100 (15.4%), even though the test finds the infection in 90% of the people who have it.

### 19. The question to ask before any counting

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 19 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-c1`]

Here is the question and its five answers in one place.

**The question:** **“What are you counting, or finding the chance of?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other four.

- **“Ways to pick one from each of several lists”**
  - Give this answer when there are several separate choices, like a size and a topping, each from its own full list, and you are asked how many different results there are.
  - It leads to **Multiplying the choices**.
- **“Ways to pick from one group, where order matters”**
  - Give this answer when things are picked one after another from one group, each pick leaves one fewer, and a different order is a different result, as with gold, silver and bronze.
  - It leads to **Permutations**.
- **“Ways to pick from one group, in any order”**
  - Give this answer when several things are picked from one group, each pick leaves one fewer, and the same things in a different order count as one result, as with a team.
  - It leads to **Combinations**.
- **“The chance that at least one of several things happens”**
  - Give this answer when you are given the chance of each of several separate things and asked how likely it is that at least one of them happens.
  - It leads to **Counting the opposite**.
- **“The chance that a test result is right”**
  - Give this answer when a test or a check has given a result, the thing it looks for is rare, the test is sometimes wrong, and you are asked how likely the result is to be right.
  - It leads to **Base rate**.

**Why it matters**

The same numbers give very different answers depending on how the picks are made: one from each of several lists, or several from one group, with the order mattering or not. The chance of at least one thing, and the chance a test is right, each need steps of their own too.

The wrong steps give a number just as neat as the right ones, and nothing in the number tells you something is wrong. Only the words of the problem do.

**How to answer it**

1. **Read the last sentence first.** It usually says what you are asked: how many results there are, or how likely something is.
2. **If it asks how many, check whether each pick has a list of its own: that is Multiplying the choices.** Then picking from one list uses nothing up on the others.
3. **If the picks all come out of one group, ask whether a different order is a different result: if it is, that is Permutations.** Gold for Ana and silver for Ben is not gold for Ben and silver for Ana.
4. **If the same people in any order are one result, that is Combinations.** A team is the same team in any order.
5. **If it asks how likely, check whether you need one or more of several separate things: that is Counting the opposite.** Then you work out the chance that none of them happens.
6. **If a test has already given a result and you must decide how far to trust it, that is Base rate.** How rare the thing is decides the answer.
7. **Find the exact words that show your answer.** If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Some problems look like two at once, such as **Multiplying the choices** and **Combinations**, or **Counting the opposite** and **Multiplying the choices** or **Base rate**. The test for each pair is below.

- Multiplying the choices or Permutations: After one pick, is the next one made from a list of the same length, or from what is left of the same group?
- Permutations or Combinations: Is the same group, picked in a different order, a different result or the same one?
- Multiplying the choices or Combinations: Does each list get one pick, or do several picks come from one list?
- Counting the opposite or Multiplying the choices: Do you want a count of results, or the chance that something happens?
- Counting the opposite or Base rate: Are there several separate things still to happen, each with its own chance, or one result that has already come in and a question about how far to trust it?

### 21. What to carry away

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 21 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap-chance`]

You have now worked all five kinds of problem on your own.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What are you counting, or finding the chance of?
- Ways to pick one from each of several lists → Multiplying the choices
- Ways to pick from one group, where order matters → Permutations
- Ways to pick from one group, in any order → Combinations
- The chance that at least one of several things happens → Counting the opposite
- The chance that a test result is right → Base rate

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Multiplying the choices**: several separate choices, each made from its own full list, and the question how many different results there are.
- **Permutations**: one group to pick from, each pick leaving one fewer, a different order counting as a different result, and the question how many different results there are.
- **Combinations**: one group to pick from, each pick leaving one fewer, the same things in any order counting as one result, and the question how many different results there are.
- **Counting the opposite**: several separate things, the chance of each, and the question how likely it is that at least one of them happens.
- **Base rate**: a test that has given a result, how rare the thing it looks for is, how often the test is wrong, and the question how likely the result is to be right.

**To carry away**

- Before you work anything out, ask what is being counted or what chance is wanted, and find the words that say it. The question is: **“What are you counting, or finding the chance of?”**
- When each choice has a full list of its own, it is **Multiplying the choices**: multiply the list sizes.
- When the picks come out of one group, and a different order is a different result, it is **Permutations**: multiply counts that fall by one each time.
- When the same people in any order are one result, it is **Combinations**: count the picks in order, then divide by the number of orders one group can come in.
- If you are asked how likely it is that one or more things happen, it is **Counting the opposite**: multiply the chances that each does not happen, and take that away from 1. Do not add the chances.
- If a test has given a result and you must decide how far to trust it, it is **Base rate**: imagine a big group, count the right and the wrong positives, and divide the right ones by all of them. How rare the thing is decides it.
