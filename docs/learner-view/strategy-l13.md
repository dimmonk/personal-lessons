# Strategy: A bet you can afford

Revision 1, draft (the screen carries the draft line). Part: A bet you can afford. Where the subject ends up: After this you can look at a business decision (expand, add a product, chase a big customer, answer a rival, invest) and tell whether it is a strategy, say where the business makes its money and why, and pick the move that keeps it different in a way rivals cannot copy, when you face that decision in your own business.

## The why

Some ideas have been tried a thousand times, and the record says how they go. Others have never been tried, and no one can say.
Experienced founders judge the two differently. Here you learn to tell which kind of bet you face, and to keep a bet on the unknown to what you can afford to lose.

## Step 1: Is there a record?

First, one question: has this kind of project been done many times, with a record of how it went? A second shop, a branch, a new truck: yes, and a county or state list often shows how long each lasted.
If there is a record, do what you did with the tables of others like it: start from how the others did, and size the bet from that.
If there is not (nobody sells it yet, so nobody can know who would buy), no forecast can tell you. Start from what you already have: your space, your skills, your customers. Bring in partners who share the risk. Bet only what you could lose without hurting the business, and learn from what happens.
Either way, borrowing for a full launch on a hope is the wrong move. Why it matters: a small bet on the unknown can teach you what the market is; a big one can end the business before you find out.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “Get a market forecast, and if it looks good, go all in.”
The right line: Where nobody has sold the thing before, a forecast is a guess with numbers on it. Try it small, with what you have and a partner, inside what you can lose.

## Step 2: Two ideas for one bakery

| A | B |
|---|---|
| A second bakery in the next town. Bakeries open every year, and the state lists how long each lasted. | Birthday cakes for dogs. No bakery in the region makes them, and nobody knows how many owners would pay. |

Compare: The first has a record: start from it. The second has none: try it with the ovens on a free day, inside what Dana could lose.

## Step 3: A worked example

Your own note, written after an argument: "Eve’s notes on an idea", Written in May.

> A swimming pool for dogs, for exercise and for dogs getting over an injury.
> Nobody in the state runs one; owners at the daycare say it sounds fun, but nobody knows whether they would pay $25 a swim.
> We already have the fenced acre, and a used pool would cost $3,000; a vet nearby says she would send dogs getting over surgery.
> We could lose $5,000 without hurting the daycare; an indoor pool would cost $200,000.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**Which way of judging this bet fits?**
- Look at how similar projects turned out
- Bet only what you can afford to lose, and learn as you go

The working, one step at a time:
1. Has this kind of project been done many times, with a record?: No: nobody in the state runs one, and nobody knows whether owners would pay. There is no record to look at.
1. Start from what is already there, with partners, and cap the bet.: The acre is hers, a used pool is $3,000, the vet will send dogs, and she can lose $5,000. So a small trial, not a $200,000 pool.

Then the answer: Bet only what you can afford to lose, and learn as you go / Put a $3,000 used pool on the acre, let the vet send dogs, and see who pays. Nobody in the state runs a dog pool, so there is no record and no forecast can say who would pay. Eve starts from her acre, a $3,000 pool and the vet as a partner, inside the $5,000 she can lose, and learns who pays.
