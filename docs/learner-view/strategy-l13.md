# Strategy: A bet you can afford

Revision 1, live. Part: A bet you can afford. Where the subject ends up: After this you can look at a business decision (expand, add a product, chase a big customer, answer a rival, invest) and tell whether it is a strategy, say where the business makes its money and why, and pick the move that keeps it different in a way rivals cannot copy, when you face that decision in your own business.

## The why

Some ideas are things people already buy, and the record of many like them says how they go. Others have never been offered to these customers, and no one can say who would buy.
Experienced founders judge the two differently, and size every bet to what they can afford to lose. Here you learn both.

## Step 1: Can anyone see who would buy?

First question: can anyone see yet whether people would buy it? A second shop, a branch, a new truck outside a factory: people already buy these, and many like them have opened. Start from how they did, as in the tables of others like it.
Something these customers have never been offered is different. The wider record (about half of new US shops and offices are still open after five years) is only a rough start: no table can say whether anyone here wants this. Try it small, with what you already have and partners who share the cost, and learn who buys.
Second question, for every bet: what can you afford to lose? That is the most you could lose and still keep the business and your household going. To find it, take the cash you hold, keep back a few months of household bills and of the business’s own bills and loans, and what is left is the most you could lose. The stake, what you would lose if it fails, stays within it, however good the record. Even a good record loses some.
So size the bet. If the whole project fits within what you can lose and the record is sound, go ahead in full. If it costs more, put less at risk: rent instead of buy, start with a part, take a partner who pays a share. If no version fits, not now.
Why it matters: a bet sized to what you can lose lets you try again; one bigger than that can end the business before you find out.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “Get a forecast, and if it looks good, go all in.”
The right line: A forecast cannot see buyers who have never been offered the thing, and even a good record loses some. Start from the record where there is one, try it small where there is not, and never stake more than you can lose.

## Step 3: A worked example

A written note: "Eve’s idea, with its numbers", Written in May.

> A swimming pool for dogs, for exercise and for dogs getting over an injury.
> Dog pools do well in big cities, but nobody in our rural county has offered one, so nobody knows whether owners here would pay $25 a swim.
> We already have the fenced acre, a used pool would cost $3,000, and a vet nearby would send dogs getting over surgery.
> We could lose $5,000 and still keep the daycare and our home going; an indoor pool would cost $200,000.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**Which way of judging this bet fits?**
- People already buy this: start from how similar projects turned out
- Nobody can see yet whether people would buy: try it small and learn

The working, one step at a time:
1. Can anyone see yet whether people here would buy it?: No: dog pools exist in cities, but owners here have never been offered one. The wider record is a rough start; it cannot say whether anyone here would pay.
1. Size the bet: what could be lost, and what is at hand?: Eve can lose $5,000. The acre is hers, a used pool is $3,000 and the vet will send dogs: a small trial inside that, not a $200,000 pool.

Then the answer: Nobody can see yet whether people would buy: try it small and learn / Put a $3,000 used pool on the acre, let the vet send dogs, and see who pays. Nobody can see yet whether owners here would pay, so no record or study settles it. Eve starts from her acre, a $3,000 pool and the vet as a partner, inside the $5,000 she can lose, and learns who pays.
