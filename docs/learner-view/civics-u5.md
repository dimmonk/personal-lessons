## Part 1 of 2: Is the law allowed, and what do its words cover?

### 1. What a judge is really being asked

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

Before you take a news story about a judge at face value, or decide to take a problem to court, check what the judge is actually being asked to do. It is always one of four things, and it decides what a judge can fix and what only voters can.

You have heard stories like these. “A judge has said the town cannot enforce its new rule.” “The judge said the law does not cover scooters.” “A judge turned down the request to lower the bus fare.” “The judge ruled that the police should have asked permission before they searched the car.”

In every one of them a judge decides something, and it is never the same thing twice. Mix them up and you will misread what the news means for you. The question is the same for a judge in a court of the whole country and for a judge in a court of one state.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“Who makes the final call?”** Its answers:

- **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**: give this answer when the final call is a vote in the House or Senate, or someone asks Congress for one, such as on a law or money. A law stays Congress’s call even once the President signs it.
- **“The President or a federal agency”**: give this answer when the final call is the President’s or a federal agency’s, or someone is asking them for one, such as an agency enforcing a law or the President refusing to sign one.
- **“A judge, in any court”**: give this answer when the final call is a judge’s, in any court, federal or state, or someone is asking a judge to decide. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“A state, city or county government”**: give this answer when the final call is made by a state’s lawmakers, governor or agencies, or a city, town or county, or someone asks them for it. A state court judge counts as a judge.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. Judicial review

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-review`]

First: a judge asked whether a law is allowed.

*The leaflet fine*

> A town passes a rule that nobody may hand out leaflets in the main square. Marisol ⟦was fined $50 for handing out leaflets about a school vote⟧. She took the town to court and ⟦told the judge that the rule goes against the right to speak that the Constitution protects⟧. The judge must now decide whether it does.

Marisol does not argue about whether she handed out leaflets. She says the rule itself is not allowed, because the Constitution protects her right to speak. The Constitution sits above every other law in the country, so a rule that clashes with it cannot stand.

The judge is not asked whether the rule is a good idea, only whether it clashes. If it does, the judge refuses to apply the rule and the town can no longer use it. A judge does this only for someone the rule has actually harmed, as it did Marisol.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the person the law harmed: Marisol, fined $50.** A judge only checks a law for someone it has really hurt.
2. **Find what she says about the law: it goes against her right to speak.** She says the law itself is not allowed.
3. **Find the question put to the judge: does the rule fit the Constitution?** The judge is asked to check the law, not to say whether it is a good idea.

This is **Judicial review**: the judge takes a second look at a law the lawmakers already made.

You may also hear this called “striking down a law” or “ruling a law unconstitutional”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Judicial review**.

### 4. What a judge does with an earlier ruling

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-precedent`]

The next name uses one more word, so here it comes first, with a story.

*The food trucks*

> Two years ago a judge in Marlow’s court ruled that a food truck is a shop under the town’s licensing law, so its owner needed a shop license. This spring a different judge in the same town is asked the same question about another owner’s food truck. She reads the earlier ruling and decides it the same way.

The town’s licensing law did not say whether a food truck is a “shop”, so the first judge decided that it is. That ruling stays on the record.

This spring a different judge got the same question about another owner’s truck. She did not start from nothing. She read what the first judge had decided about the same word, and decided it the same way.

**The word for this.** *Precedent*: an earlier court ruling on the same question, which later courts follow.

So when a judge works out what the words of a law cover, earlier rulings on the same words are one of the first places to look.


### 5. Interpreting a law

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 5 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-interpret`]

Second: a judge asked what a law’s words cover.

*The rooftop hives*

> A state law gives a lower tax bill to farms. Dora keeps eight beehives on a flat roof in the city and sells the honey. The tax office says that a roof is not a farm and sent her the full bill. Dora has ⟦asked a judge to decide whether her hives make her roof a farm under the law⟧. ⟦She does not say that the law is wrong⟧.

Nobody says the farm law is wrong. The trouble is one word. Whoever wrote “farm” was probably thinking of fields and barns, not beehives on a roof, and the word does not say yes or no. The tax office and Dora each want it to mean whatever suits them.

So the judge decides what the word covers, and does not choose what would be best. The judge goes to the law: its words, the rest of the law around them, what it was for, and any earlier ruling on the same words. That last one is a *precedent*.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the law nobody attacks: the lower tax bill for farms.** Nobody says it clashes with the Constitution.
2. **Find the word in dispute: “farm”. Does it reach eight beehives on a roof?** The whole question is how far one word stretches.
3. **Find the request: Dora asks the judge to decide whether her roof is a farm.** The judge is asked to read the law, not to say whether the law is good.

This is **Interpreting a law**: the judge works out what the law means for a situation its writers never pictured.

You may also hear this called “statutory interpretation”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Interpreting a law**.

## Part 2 of 2: A better rule, an accused person, then the drill

### 8. Left to the voters

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 8 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-notlegal`]

Third: someone asks a judge to choose a better rule, and no law decides it.

*The bus fare*

> The bus fare in Alder Town is $2. A group of regular riders ⟦asks a judge to order the town council to cut the fare to $1⟧, saying that would be fairer to people who earn little. ⟦No law sets what a bus fare must be, and nobody says the fare takes away a right⟧. The council voted to keep the fare at $2.

The riders do not say the fare breaks the Constitution, and they do not ask what a law means. They ask the judge to choose between $2 and $1. People disagree about that for good reasons, as they do about taxes and speed limits.

No law or right decides it, so a judge who chose would be putting their own view in place of the council’s. That choice belongs to voters and the leaders they elect, so the judge says no. The riders can still vote, sign petitions and write to the council. A lawsuit will not change the fare.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find what the riders ask the judge to do: cut the fare to $1.** They want the judge to pick a better rule.
2. **Look for a law that sets the fare, or a right the fare takes away: there is none.** With no law and no right, the judge has nothing to read or check.
3. **Find their reason: it would be fairer.** That is a view about what is better, and voters can settle it.

This is **Left to the voters**. A judge says no, because the choice belongs to the people who vote.

You may also hear this called “a political question” or “a matter for the voters”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Left to the voters**.

### 11. The rights of the accused

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 11 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-trialrights`]

Fourth: a judge asked whether the government treated an accused person fairly.

*The roadside search*

> Police stop Joy’s car because a rear light is broken. One officer opens the trunk and ⟦searches it with no warrant and without asking Joy⟧, and finds a box of stolen tools. Joy is charged with theft. At her trial her lawyer asks the judge to decide ⟦whether the search was unreasonable, as the Fourth Amendment forbids⟧.

Joy does not say the theft law is wrong. She says the police skipped a step the Constitution promises anyone accused of a crime: no unreasonable search. Usually that means a warrant, which is a judge’s written permission to search. The judge is asked whether the police skipped it.

The Constitution promises other steps too: the right to stay silent, a lawyer (a free one if you cannot pay), a speedy public trial by jury, no excessive bail or fines, and no cruel or unusual punishment. They protect everyone accused of a crime, citizen or not.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the person accused of a crime: Joy, charged with theft.** These steps protect someone the government has accused.
2. **Find the step that may have been skipped: the police searched her trunk with no warrant.** The Constitution promises these steps whatever the crime.
3. **Check that nobody attacks the law itself: nobody says the theft law is wrong.** The question is how Joy was treated, not whether the law is allowed.

This is **The rights of the accused**. The judge is asked whether the government followed the steps, not whether Joy did it.

You may also hear this called “trial rights” or “a fair trial”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **The rights of the accused**.

### 16. The one question to ask about a judge

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 16 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-judge`]

Here is the question and its four answers in one place.

**The question:** **“What is the judge asked to decide?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other three.

- **“Check a law against the Constitution”**
  - Give this answer when someone the law actually harmed, like a person fined or charged, goes to court saying the law, or what the government did under it, breaks the Constitution.
  - It leads to **Judicial review**.
- **“Make sure an accused person was treated fairly”**
  - Give this answer when someone is accused of a crime, and the judge is asked whether they got the protections the Constitution promises, like a lawyer or a jury trial.
  - It leads to **The rights of the accused**.
- **“Say what the words of a law cover”**
  - Give this answer when nobody says the law breaks the Constitution, and the question is whether its words reach a situation, decided from the words, what the law was for, and earlier rulings.
  - It leads to **Interpreting a law**.
- **“Choose which policy is better”**
  - Give this answer when someone asks the judge to decide what would be wiser or fairer, and no law or right decides it, so the choice belongs to the voters and those they elect.
  - It leads to **Left to the voters**.

**Why it matters**

Each of the four names is one thing a judge can be asked to decide. Which court it is, how big the news is, or whether the judge agrees changes nothing.

Two stories about the very same rule can need different names, and the topic, the people and how serious it sounds tell you nothing. Only what the judge is asked to do does. Get it wrong and you will expect a judge to fix something only voters can.

**How to answer it**

1. **Find the request to the judge: the sentence with “asks the judge to decide” or “asks a judge to order”.** The fine, the charge and the vote only show how it got there.
2. **Does the person say the law breaks the Constitution? Then it is Judicial review.** They are saying the law itself is not allowed.
3. **Does the person say a promised step was skipped? Then it is The rights of the accused.** The law is accepted, and the complaint is how the person was treated.
4. **Does the person ask whether the law’s words reach what happened? Then it is Interpreting a law.** Nobody says the law is wrong, so the judge reads its words.
5. **Is the judge asked to choose a better rule, with no law or right to settle it? Then it is Left to the voters.** There is nothing to read or check, so the judge says no.
6. **Find the exact words that show your answer.** If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Sometimes two answers seem to fit: a person charged under a law may ask what its words cover, or say the law breaks the Constitution. Go back to the words in the story. The test for each pair is below.

- Judicial review or Interpreting a law: Does the person say the law should not exist, or only ask whether it covers what they did?
- Judicial review or Left to the voters: Does the person name a right in the Constitution that the rule breaks, or only say a different rule would be better?
- Interpreting a law or Left to the voters: Is there a law whose words, or earlier rulings on them, can answer the question, or is the person asking the judge to choose?
- Judicial review or The rights of the accused: Does the person say the law itself is not allowed, or that a step promised to someone accused was skipped?
- Interpreting a law or The rights of the accused: Is the judge asked whether the law covers what the person did, or whether the person was treated as the Constitution promises?

### 19. What to carry away

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 19 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap`]

You have now answered the question on your own.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What is the judge asked to decide?
- Check a law against the Constitution → Judicial review
- Make sure an accused person was treated fairly → The rights of the accused
- Say what the words of a law cover → Interpreting a law
- Choose which policy is better → Left to the voters

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Judicial review**: a law, or something the government did under it, a person it actually harmed taking it to court, and a judge asked whether it breaks the Constitution.
- **Interpreting a law**: a law nobody says breaks the Constitution, a situation its words may or may not reach, and a judge asked to decide.
- **Left to the voters**: someone asking a judge to pick the better policy, with no law and no right in the Constitution that decides it.
- **The rights of the accused**: a person accused of a crime, a protection the Constitution promises them, like a lawyer or a jury, and a judge asked whether they got it.

**To carry away**

- Find what the judge is asked to do, and find the exact words that show it. If you cannot, you do not have an answer yet.
- The story never decides it. Neither does the size of the fine, the importance of the right or a courtroom: a story about a protester can be **Interpreting a law**, and a story with a courtroom in it can be **Judicial review**.
- It is **Judicial review** only when someone a law has harmed says the law breaks the Constitution. “Unfair” is not enough, and neither is a right mentioned in passing.
- If a rule is only unfair or unwise, with no law or right it breaks, a judge will say no and the choice is left to voters. Voting, petitions and writing to the council are what work.
