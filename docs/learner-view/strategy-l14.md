# Strategy: Run the six questions

Revision 1, draft (the screen carries the draft line). Part: Run the six questions. Where the subject ends up: After this you can look at a business decision (expand, add a product, chase a big customer, answer a rival, invest) and tell whether it is a strategy, say where the business makes its money and why, and pick the move that keeps it different in a way rivals cannot copy, when you face that decision in your own business.

## The why

Each question so far was asked on its own. A real decision needs all of them, in order, and each answer feeds the next.
Strategists work through the same short run of questions on any decision. Here you take whole decisions through all six, then face ten new questions from every topic, with no help.

## Step 1: The six questions, in order

1. Is it a strategy problem? If the business is behind rivals at the same work, run it better. If it already runs as well as they do and is squeezed anyway, it needs a different position.
2. What is in the way, and why? A number that fell is not yet an answer.
3. Where does the money come from? The line customers come for that earns the most after its costs, not the biggest seller, and who takes the rest.
4. What makes the business different, what does that give it, and what stops a rival copying it?
5. The choice: where to play and how to win, what it will not do, and activities that fit.
6. Before betting: has this been done many times, with a record? Start from the record. If not, bet only what you can lose.
Why it matters: each question catches a mistake the others miss. Skipping one is how a sensible-looking plan goes wrong.

## Step 2: A worked example

Your own note, written after an argument: "Dana’s notes for the year ahead", Written in January.

> Our ovens, bakers and waste are as good as any bakery’s in the county.
> Since the supermarket opened its own bakery, selling a loaf at half our price, our bread sales fell by a third.
> Bread still brings in the most money; wedding cakes earn the most after their costs, and couples drive across the county for them.
> Our cakes rest on a talk with each couple, bakers who make nothing else, and photos of every past cake: the supermarket would have to change how its bakery runs to match all of it.
> Cake shops that make cakes to order have opened in many towns, and the state lists how long each lasted.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**1. What does this business need?**
- Run it better
- Choose a different position

The working, one step at a time:
1. 1. Is it a strategy problem?: Dana already runs as well as any bakery, and the supermarket sells at half her price. Running better will not fix that: she needs a different position.
1. 2. What is in the way?: The supermarket’s bread at half the price, which cut her bread sales by a third.
1. 3. Where does the money come from?: Wedding cakes: they earn the most after their costs, and couples come from across the county for them. Bread only brings in the most.
1. 4. What stops a copy?: The cakes rest on several pieces that fit: the talk, the bakers, the photos. The supermarket would have to change all of it.
1. 5. Which plan chooses?: Cakes made to order for parties, with bread cut back. Cutting the bread price takes on the supermarket where it is strongest.
1. 6. How to judge the bet?: Cake shops like it have opened in many towns and the state lists how they did: start from that record.

Then the answer: Choose a different position / Since the supermarket opened its own bakery, selling a loaf at half our price, our bread sales fell by a third. / Wedding cakes, which couples come for and which earn the most / It rests on many activities that fit together: a rival would have to copy them all / Play for couples and parents who want a cake made for their party; win with cakes made to order; keep only three loaves / Look at how similar projects turned out. Dana runs as well as any bakery, so the supermarket’s half-price bread is a strategy problem. Her money is in wedding cakes, which rest on pieces the supermarket would have to copy all at once; the plan that chooses plays for cake buyers and cuts bread back. Cake shops have a record, so she judges the bet from it.
