## Part 1 of 2: Money, a password, an app

### 1. Facts to hold, for the day something has already gone wrong

*Unit Six · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 37*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient-late`]

These are facts to hold: what to do, and whom to tell, once something has already left your hands. By the end you can say each fact without looking it up, which is what you need on the day it matters, when you are upset and in a hurry.

The first hours count: money can sometimes still be recalled, and an account can be locked before the damage spreads. A fact that you already know costs you no time at all.

Think of the moment after you press send on a payment, and then you see the reply that shows it was a scam. Your stomach drops. Three thoughts cost people the time they need: ‘I’m so stupid’, ‘I’ll sort it out myself’, and ‘If I pay one more fee, I’ll get it back’.

They are what being tricked feels like, and what the scammer is counting on. A person who already knows the first move can make it before the feeling takes over.

**What this unit is**

This unit is facts to hold, not a skill to apply. There are no questions to work through. Each fact is something you will be asked from memory, and it comes back on later days.

**The unit holds 21 facts, in six groups:**

- Money has already gone
- A password has already been typed in
- A code has been read out, or an app has been allowed
- Something was installed, or someone watched your device
- Papers or numbers have been sent
- Someone offers to get your money back

The unit has two parts, and you can stop after any of them.

1. Money, a password, an app
2. A device, papers and numbers, the offer that follows a loss, then the drill

Whether a payment can still be recalled depends on your bank. No step here promises that anything will come back; each one only gives you a chance.

Each group starts with a case, then the idea in plain words, then the facts. After each fact you are asked it from memory, and the answer and how it fits are shown right away. Nothing here is graded. A miss only decides what comes back.

### 2. Money has already gone

*Unit Six · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 37*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-money`]

The first group is about the loss that moves fastest and is hardest to undo: money that you sent yourself.

*Priya’s payment*

> Priya pays her builder’s invoice of $2,000 into the new account named in an email that says the builder has changed banks. An hour later the builder calls about something else and mentions that nothing has changed on his side. Priya’s stomach drops. She thinks: ‘I’m so stupid. I’ll sort it out myself. Perhaps I should wait and see.’ Then she takes her bank card out of her wallet and calls the number printed on the back of it. She says the payment was a scam payment and asks the bank to try to recall it. The bank takes the details, and says it will try but cannot promise that the money will come back.

Priya did not wait, and she did not try to sort it out herself. She went straight to the one place that can try to get a payment back: the bank that sent it for her.

In the first hours, a payment can sometimes still be recalled, which means that your bank asks the bank that received the money to send it back. Nobody can promise that it works, but every hour you wait uses some of the chance up. A call that turns out to be unnecessary costs ten minutes. A call that you put off can cost the money.

After the call, report the scam to the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and keep the messages: leave the chat, the emails and the call log as they are. Scammers rely on embarrassment to keep people quiet, and a person who keeps quiet gives the scammer time. Speed matters more than shame.

### 9. A password has already been typed in

*Unit Six · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 9 of 37*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-password`]

The first group was about money. The second is about a way into an account: a password that you typed into a page that was not the real one.

*Hana’s email password*

> Hana gets a text: ‘Your package is held. Sign in to your email to confirm your address.’ The link opens a page that looks like her email provider’s sign-in page. She types her email address and her password, and the page says ‘Thank you’. That evening she sees that the address of the page was not her provider’s. She uses the same password for her online store and for her gym account.

Hana typed her email address and her password into a page that only looked like her provider’s. Whoever runs that page now has both.

The password cannot be taken back out of their hands. What she can do is make it useless, and that means changing it. If she followed a link in a message to change it, that would be the same trick again. So she goes to the real site, through *a way you already had*: an address that she types in herself or saved long ago, or an app that she installed herself.

She also changes the same password wherever else she used it, because the scammer can try it on her other accounts. Hana used hers for her online store and for her gym account, so those need a new one each. Last, she switches on two-step sign-in, which asks for a code or an approval as well as the password. With it, a stolen password on its own is not enough.

### 14. A code has been read out, or an app has been allowed

*Unit Six · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 14 of 37*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-app`]

A password is one way into an account. The third group is about two others: a *one-time code* that you read out, and an app that you pressed Allow for.

*Tessa’s shared document*

> Tessa gets a message from a colleague with a link to a shared document. The link opens a box: ‘DocView would like to read, send and delete all your mail. Allow or Cancel.’ She presses Allow. Later her colleague says she never sent the message. Tessa changes her email password at once and feels better. Two days later, her mail contacts tell her that they are still getting messages from her that she did not write.

Look at what Tessa did, and what it did not do. She pressed Allow on a box that asked whether DocView could read, send and delete all her mail. That box was a *permission screen*. Then she changed her password at once, and the messages went on coming from her account.

An app that you allow does not use the password. It was given access by your pressing Allow, so it carries on using that access until the permission is taken away. A new password does not take it away. Only removing the app does, and it works at once. You do it in your account’s settings, in the list of connected apps, and you remove any app that you cannot place.

A *one-time code* works once, so what matters after you have read one out is what was done with it. The code came from your bank or the provider of the account, so they are the ones to tell. The same goes for an app that you allowed and do not trust. Changing the password is still worth doing, as part of the same job.

### 19. Removing an app, and changing the password

*Unit Six · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 19 of 37*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-app`]

Two of the three facts are both things you do to an account after an app was allowed. People swap them, and the story of Tessa shows what the swap costs.

**Fact A**

> What takes an app’s access to your account away at once?

**Fact B**

> What do you also change, even though it does not take the app’s access away?

**What to compare.** Compare what each one takes away: the app’s access, or only the old password.

**You are asked:** Which of these two facts has the answer “Remove the app in your account’s connected-apps list”? (Fact A / Fact B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Fact A.

**Why this one and not the other**

Fact A is the one that works on the app: **Remove the app in your account’s connected-apps list**. It takes away what gave the app its access, and it works at once.

Fact B is only a part of the job: **Change the password as well**. It changes what a person types to sign in, and an app that you allowed does not type it.

That is why Tessa’s password change did not stop the messages. She did the second and not the first.

**How to tell them apart**

Does it take away the app’s access, or does it only change the password?

*Sam’s refund call*

> Rosa is offered a job by an employer she has never met. Before any contract, a form asks her to upload a photo of her passport, a photo of herself holding it, and her Social Security number. She also reports it at IdentityTheft.gov, the FTC’s site for this kind of crime.

She watches her accounts afterwards, because what she sent can be used later and not only on the day she sent it. And she changes any password that matches what she sent.

### 31. Someone offers to get your money back

*Unit Six · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 31 of 37*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-second`]

The five groups so far are about what to do about the first loss. The sixth is about the second thing that often follows it: an offer to put the first right.

*Abe’s second message*

> Abe lost $14,000 to an investment site ten months ago and has told nobody but his bank. A message arrives: ‘We are a fund recovery firm. We have traced your $14,000. Pay a retainer of $2,500 today and we will release it to you.’ Abe thinks: ‘If I pay one more fee, I’ll get it back.’

Abe has already lost money once, and has told nobody but his bank. Now a stranger has found him and offers to get it back, for a fee. It reads like help. It is also how people who have lost money are so often contacted again: the people who took the money often come back. If you have already paid someone like that, tell your bank.

Real help with a loss comes from your bank and from the FTC, which takes fraud reports at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. You go to them, they do not charge you, and they do not contact you first in order to sell you help. A bank may call you about fraud on your account, and if one does, *the check* settles it: hang up, and call the number on your card, which is *a way you already had*.

- **Where does real help with a loss come from?** Your bank and the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov
- **What do those real places not do?** Charge you, or contact you first to sell you help

**To carry away**

- Each thing that can leave your hands has its own first move: money, a password, a code, an app, a device, papers and numbers. Know which one you are dealing with, and make that move first.
- For money, the first hours are the chance. A call that was not needed costs ten minutes, and a call put off can cost the money.
- Changing a password and removing an app are two different jobs. A new password does not take away an app that you allowed.
- Use only *a way you already had* for every call and every sign-in after a scam. A number, a link or an app that came with the scam is never one, even if you are the one who dials it or taps it.
- Anyone who contacts you offering to get your money back is a warning in itself. Real help comes from your bank and from the FTC (ReportFraud.ftc.gov), and you go to them.

### 37. A plan, if you want one
