## Part 1 of 4: Money that has been sent, and telling the right people

### 1. Facts to hold, for the day something has already gone wrong

*Unit Six · rev 2 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 4 · Card 1 of 54*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient-late`]

This unit is different from the others in this subject. They teach you to tell one kind of request from another. This one is a set of facts to hold: what to do, and whom to tell, once something has already left your hands. By the end you can say each fact without looking it up, which is what you need on the day it matters, when you are upset and in a hurry.

The facts are worth holding because the first hours count. In the first hours, money can sometimes still be recalled, and an account can be locked before the damage spreads. The chance is in those hours. A fact that you already know costs you no time at all, and time is the one thing that you do not have on that day.

Think of the moment after you press send on a payment, and then you see the reply that shows it was a scam. Your stomach drops. Three thoughts cost people the time they need: ‘I’m so stupid’, ‘I’ll sort it out myself’, and ‘If I pay one more fee, I’ll get it back’.

None of those is a fact about scams. They are what being tricked feels like, and they are what the scammer is counting on. A person who already knows the first move can make it before the feeling takes over.

**What this unit is**

This unit is facts to hold, not a skill to apply. There are no questions to work through. Each fact is something you will be asked from memory, and it comes back on later days.

**The unit holds 31 facts, in seven groups:**

- Money has already gone
- Tell the right people, and keep what you have
- A password has already been typed in
- A code has been read out, or an app has been allowed
- Something was installed, or someone watched your device
- Papers or numbers have been sent
- Someone offers to get your money back

The unit has four parts, and you can stop after any of them.

1. Money that has been sent, and telling the right people
2. A password, a code or an app
3. A device, and papers or numbers
4. The offer that follows a loss, then the drill

Each group in this unit starts from one thing that can leave your hands, such as money, a way into an account, control of a device, or papers and numbers. It opens with a short story, then explains the idea, then gives the facts for that group in a table. After the table, each fact is asked once, from memory.

One thing in this unit depends on which bank you use: whether a payment can still be recalled. Where that is so, the unit says so. The unit does not promise that any step will get anything back. It says what the steps are, because they are what gives you a chance.

Each group starts with a case, then the idea in plain words, then the facts. After each fact you are asked it from memory, and the answer and how it fits are shown right away. Nothing here is graded. A miss only decides what comes back.

### 2. Money has already gone

*Unit Six · rev 2 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 4 · Card 2 of 54*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-money`]

The first group is about the loss that moves fastest and is hardest to undo: money that you sent yourself.

*Priya’s payment*

> Priya pays her builder’s invoice of $2,000 into the new account named in an email that says the builder has changed banks. An hour later the builder calls about something else and mentions that nothing has changed on his side. Priya’s stomach drops. She thinks: ‘I’m so stupid. I’ll sort it out myself. Perhaps I should wait and see.’ Then she takes her bank card out of her wallet and calls the number printed on the back of it. She says the payment was a scam payment and asks the bank to try to recall it. The bank takes the details, and says it will try but cannot promise that the money will come back.

Look at what Priya did, and at what she did not do. She was tempted to wait, and to sort it out herself, and she did neither. She went straight to the one place that can try to get a payment back: the bank that sent it for her.

Money is the loss that moves fastest. A payment that you sent yourself is hard for your bank to take back, and the bank’s own fraud checks usually let it through, because you were the one who sent it. That is why this group comes first.

There is one good chance, and it is short. In the first hours, a payment can sometimes still be recalled, which means that your bank asks the bank that received the money to send it back. Whether that works depends on your bank and on how quickly you call, and nobody can promise it. What is certain is that every hour you wait uses some of the chance up. A call that turns out to be unnecessary costs ten minutes. A call that you put off can cost the money.

The five facts below are the whole call: whom you call, which number, one trap if the scam began with a phone call, what you say, and how soon.


### 3. The call to your bank

*Unit Six · rev 2 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 4 · Card 3 of 54*

[reviewers only: card kind `facts`, id `facts-money`]

These are the five facts of the call, each with how it fits the idea that the first hours are the chance.

| The fact | The answer |
|---|---|
| Whom do you call first after money has gone to a scammer? | Call your bank |
| Which number do you call? | Use the number on your card |
| The scam began with a phone call. What do you do before you call your bank? | Wait a few minutes, or use a different phone |
| What do you say to your bank on that call? | Say it was a scam payment, and ask the bank to try to recall it |
| How soon do you call? | Call right away |

**How each fact fits the idea**

- **Call your bank**: Your bank is the one that sent the payment, so it is the one you can ask to try to get it back. It comes before anyone else because the first hours are the chance.
- **Use the number on your card**: The number on the back of your card was yours before the scam began, so it is *a way you already had*. A number that came with the scam never is, even if you are the one who dials it.
- **Wait a few minutes, or use a different phone**: A scammer can keep the line open and answer your call to ‘the bank’. Waiting a few minutes, or using a different phone, is what makes sure that your call goes to your bank and not to them.
- **Say it was a scam payment, and ask the bank to try to recall it**: The first part tells the bank what it is dealing with: a payment that you were tricked into making. The second part is what you are asking it to do about it. Recalling is the job that the first hours are for.
- **Call right away**: In the first hours a payment can sometimes still be recalled. That is the chance, and waiting uses it up. A call that was not needed costs ten minutes, and a call put off can cost the money.

### 10. Tell the right people, and keep what you have

*Unit Six · rev 2 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 4 · Card 10 of 54*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-report`]

The call to your bank is for getting money back. Whatever was lost, there is a second job: telling the people who deal with scams, and keeping the record of what happened.

*Dan’s deleted chat*

> Dan sends $600 to a ‘safe account’ because a caller who says he is from the bank tells him to. When he understands what has happened, he calls his bank. Then he deletes the whole chat with the caller, because he is ashamed, and tells nobody for three days. When he finally decides to report it, the messages are gone, and all he has is what he remembers of what was said.

Dan called his bank, and that part was right. What went wrong was what came after. Out of embarrassment he deleted the chat and told nobody for three days. Each day cost him something. The messages were the record of what the caller had said and asked for, and once they were deleted he could not show them to anyone.

There are two things to do beside the call to your bank. The first is to report the scam to the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and to do it after the call to your bank. If the scam happened over the internet, you can also report it to the FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center at IC3.gov. The second is to keep the messages: leave the chat, the emails and the call log as they are.

Under both is a feeling. Scammers rely on embarrassment to keep people quiet, and a person who keeps quiet gives the scammer time. Speed matters more than shame.

## Part 2 of 4: A password, a code or an app

### 15. A password has already been typed in

*Unit Six · rev 2 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 4 · Card 15 of 54*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-password`]

The first two groups were about money and about reporting. The third is about a way into an account: a password that you typed into a page that was not the real one.

*Hana’s email password*

> Hana gets a text: ‘Your package is held. Sign in to your email to confirm your address.’ The link opens a page that looks like her email provider’s sign-in page. She types her email address and her password, and the page says ‘Thank you’. That evening she sees that the address of the page was not her provider’s. She uses the same password for her online store and for her gym account.

Hana typed her email address and her password into a page that only looked like her provider’s. Whoever runs that page now has both. The page said ‘Thank you’ and looked finished, but for Hana the problem had only started.

The password cannot be taken back out of their hands. What she can do is make it useless, and that means changing it. How she changes it matters, because the same trick can be played twice. If she followed a link in a message to change it, that would be the same thing again. So she goes to the real site, through *a way you already had*: an address that she types in herself or saved long ago, or an app that she installed herself.

She does it from a different device, if she can, so that the new password is typed somewhere that nothing from the scam has touched. And she changes the same password wherever else she used it, because the scammer now has it and can try it on her other accounts. Hana used hers for her online store and for her gym account, so those need a new one each.

Last, she switches on two-step sign-in, which asks for a code or an approval as well as the password. With it, a stolen password on its own is not enough. It does not protect you from a code that you read out yourself.

### 22. A code has been read out, or an app has been allowed

*Unit Six · rev 2 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 4 · Card 22 of 54*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-app`]

A password is one way into an account. The fourth group is about two others: a *one-time code* that you read out, and an app that you pressed Allow for.

*Tessa’s shared document*

> Tessa gets a message from a colleague with a link to a shared document. The link opens a box: ‘DocView would like to read, send and delete all your mail. Allow or Cancel.’ She presses Allow. Later her colleague says she never sent the message. Tessa changes her email password at once and feels better. Two days later, her mail contacts tell her that they are still getting messages from her that she did not write.

Look at what Tessa did, and what it did not do. She pressed Allow on a box that asked whether DocView could read, send and delete all her mail. That box was a *permission screen*. Then she changed her password at once, and the messages went on coming from her account.

That is not a mistake in how she changed it. An app that you allow does not use the password. It was given access by your pressing Allow, so it carries on using that access until the permission is taken away. A new password does not take it away. Only removing the app does, and it works at once.

Removing it is done in your account’s own settings, in a list of the apps that are connected to the account. You may not remember every app that you pressed Allow for, so the list is worth looking through even when you remember one. An app that you cannot place has no reason to be in it, and you remove it.

A *one-time code* works once, so it cannot be used a second time. What matters after you have read one out is what was done with it, and the code came from your bank or the provider of the account, so they are the ones to tell. The same goes for an app that you allowed and do not trust: tell your bank or the provider. If the code was for your phone account, ask your cell phone carrier what protection it offers against someone moving your number to another SIM. Changing the password is still worth doing, as part of the same job.

### 29. Something was installed, or someone watched your device

*Unit Six · rev 2 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 4 · Card 29 of 54*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-device`]

A way into an account is one thing that can leave your hands. The fifth group is about the device itself: a program that you were told to install, or *screen-sharing*.

*Sam’s refund call*

> A caller tells Sam that he is owed a refund of $48 for an outage. The caller asks him to install a support app, and Sam does. He then logs in to his banking while the caller watches his phone. When the call ends, Sam understands that he let a stranger watch his phone while he typed his banking password.

Sam let a stranger watch his phone while he typed his banking password. The stranger could see everything that Sam could. That is what *screen-sharing* is, and it also covers a support app that lets someone control the device.

A device that has had something installed on it, or that someone has watched, cannot be trusted until you have dealt with it, and everything that you type on it may be seen. So the facts in this group are about cutting the device off, removing what was put on it, and doing the important things from somewhere else.

End the session that lets them watch. Switch off the internet connection on the device, so that it cannot be reached from far away. Uninstall what you were told to install. If what you opened was a file and not a program, also run a full scan with your security software.

Then change your passwords from another device, and check your real balance in your own banking app on that other device. What the first device showed Sam was controlled by someone else, and means nothing. Last, call your bank at the number on your card, through *a way you already had*, and tell them.

### 45. Someone offers to get your money back

*Unit Six · rev 2 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 4 of 4 · Card 45 of 54*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-second`]

The six groups so far are about what to do about the first loss. The seventh is about the second thing that often follows it: an offer to put the first right.

*Abe’s second message*

> Abe lost $14,000 to an investment site ten months ago and has told nobody but his bank. A message arrives: ‘We are a fund recovery firm. We have traced your $14,000. Pay a retainer of $2,500 today and we will release it to you.’ Abe thinks: ‘If I pay one more fee, I’ll get it back.’

Abe has already lost money once, and has told nobody but his bank. Now a stranger has found him and offers to get it back, for a fee. It reads like help. It is also how people who have lost money are so often contacted again: the people who took the money often come back.

The facts below are what to do with that message, and where real help comes from instead. Real help with a loss comes from your bank and from the FTC, which takes fraud reports at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. They are places that you go to, they do not charge you, and they do not contact you first in order to sell you help. A bank may call you about fraud on your account, and if one does, *the check* settles it: hang up, and call the number on your card, which is *a way you already had*.

Abe’s thought, ‘If I pay one more fee, I’ll get it back’, is one that costs people time and money. If you have already paid someone like that, tell your bank, because the same people often return.

### 53. What to carry away

*Unit Six · rev 2 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 4 of 4 · Card 53 of 54*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap-late`]

You have now met every fact in the unit, in its group. This card puts them together, and then adds what to carry.

**The call to your bank**

- **Whom do you call first after money has gone to a scammer?** Call your bank
- **Which number do you call?** Use the number on your card
- **The scam began with a phone call. What do you do before you call your bank?** Wait a few minutes, or use a different phone
- **What do you say to your bank on that call?** Say it was a scam payment, and ask the bank to try to recall it
- **How soon do you call?** Call right away

**Report it, and keep the proof**

- **Besides your bank, whom do you report a scam to?** Report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov
- **What do you keep, and not delete?** Keep the messages
- **You feel too embarrassed to say anything. What matters more than that feeling?** Speed

**A password typed into the wrong page**

- **Where do you change a password that you typed into a copied sign-in page?** Change it on the real site, and not through the link
- **Which device do you use to change it, if you can?** Change it from a different device, if you can
- **Where else do you change it?** Change it on every other account that used the same password
- **What do you switch on, so that a password alone is not enough?** Switch on two-step sign-in

**An app allowed, or a code read out**

- **Whom do you tell after you have read out a code, or pressed Allow for an app you do not trust?** Tell your bank or provider
- **What takes an app’s access to your account away at once?** Remove the app in your account’s connected-apps list
- **Which apps do you remove from that list?** Remove any app you cannot place
- **What do you also change, even though it does not take the app’s access away?** Change the password as well

**A device that someone has had control of**

- **Someone is watching your device from far away. What do you do to that?** End the session
- **What do you switch off on the device?** Switch off the internet connection
- **What do you do to a program that they had you install?** Uninstall it
- **Where do you change your passwords?** Change them from another device
- **How do you check your real balance?** Check it in your own banking app, on a different device
- **Whom do you tell, and at what number?** Tell your bank, at the number on your card

**Papers and numbers that went to the wrong people**

- **Whom do you tell about papers or numbers that you sent to a scammer?** Tell your bank
- **Whom do you ask to put a fraud warning on your file?** Ask the credit bureaus
- **What do you do afterwards, to see anything that you did not do?** Watch your accounts
- **Which of your passwords do you change?** Change any that match what you sent

**The offer that follows a loss**

- **Someone who contacted you offers to get your lost money back. What do you do?** Do not reply or pay
- **Who is often behind that offer?** The same people, returning
- **Where does real help with a loss come from?** Your bank and the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov
- **What do those real places not do?** Charge you, or contact you first to sell you help
- **Whom do you tell if you have already paid such an offer?** Tell your bank

**To carry away**

- Each thing that can leave your hands has its own first move: money, a password, a code, an app, a device, papers and numbers. Know which one you are dealing with, and make that move first.
- For money, the first hours are the chance. A call that was not needed costs ten minutes, and a call put off can cost the money.
- Changing a password and removing an app are two different jobs. A new password does not take away an app that you allowed.
- Use only *a way you already had* for every call and every sign-in after a scam. A number, a link or an app that came with the scam is never one, even if you are the one who dials it or taps it.
- Anyone who contacts you offering to get your money back is a warning in itself. Real help comes from your bank and from the FTC (ReportFraud.ftc.gov), and you go to them.
- Where a step depends on your bank, such as whether a payment can be recalled, ask your bank.

### 54. A plan, if you want one

*Unit Six · rev 2 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 4 of 4 · Card 54 of 54*

[reviewers only: card kind `plan`, id `plan-late`]

The unit has given you the facts. This card is for the moment that you will need them, and it is yours to fill in or to leave.

A plan is one line: if this happens, then I will do that. It is worth writing down because the moment you find out that something has gone wrong is the worst moment to work out what to do, and the best moment to do something that you decided in advance.

The lines below are examples to start from. You can use one, change it, or write your own two lines. Nothing is saved until you press the button.

- I find out that money I sent was a scam payment, call my bank right away at the number on my card, and say that it was a scam payment and ask them to try to recall it, before I do anything else
- I typed my password into a page that came in a message, change it on the real site, from a different device if I can, and change it anywhere else I used the same one
- I pressed Allow for an app that I do not trust, or read out a code, tell my bank or provider, and remove the app in my account’s connected-apps list
- someone had me install something, or watched my device, end the session, switch off the internet connection, and do my passwords and my balance from another device
- someone who contacted me offers to get my lost money back, not reply, not pay, and tell my bank if I already have

Or write your own: If …, then I will …
