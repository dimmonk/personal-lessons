## Part 1 of 2: Money, a password, an app you allowed

### 1. What to do first, when a scam has already worked

*Unit Six · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 37*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient-late`]

If you ever send money, a password or a code to a scammer, you will be upset and in a hurry. This unit gives you the first move for each way it can go wrong, so you can make it without looking anything up.

The first hours count. A payment can sometimes still be recalled, and an account can be locked before the damage spreads.

Picture it: you press send on a payment, then a reply shows that it was a scam. Your stomach drops. Three thoughts burn the time you need: ‘I’m so stupid’, ‘I’ll sort it out myself’, and ‘If I pay one more fee, I’ll get it back’.

Those thoughts are what being tricked feels like, and the scammer is counting on them. If you already know the first move, you can make it before the feeling takes over.

**What this unit is**

This unit is facts to hold, not a skill to apply. There are no questions to work through. Each fact is something you will be asked from memory, and it comes back on later days.

**The unit holds 21 facts, in six groups:**

- Money has already gone
- A password has already been typed in
- A code has been read out, or an app has been allowed
- Something was installed, or someone watched your device
- Papers or numbers have been sent
- Someone offers to get your money back

The unit has two parts, and you can stop after any of them.

1. Money, a password, an app you allowed
2. Your device, your papers, and offers to get your money back

Whether a payment can still be recalled depends on your bank. Nothing here promises that money comes back; each step only gives you a chance.

Each group starts with a story, then the idea in plain words, then the facts. After each fact you are asked it from memory, and the answer and how it fits are shown right away. Nothing here is graded. A miss only decides what comes back.

### 2. Money has already gone

*Unit Six · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 37*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-money`]

First, money you sent yourself. It moves fastest and is the hardest to undo.

*Priya’s payment*

> Priya pays her builder’s invoice of $2,000 into the new account named in an email that says the builder has changed banks. An hour later the builder calls about something else and mentions that nothing has changed on his side. Priya’s stomach drops. She thinks: ‘I’m so stupid. I’ll sort it out myself. Perhaps I should wait and see.’ Then she takes her bank card out of her wallet and calls the number printed on the back of it. She says the payment was a scam payment and asks the bank to try to recall it. The bank takes the details, and says it will try but cannot promise that the money will come back.

Priya did not wait, and she did not try to fix it alone. She called the one place that can try to get a payment back: her bank.

In the first hours, a payment can sometimes still be recalled. That means your bank asks the bank that received the money to send it back. Nobody can promise it works, but every hour you wait uses up some of the chance. A call you did not need costs ten minutes. A call you put off can cost the money.

After the call, report the scam to the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Keep every message: leave the chat, the emails and the call log as they are. Scammers count on embarrassment to keep people quiet, and the longer you stay quiet, the more time they have. Speed matters more than shame.

### 9. A password has already been typed in

*Unit Six · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 9 of 37*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-password`]

Next, a password you typed into a page that was not the real one.

*Hana’s email password*

> Hana gets a text: ‘Your package is held. Sign in to your email to confirm your address.’ The link opens a page that looks like her email provider’s sign-in page. She types her email address and her password, and the page says ‘Thank you’. That evening she sees that the address of the page was not her provider’s. She uses the same password for her online store and for her gym account.

Hana typed her email address and her password into a page that only looked like her provider’s. Whoever runs that page now has both.

She cannot take the password back, so she makes it useless by changing it. She does not use the link in the text: that would be the same trick again. She goes to the real site through *a way you already had*: an address she types in herself or saved long ago, or an app she installed herself.

Then she changes the same password on every other account that used it, because the scammer will try it there. Hana used hers for her online store and her gym account, so each needs a new one.

Last, she switches on two-step sign-in. It asks for a code or an approval as well as the password, so a stolen password on its own is no longer enough.

### 14. A code has been read out, or an app has been allowed

*Unit Six · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 14 of 37*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-app`]

A password is one way into an account. Here are two more: a *one-time code* you read out, and an app you pressed Allow for.

*Tessa’s shared document*

> Tessa gets a message from a colleague with a link to a shared document. The link opens a box: ‘DocView would like to read, send and delete all your mail. Allow or Cancel.’ She presses Allow. Later her colleague says she never sent the message. Tessa changes her email password at once and feels better. Two days later, her mail contacts tell her that they are still getting messages from her that she did not write.

Tessa pressed Allow on a box that asked whether DocView could read, send and delete all her mail. That box was a *permission screen*. She then changed her password at once, and the messages kept coming from her account.

Why did the new password not help? An app you allow never uses a password. It got in because you pressed Allow, and it keeps that access until the permission is taken away. Only removing the app does that, and it works at once. Open your account’s settings, find the list of connected apps, and remove any app you cannot place.

A *one-time code* is different: it works once, so what matters is what was done with it. The code came from your bank or the account’s provider, so tell them. Tell them about an app you allowed and do not trust, too. Still change the password: it is part of the same clean-up.

## Part 2 of 2: Your device, your papers, and offers to get your money back

### 20. Something was installed, or someone watched your device

*Unit Six · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 20 of 37*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-device`]

Next, your phone or computer itself: a caller had you install something, or watch your screen.

*Sam’s refund call*

> A caller tells Sam that he is owed a refund of $48 for an outage. The caller asks him to install a support app, and Sam does. He then logs in to his banking while the caller watches his phone. When the call ends, Sam understands that he let a stranger watch his phone while he typed his banking password.

Sam let a stranger watch his phone while he typed his banking password. The stranger saw everything Sam saw. That is *screen-sharing*, and it also covers a support app that lets someone control the device.

You cannot trust a device that someone watched or put a program on, and anything you type on it may be seen. So cut it off, remove what was put on it, and do the important jobs from another device.

First end the session that lets them watch, and switch off the device’s internet connection. Then uninstall what you were told to install. Next, from another device, change your passwords and check your real balance in your own banking app: someone else was controlling what Sam’s phone showed him. Next, papers, photos and numbers that identify you, sent to someone who should not have them.

*Rosa’s job papers*

> Rosa is offered a job by an employer she has never met. Before any contract, a form asks her to upload a photo of her passport, a photo of herself holding it, and her Social Security number. She tells her bank. Then she asks the credit bureaus to put a fraud alert or a freeze on her file. A credit bureau is a company that keeps the record lenders check before they give you credit; the three in the United States are Equifax, Experian and TransUnion. A fraud alert says someone else may be using your details. A credit freeze, which costs nothing, stops new credit being opened in your name until you lift it. She also reports it at IdentityTheft.gov, the FTC’s site for reporting that someone is using your details.

Afterwards she watches her accounts, because what she sent can be used weeks later and not only on the day she sent it. And she changes any password that matches what she sent.

### 31. Someone offers to get your money back

*Unit Six · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 31 of 37*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-second`]

Last, the second thing that often follows a loss: an offer to put the first one right.

*Abe’s second message*

> Abe lost $14,000 to an investment site ten months ago and has told nobody but his bank. A message arrives: ‘We are a fund recovery firm. We have traced your $14,000. Pay a retainer of $2,500 today and we will release it to you.’ Abe thinks: ‘If I pay one more fee, I’ll get it back.’

Abe lost money once and told only his bank. Now a stranger has found him and offers to get it back, for a fee. It reads like help, but it is how people who lost money are so often caught a second time: the people who took the money often come back. If you have already paid someone like that, tell your bank.

Real help comes from your bank and from the FTC, which takes fraud reports at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. You go to them, they do not charge you, and they do not contact you first to sell you help. A bank may call you about fraud on your account. If one does, *the check* settles it: hang up, and call the number on your card, which is *a way you already had*.

### 36. What to carry away

*Unit Six · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 36 of 37*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap-late`]

You have now met every fact in the unit. Here they are together, with what to carry away.

**The call to your bank, and what comes after**

- **Which number do you use to call your bank?** The number on the back of your card
- **What do you tell your bank on the call?** Say it was a scam payment, and ask for a recall
- **How soon do you call your bank?** Call right away, before anything else
- **Besides your bank, who takes your report of the scam?** Report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov
- **What do you keep and not delete?** Keep every message, and delete nothing

**A password typed into the wrong page**

- **Where do you change a password that you typed into a copied sign-in page?** Change it on the real site, not through the link
- **Where else do you change it?** Change it on every other account that used it
- **What do you switch on, so that a password alone is not enough?** Switch on two-step sign-in

**An app allowed, or a code read out**

- **Whom do you tell after you have read out a code, or pressed Allow for an app you do not trust?** Tell your bank or provider
- **What takes an app’s access to your account away at once?** Remove the app from your connected apps
- **What do you also change, even though it does not take the app’s access away?** Change the password as well

**A device that someone has had control of**

- **Someone is watching your device from far away. What do you do to that?** End the session
- **What do you switch off on the device?** Switch off the internet connection
- **What do you do to a program that they had you install?** Uninstall the program
- **Where do you change your passwords?** Change them from another device

**Papers and numbers that went to the wrong people**

- **Whom do you tell about papers or numbers that you sent to a scammer?** Tell your bank
- **Whom do you ask to put a fraud warning on your file?** Ask the credit bureaus
- **What do you do afterwards, to see anything that you did not do?** Watch your accounts

**The offer that follows a loss**

- **Someone who contacted you offers to get your lost money back. What do you do?** Do not reply or pay
- **Where does real help with a loss come from?** Your bank and the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov
- **What do those real places not do?** Charge you, or contact you first to sell help

**To carry away**

- Every way something can leave your hands has its own first move: money, a password, a code, an app, a device, papers and numbers. Work out which one it is, and make that move first.
- For money, the first hours are the chance. A call you did not need costs ten minutes; a call you put off can cost the money.
- Changing a password and removing an app are two different jobs. A new password does not remove an app you allowed.
- After a scam, call and sign in only through *a way you already had*. A number, a link or an app that came with the scam is never one, even if you are the one who dials or taps it.
- An offer to get your money back, from someone who contacted you, is a warning in itself. Real help comes from your bank and from the FTC (ReportFraud.ftc.gov), and you go to them.
