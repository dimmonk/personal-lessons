# Statistical Claims: Could anything else explain it?

Revision 1, live. Part: Could anything else explain it?. Where the subject ends up: When a headline and its article, a chart, an ad or a post on your phone is about to make you believe, share or act on a number, you can say whether the number shows what it is used to claim, point to the line that decides it (or say what is missing), and say how big it really is in plain counts, such as from 3 in 1,000 to 2 in 1,000 over ten years.

## The why

“Coffee drinkers live longer” and “coffee makes you live longer” are different claims. The second needs much more.
People who study what causes disease put one question first: is there any other way to explain these facts? Here you learn to put it to a headline, and to name the other explanation when there is one.

## Step 1: What else could explain it

When one group did better than another, or things changed after something new, put one question: could anything else explain it? Four answers come up again and again.
Other differences between the groups. People who chose to drink tea may also exercise more or smoke less, and that could be what made the difference.
The arrow the other way. Maybe the second thing caused the first: unhappy people may spend more time online, rather than the other way round.
A bounce back. Something picked because it just had its worst year usually has a better one next, with or without a change.
Nothing to compare with. A fall after a new rule may have happened anyway; you need places without the rule over the same years.
Only luck is left when the groups were split by chance, by a coin toss or a lottery: then they are alike in everything but the thing tested and what chance gives each. With a few hundred people and a clear gap, luck is unlikely; with a handful of people or a tiny gap, it could be luck, as with a survey’s margin. A dummy pill that looks the same (a placebo) also takes care of people feeling better just from taking something.

## Step 2: Then the verdict

A claim that one thing makes another happen holds up only when the groups were split by chance, there were enough of them for luck to be unlikely, and the numbers fit the size it gives.
If the text shows people chose their group, gives only a before and after with nothing to compare with, picked something right after its worst year, or names another explanation, a claim that one thing makes another happen does not show it. So does a split by chance with too few people to rule out luck, and numbers that say less than the size the claim gives.
A claim that two things only went together can hold up even when something else could explain it: it claims no more.
If the text does not say how the groups were formed, you cannot tell.

| A | B |
|---|---|
| A post, from "A trial":  > 400 adults were split by coin toss: half drank Restwell tea each night, half a tea that looks and tastes the same with nothing in it. 20 in 100 of the Restwell group woke in the night, against 30 in 100 of the others. | A post, from "A survey":  > 400 adults said whether they drink Restwell tea each night. 20 in 100 of the tea drinkers woke in the night, against 30 in 100 of the others. |

Compare: The same numbers. Which shows that the tea makes people sleep through? The first: a coin toss decided who drank it, so the groups were alike in everything else. In the second people chose, and tea drinkers may differ in other ways, such as going to bed earlier.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “A big study that followed thousands of people proves it.”
The right line: Ten thousand people who chose their own coffee are still people who chose. Size does not split groups by chance; only a coin toss or a lottery does.

## Step 3: A worked example

A news story, from "Valley Times · Schools":

> # Music lessons make children better at math, study finds
>
> Fourth graders who took music lessons scored 8 points higher on the state math test than those who did not, a study of 2,400 children found.
> Parents chose whether their children took lessons. Children in lessons also came more often from homes with more money and more books, the researchers noted.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**What is this claiming?**
(the options come in an order that depends on the seed of the question)
- Taking music lessons improves children’s math
- Children who take music lessons score higher in math
- Music lessons make adults better at math

The working, one step at a time:
1. Find what the headline claims. “Make” says the lessons cause the higher scores, not only that the two went together.
1. Find the number it rests on. The first sentence: 8 points higher on the state math test.
1. Look for what else could explain it. Parents chose the lessons, and the children in them came more often from homes with more money and books. That alone could give higher math scores.
1. Judge the claim. A claim that lessons make the difference holds only when the groups were split by chance. Here families chose, and the text names another explanation, so it does not show it.

Then the answer: Taking music lessons improves children’s math / Fourth graders who took music lessons scored 8 points higher on the state math test than those who did not, a study of 2,400 children found. / Children in lessons differed in other ways, such as homes with more books / Does not show it. The children were not split by chance: parents chose, and the children in lessons came from homes with more money and books. Those homes could explain the higher scores, so the text does not show that the lessons did it.
What you would need to see: You would need children split by chance into lessons and no lessons, and the gap still there.
