By the end you can say, without looking it up, what happened from the years of the great arrivals to September 11, 2001, when and how the right to vote was widened, and the plain facts about the flag and the names that stand for the country. ### 2. The great arrivals, in three years

*Unit Ten · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 65*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-wave`]

Three landmarks of the years when the country filled with factories and with new arrivals.

*Noor’s family card*

> Noor’s grandmother leaves her a small card, brown at the edges. It says that her great-grandfather came by steamship to New York and was examined at Ellis Island in 1905. Noor looks up Ellis Island and finds that it is in New York Harbor, where a large copper statue stands too. She wants to know which of the two came first, and what else happened in those years.

From 1877 to 1914 railways crossed the continent, factories and cities grew, and millions of immigrants, mostly from Europe, came to work in them. Three landmarks of those years each have a year.

The Chinese Exclusion Act, passed by Congress in 1882, was the first major law to bar a group of people from coming in because of where they came from. The Statue of Liberty, a gift from France, was dedicated in New York Harbor in 1886 and became a symbol of welcome. Ellis Island, in the same harbor, opened in 1892 as the federal station where arrivals were examined, and about twelve million people passed through it.

These three years are almost all that this course holds from 1877 to 1900.

### 15. Hard times, and what was done about them

*Unit Ten · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 15 of 65*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-hard`]

What the Depression was, and what the President did about it.

*Ines’s grandmother’s card*

> Ines is clearing out her grandmother’s desk. In a drawer she finds a small card with a number printed on it, and the words ‘Social Security’. Her grandmother used to say that the card was one of the things that came out of the Depression. Ines knows that the Depression was a hard time, but she does not know what it was, what the government did about it, or who led it.

In 1929 the Great Depression began: banks failed, and about a quarter of workers lost their jobs. That is one worker in four.

The President’s answer was a set of new programs called the New Deal, led by Franklin D. Roosevelt. Social Security, the program on Ines’s card, is the one to remember. New programs like it were run by new federal offices, and an office of that kind is called an *agency*. What these offices did is **Carrying out the law**: putting laws into practice, at a scale the founders never saw.

### 26. The civil rights movement: a court, a law and many people

*Unit Ten · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 26 of 65*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-civil`]

The movement, from 1954 to 1965, to end segregation.

*Amir’s daughter’s project*

> Amir’s daughter has a school project. Her teacher gives her a sentence to start from: ‘Once, Black and white children were kept in separate public schools. That ended, and it took a court, a law and the work of many people.’ Amir’s daughter asks him three things: which court, which law, and who the people were. Amir knows that the answer is in the history of the 1950s and 1960s, but he cannot name any of the three.

Amir’s daughter already has the three parts of the answer in her question: a court, a law and people. They are the three landmarks of the civil rights movement, which pushed to end segregation (keeping people of different races apart, as in separate public schools for Black and white children) and discrimination (treating people worse because of their race).
