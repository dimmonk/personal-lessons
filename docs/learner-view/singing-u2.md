## Part 1 of 3: The two voices, a top that is fine, and a top that is shouted

### 1. The top notes are where most people give up

*Unit Two · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 1 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

When the top of a line goes wrong, you can tell which of five things is happening, and you have something to try tonight.

The chorus climbs. On the high note you shout, or your voice flips, or your throat clamps, or the note is simply not there. Most people decide they cannot sing high and stop trying.

Four of the five things that happen at the top have a fix you can try tonight. The fifth needs no fix at all: it is a voice going high the easy way, and beginners often mistake it for a weak one. One look at what your voice did on the way up tells you which one you have.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“What bothers you about it?”** Its answers:

- **“How the top notes come out”**: give this answer when the trouble starts where the notes go up: at the top of the line the voice shouts, flips, clamps, cannot reach the note, or goes light in a way you are not sure of. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“How the air holds up”**: give this answer when the trouble is with the air: it is gone before the line ends, grabbed in the middle of a word, heard leaking out with the sound, or driven out hard, or you suspect the way you breathe.
- **“Whether you are on the note”**: give this answer when what you doubt is the note itself: whether the note you sing is the song’s note, because it sounds a shade off, you slide into it, you hunt for it, or you simply are not sure.
- **“How the words sound when they come out”**: give this answer when the notes and the air are fine, and what bothers you is the sound: pinched and nasal, dull and muffled, words that run together, or a voice that only sounds strange recorded.
- **“Only that it is not the record’s voice”**: give this answer when the notes are right, nothing is tight, sore or short of air, the words are clear, and the only complaint is that it does not sound like the singer on the record.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. The voice you talk in

*Unit Two · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 2 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-chest`]

Everything at the top of a line depends on two voices, and you already use one of them every day.

*The low hum*

> Dolores hums low notes along with the radio on the way to school. At a red light she rests a hand on her chest and feels it buzz. Then she says “Good morning” to her son in the back seat, and her hand feels the same buzz.

Rest a hand on your chest and talk: you feel a buzz. Dolores feels the same buzz when she hums low notes, because her low notes come out of the same voice she talks in.

**The word for this.** *Chest voice*: the heavier, fuller voice you talk in and sing your low notes in.


### 3. The lighter voice up high

*Unit Two · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 3 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-head`]

The other voice is the one Nora uses on the high notes of her lullaby.

*The bedtime song*

> At bedtime Nora sings a lullaby to her daughter. The tune climbs, and on the highest notes her voice turns light and thin, with no push behind it. She can feel the sound ringing up behind her nose and forehead instead of in her chest.

Slide from a low note up to a high, soft one, with a hand on your chest. The buzz fades, and the sound seems to move up into your head. The high notes are lighter and thinner.

**The word for this.** *Head voice*: the lighter, thinner voice your high notes come out in, which seems to ring up in your head rather than in your chest.

Going up a line means changing from *chest voice* to *head voice* somewhere in the middle. Everything that follows is about how that change goes.


### 4. A light, easy top

*Unit Two · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 4 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-lighttop`]

Start with the top that is not a problem, so you know what the others are measured against.

*The weak-feeling top*

> Mina sings along with a slow song in the car. When the line climbs to its highest note, ⟦her voice goes lighter and thinner than it was on the low notes, and the note comes out clean⟧. ⟦Her neck stays loose and nothing aches⟧. To her it feels weak, but her friend in the back seat says it sounds lovely.

Mina’s top note feels weak to her, but it is how a voice goes up when nothing is forced. Her voice changed from the heavy *chest voice* she talks in to the lighter *head voice*, so the sound got thinner and the note stayed clean.

From inside, light feels like less. Her friend in the back seat hears a clean note that carries.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the top of the line: the highest note Mina reaches.** The low part of a line tells you nothing here; the top does.
2. **Listen to the sound there: lighter and thinner than her low notes, and clean.** Going lighter is how a voice goes high without effort.
3. **Check your body: her neck stays loose and nothing aches.** Strain shows up in the neck, jaw and throat, so loose means no strain.
4. **Ignore the feeling that it is weak.** From inside it feels like less, but her friend hears a note that carries.

This is **A light, easy top**. Nothing is wrong here. It has a name so that you do not try to fix a top note that is already fine.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Keep it.** It is already doing the job.
2. **If you want it fuller, add a little volume while it stays easy.** More volume is fine as long as nothing tightens.
3. **Stop adding the moment your throat tightens.** Tightness means you have gone past easy.
4. **Record the line and listen back.** It usually sounds stronger outside than it feels inside.

You may also hear this called “head voice doing its job” or “the thin voice up top”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **A light, easy top**.

### 6. Pushing the top notes

*Unit Two · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 6 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-pushing`]

Now the same top note, reached the other way: by getting louder instead of lighter.

*The party shout*

> At a party, Dale sings along to a loud song with his friends. The song climbs, and ⟦he gets louder with every line⟧. By the top note ⟦his neck is tight and his face is red, and the note comes out as a shout⟧. It does not flip into a thin voice; it just gets bigger and harder.

Dale never changed voice. He kept the heavy *chest voice* and made it louder as the line rose, so the top came out as a shout and his neck tightened to help. It is a note like the one Mina sang lightly, reached by force instead.

Louder feels like the way to reach a high note. Lighter is the way a voice gets there easily, as Mina’s did.

**How to spot it**

1. **Listen to the volume as the line climbs: Dale gets louder with every line.** Pushing builds all the way up, not only on the top note.
2. **Feel your neck and jaw at the top: his neck is tight and his face is red.** Tightening is how force shows in the body.
3. **Listen to the top note itself: it comes out as a shout.** A shout means the heavy voice came all the way up with him.
4. **Check that it did not flip: it stays one hard sound.** A flip into a thin voice would be something else.

This is **Pushing the top notes**: the top notes are reached by force, and the voice never gets lighter.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Sing the line again at talking volume, and let the top go lighter, even if it feels thin.** Lighter lets the throat loosen.
2. **Slide slowly from a low note to a high one on “oo”, or on a lip trill (blow air through loose, fluttering lips while you sing), letting the voice get lighter as it climbs.** The slide lets the voice change without force.
3. **Sing the top note softly first, then add a little.** You add volume onto a loose throat, not a tight one.
4. **Never get louder to reach a note.** Louder is how the neck tightens.

You may also hear this called “yelling the high notes” or “belting it” or “pulling chest voice up”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Pushing the top notes**.

## Part 2 of 3: A flip, and a throat that clamps

### 9. Cracking

*Unit Two · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 9 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-cracking`]

Pushing never changes voice, and a light top changes smoothly. This one changes voice with a jolt.

*The flip*

> Sara sings along to a ballad at home. Her voice is full and strong as the line climbs, until one note, where it ⟦suddenly flips into a thin, airy sound with a little jolt⟧. ⟦The notes after the flip are there⟧, just light. When the line comes back down, her voice is full again.

Sara’s voice did change from the heavy *chest voice* to the lighter *head voice*, which is right, but it changed all at once instead of smoothly, and that is the flip you hear. It usually happens when the heavy voice is carried too far up before it lets go.

The notes after the flip are there, so her voice has them. It only has to learn to make the change gently.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the one note where the sound changes: Sara’s voice flips partway up.** The flip happens on a single note, not over the whole line.
2. **Listen to the sound after the flip: thin and airy.** The lighter voice has taken over.
3. **Listen for the jolt: a little gap, not a smooth slide.** A smooth change is the easy kind.
4. **Check the notes after the flip: Sara’s are there, just light.** If the note were missing, it would be something else.

This is **Cracking**: the change from *chest voice* to *head voice*, made all at once.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Do not push harder to get past it.** Pushing is what causes it.
2. **Slide slowly from low to high on “oo”, “ng” (the sound at the end of “sing”) or a lip trill, right through the spot where it flips.** The slide teaches the voice to change smoothly.
3. **Let the voice get lighter a few notes below the one that flips.** The change then comes early and gently, not late and all at once.
4. **Practice the slides every day for a week or two.** A smooth change comes from repetition.
5. **In the song, sing the line on “oo” first, then with the words.** Without words, the change is all you have to think about.

You may also hear this called “a voice crack” or “the break” or “flipping into falsetto”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Cracking**.

### 12. Squeezing

*Unit Two · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 12 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-squeezing`]

Pushing was tight because the voice got louder. This one is tight without getting any louder.

*The locked jaw*

> Joel sings at an open mic, a song that climbs toward its chorus. As the line goes up, ⟦his throat clamps and his jaw locks⟧. ⟦The sound goes thin and tight, but not louder⟧. When he finishes, ⟦his throat aches⟧.

Joel’s throat and jaw are clamping (a tongue can clamp too). A clamped throat makes the sound thin and tight instead of bigger, and it tires the throat, which is why his aches afterward.

It can feel like effort, but nothing is getting louder: the sound is being squeezed, not pushed.

**How to spot it**

1. **Feel your throat, jaw and tongue as the line climbs: Joel’s throat clamps and his jaw locks.** The clamp starts in the places that should stay loose.
2. **Listen to the sound: thin and tight, and not louder.** Louder as well would make it **Pushing the top notes**.
3. **Check your throat afterward: Joel’s aches.** A sore or tired throat after singing often goes with a clamp.

This is **Squeezing**: the throat clamps on the top notes.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Stop and do a yawn-sigh: a big yawn, then a sigh that slides from high to low.** It loosens the throat.
2. **Let your jaw hang loose and chew while you hum.** It shows you what a loose jaw feels like.
3. **Rest the tip of your tongue behind your lower front teeth.** It keeps the tongue from clamping.
4. **Sing the line on a lip trill before you add the words.** Loose, fluttering lips are an easy way to sing it with nothing locked.
5. **If it hurts, stop for the day.** Singing should never hurt.

You may also hear this called “singing from the throat” or “throat tension” or “a strangled sound”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Squeezing**.

## Part 3 of 3: A note that is not there, the question, one whole story, then the drill

### 15. Out of your range

*Unit Two · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 15 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-outofrange`]

The last one is not about how you sing the note. It is about whether your voice has the note.

*The high chorus*

> Pedro picks a karaoke song whose chorus sits very high. On the top note ⟦he tries going light, with his throat relaxed⟧. ⟦The note is still not there⟧; his voice just stops below it. Even the lines before it feel like a stretch.

Pedro tried the note lightly, with a loose throat, and it is still not there. That means this song is higher than his voice reaches today.

That is not a flaw in his voice. Pushing cannot give a voice a note it does not have today, and the top of a range grows a little with practice.

**How to spot it**

1. **Try the top note lightly, with your throat loose: Pedro goes light and relaxed on it.** A light try with a loose throat is the only way to find out whether the note is there.
2. **Listen to the result: the note is still not there.** If it comes out, something else was in the way.
3. **Look at the rest of the song: the lines before it feel like a stretch too.** When a whole song sits too high, every line strains, not only the top.

This is **Out of your range**. Nothing is wrong with Pedro’s voice: this song sits above what it has today.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Move the song lower, two or three steps, until the chorus is comfortable.** Karaoke apps and backing tracks have a setting for it, usually called pitch or transpose.
2. **Or pick another song.** Plenty of songs sit where your voice is comfortable.
3. **Do not push to reach the note.** A note your voice does not have today is not reached by pushing.
4. **Keep practicing, and try the top again in a few weeks.** The top of a range grows a little with practice.

You may also hear this called “the song is too high” or “not in your range”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Out of your range**.

### 17. Out of your range or Pushing the top notes: telling them apart

*Unit Two · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 17 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-outofrange-pushing`]

Both of these strain at a high note. What differs is whether the note was ever tried lightly.

**Story A**

> Anya picks a song with a very high chorus at karaoke. On the top note she gets louder and louder, her neck tight, and shouts it out. She does not try it light.

**Story B**

> Anya picks a song with a very high chorus at karaoke. On the top note she tries going light, with her throat relaxed. The note is not there; her voice stops below it.

**What to compare.** Both stories are about Anya and the same high chorus at karaoke. Compare one thing: whether she tried the top note lightly.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Even sung lightly, the note is not there”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A she gets louder and louder and shouts the note. She never tried it light, so nobody knows yet whether it is there. That is **“It gets louder and louder, and the top is a shout”**, so it is **Pushing the top notes**.

In Story B she tries the note lightly, with her throat relaxed, and it is not there. That is **“Even sung lightly, the note is not there”**, so it is **Out of your range**.

So the first thing to do with a note you have been shouting is to try it light. If it comes, you were pushing. If it does not, move the song lower.

**How to tell them apart**

Did you try the note lightly, with a loose throat, or only by getting louder?

### 19. The question to ask at the top of every line

*Unit Two · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 19 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-high`]

Here is the question and its five answers in one place.

**The question:** **“What happens at the top of the line?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other four.

- **“It goes light and clear, with nothing tight”**
  - Give this answer when the top notes come out lighter and thinner than the low ones, clean and easy, with no jolt, and nothing tight or sore.
  - It leads to **A light, easy top**.
- **“It gets louder and louder, and the top is a shout”**
  - Give this answer when as the line climbs the voice gets louder, the neck or the jaw tightens, and the top note comes out as a shout or a strain, without flipping into a lighter voice.
  - It leads to **Pushing the top notes**.
- **“It flips to a thin, airy voice on one note”**
  - Give this answer when on the way up the sound suddenly flips into a thin, airy voice, with a jolt or a gap, and the note after the flip is there.
  - It leads to **Cracking**.
- **“It goes tight and strangled, and the throat aches”**
  - Give this answer when the throat, jaw or tongue clamp, the sound goes thin, tight or strangled without getting louder, and the throat is sore or tired afterward.
  - It leads to **Squeezing**.
- **“Even sung lightly, the note is not there”**
  - Give this answer when you try the top note lightly, with the throat loose, and it still is not there, or the whole song sits where every line strains.
  - It leads to **Out of your range**.

**Why it matters**

Each of the five is fixed a different way: one by getting lighter instead of louder, one by practicing the join between the heavier voice and the lighter one, one by loosening the throat, one by moving the song lower, and one by leaving it alone. How loud the singer on the record is, how much you want the note, and how it felt last week do not change the answer.

You cannot tell from the song or from memory. Sing the top of the line once more and listen to your voice on the way up: that is the whole test.

**How to answer it**

1. **Sing the top of the line again, and listen to what your voice does on the way up.** The answer comes from what it did, not from what you hoped it would do.
2. **Listen for a voice that stays light and easy, with nothing tight and nothing sore.** Then nothing is wrong: it is **A light, easy top**.
3. **Listen for a voice that gets louder and louder until the top is a shout.** That is **Pushing the top notes**, even if your throat is tight too.
4. **Listen for a sudden flip into a thin, airy sound, and for whether the next note is there.** If it is there, it is **Cracking**. If it is not, it is **Out of your range**.
5. **Notice whether something locked up in your throat, jaw or tongue.** If the sound also went thin and did not get louder, that is **Squeezing**.
6. **Sing the top note lightly, with your throat loose.** If it is still not there, it is **Out of your range**, whatever else happened.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Sometimes a story shows two answers at once. Each pair below has one question that separates it. If a story shows two, use the answer named under the pair.

- A light, easy top or Pushing the top notes: As the line climbed, did the voice get lighter or louder, and did the neck stay loose or tighten?
- Cracking or A light, easy top: Was the change from the heavier voice to the lighter one smooth, or did it flip on one note with a jolt?
- Out of your range or Pushing the top notes: Did you try the note lightly, with a loose throat, or only by getting louder?
- Cracking or Out of your range: After the flip, was the next note there, or was it still missing when you sang it lightly?
- Pushing the top notes or Squeezing: Did the voice keep getting louder, or did it go thin and tight without getting louder?
- A light, easy top or Squeezing: Was the throat loose, or did it clamp, and did it ache afterward?
- A light, easy top or Out of your range: Did the note come out clean, only lighter than the low ones, or was it missing?
- Cracking or Pushing the top notes: Did the sound stay heavy and get louder, or did it flip into a thin, airy sound?
- Cracking or Squeezing: Was the thin sound airy and sudden, or tight with the throat clamped?
- Out of your range or Squeezing: Did the throat clamp as you reached for the note, or was it loose and the note still missing?

### 22. What to carry away

*Unit Two · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 22 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap`]

You can now tell all five apart on your own.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What happens at the top of the line?
- It goes light and clear, with nothing tight → A light, easy top
- It gets louder and louder, and the top is a shout → Pushing the top notes
- It flips to a thin, airy voice on one note → Cracking
- It goes tight and strangled, and the throat aches → Squeezing
- Even sung lightly, the note is not there → Out of your range

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **A light, easy top**: top notes that come out lighter and thinner than the low ones, clean and easy, with no jolt and nothing tight or sore.
- **Pushing the top notes**: a line that climbs, a voice that gets louder as it climbs, a tightening neck or jaw, and a top note that comes out as a shout or a strain, with no flip into a lighter voice.
- **Cracking**: a line that climbs, a sudden flip into a thin, airy voice with a jolt or a gap, and a note after the flip that is there.
- **Squeezing**: a line that climbs, a throat, jaw or tongue that clamps, a sound that goes thin, tight or strangled without getting louder, and a throat that is sore or tired afterward.
- **Out of your range**: a top note tried lightly, with a loose throat, and still not there, or a whole song that sits where every line strains.

**To carry away**

- When the top of a line goes wrong, sing it again and listen to what your voice does on the way up. Do not guess from how the note felt.
- If the top is light and easy, keep it. If you want it fuller, add a little volume, and stop the moment your throat tightens.
- Never get louder to reach a note. For **Pushing the top notes**, go lighter; for **Squeezing**, loosen the throat first; for **Cracking**, practice slow slides through the spot where it flips.
- When a story shows two answers, a louder voice with a tight throat is **Pushing the top notes**, and a flip followed by a missing note is **Out of your range**.
- For **Out of your range**, move the song lower or pick another one. A note you do not have today is not reached by pushing.
