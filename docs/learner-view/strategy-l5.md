# Strategy: Find the core

Revision 1, live. Part: Find the core. Where the subject ends up: After this you can look at a business decision (expand, add a product, chase a big customer, answer a rival, invest) and tell whether it is a strategy, say where the business makes its money and why, and pick the move that keeps it different in a way rivals cannot copy, when you face that decision in your own business.

## The why

Before growing a business, find out which of its lines really pay. In many businesses a few products, customers or ways of selling earn most of the profit, and a busy line can earn little or lose money.
Strategists read the owner’s own books for this first. Here you learn to find the core in a table of lines, then do it on your own business.

## Step 1: What each line earns

A line is one product or service, one customer (or group of customers), or one way you sell: a shop, a website, a market stall. Compare lines of one kind at a time, and count each sale once.
For each line, take its direct cost from what it brought in. A direct cost is one that would stop if the line stopped: its materials, the staff hours you would no longer pay, its delivery. Rent, and the other costs that stay whatever you drop, stay out.
What is left is what the line earns. Judge every line by that, never by its sales.
The core is the line that is both distinctive, what customers come to you for rather than to a rival, and earns the most. Usually that is the line that earns the most of all. But a line every rival sells the same way can earn well this year and still be competed away, as copied gains are: it is not the core. Then the core is the distinctive line that earns the most.
Its share is what it earns divided by what all the lines of that kind earn together, the losses of losing lines taken off. With a losing line, the share can pass 100%: it does when the losing lines lose more than the other lines earn, so the losses take back part of what the core earned.
Keep and grow the core before growing anything else; if it cannot grow, protect it.
A line that loses money: first, could a change of price or of what it costs to serve make it pay (charge what rivals charge, a smallest order, a delivery fee). If it could, keep it and fix it.
If no change would make it pay, what ties it to the core? Do the core’s customers buy it along with the core, or find you through it (a free class that brings the families who later book the paid sessions)? Then dropping it costs the core too: keep it and cut its loss as far as you can. If neither, let it go.
Why it matters: growth spent on a line that earns little, or loses, takes time and money from the line that pays for everything. Rent and shared costs are paid from what the lines earn together, so a business can earn on most lines and still lose money overall; this step finds which lines pay, not whether the whole business does.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “Bread is our biggest seller, so bread is our most important line.”
The right line: Sales are not earnings. Dana’s bread brings in the most and earns the least of her lines that make money, once its flour and the baker’s hours are paid. Judge each line by what it earns after its direct cost.

## Step 2: A worked example

A plain table, "Dana’s bakery: last year, by product":

> Line  |  Brought in  |  Direct cost  (the column heads)
> Bread  |  $60,000  |  $52,000
> Wedding cakes  |  $48,000  |  $18,000
> Birthday cakes  |  $30,000  |  $18,000
> Catering  |  $20,000  |  $26,000
> (Other caterers in town charge about a third more than we do for the same party.)
> (Most couples who order a wedding cake also book our catering for the party.)
> (Couples drive in from three towns for our wedding cakes; no other bakery nearby makes them.)

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**Which line is the core: what customers come here for rather than to a rival, and earns the most after its direct cost?**
(the options come in an order that depends on the seed of the question)
- Bread
- Wedding cakes
- Birthday cakes
- Catering

The working, one step at a time:
1. Take each line’s direct cost from what it brought in. Then find the line customers come here for, and check it earns the most.: Bread: $8,000; Wedding cakes: $30,000; Birthday cakes: $12,000; Catering: −$6,000. Wedding cakes earns the most, though Bread brings in more, and it is what customers come here for.
1. Divide what the core earns by what all the lines earn together, losses taken off.: $30,000 ÷ $44,000 = 68.2%. The loss on Catering is taken off the total.
1. Decide on the line that loses money: could a price or cost change make it pay? If not, does it bring the core’s customers or come with the core?: Other caterers in town charge about a third more than we do for the same party. Most couples who order a wedding cake also book our catering for the party. So: a price or cost change could make it pay: keep it and fix it.

Then the answer: Wedding cakes / 68.18% / Keep it, but fix its price or cost. Wedding cakes is what customers come here for and earns $30,000, 68.2% of what the lines earn together, more than Bread with its bigger sales: that is the core. Catering loses $6,000: a price or cost change could make it pay: keep it and fix it.
