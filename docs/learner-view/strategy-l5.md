# Strategy: Find the core

Revision 1, draft (the screen carries the draft line). Part: Find the core. Where the subject ends up: After this you can look at a business decision (expand, add a product, chase a big customer, answer a rival, invest) and tell whether it is a strategy, say where the business makes its money and why, and pick the move that keeps it different in a way rivals cannot copy, when you face that decision in your own business.

## The why

Before growing a business, find out which of its lines really pay. In many businesses a few products, customers or ways of selling earn most of the profit, and a busy line can earn little or lose money.
Strategists read the owner’s own books for this first. Here you learn to find the core in a table of lines, then do it on your own business.

## Step 1: What each line earns

A line is one product, one customer (or group of customers), or one way you sell: a shop, a website, a market stall. Compare lines of one kind at a time.
For each line, take its direct cost from what it brought in. A direct cost is one the line causes: its materials, the staff time it takes, its delivery. Rent and the other costs every line shares stay out.
What is left is what the line earns. Judge every line by that, never by its sales.
The line that earns the most is the core. Keep it and grow it, before growing anything else. Its share is what it earns divided by what the lines that make money earn together; a line that loses money stays out of that sum.
A line that loses money: if the core’s customers buy it along with the core, keep it, but fix its price or its cost. If nothing ties it to the core, let it go.
Why it matters: growth spent on a line that earns little, or loses, takes time and money from the line that pays for everything.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “Bread is our biggest seller, so bread is our most important line.”
The right line: Sales are not earnings. Dana’s bread brings in the most and earns the least of her lines that make money, once its flour and the baker’s hours are paid. Judge each line by what it earns after its direct cost.

## Step 2: A worked example

A plain table, "Dana’s bakery: last year, line by line":

> Line  |  Brought in  |  Direct cost  (the column heads)
> Bread  |  $60,000  |  $52,000
> Wedding cakes  |  $48,000  |  $18,000
> Birthday cakes  |  $30,000  |  $18,000
> Catering  |  $20,000  |  $26,000
> (Most couples who order a wedding cake also book our catering for the party.)

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**Which line is the core: the one that earns the most after its direct cost?**
(the options come in an order that depends on the seed of the question)
- Bread
- Wedding cakes
- Birthday cakes
- Catering

The working, one step at a time:
1. Take each line’s direct cost from what it brought in, and find the line that earns the most.: Bread: $8,000; Wedding cakes: $30,000; Birthday cakes: $12,000; Catering: −$6,000. Wedding cakes earns the most, though Bread brings in more.
1. Divide what it earns by what the lines that make money earn together.: $30,000 ÷ $50,000 = 60%. Catering loses money, so it is left out of the sum.
1. Decide on the line that loses money: do the core’s customers buy it along with the core?: Most couples who order a wedding cake also book our catering for the party. So keep it, but fix its price or cost.

Then the answer: Wedding cakes / 60% / Keep it, but fix its price or cost. Wedding cakes earns $30,000 after its direct cost, 60% of what the lines that make money earn, more than Bread with its bigger sales: that is the core. Catering loses $6,000, and the core’s customers buy it too, so fix its price or cost.
