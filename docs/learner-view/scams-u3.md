## Part 1 of 2: The real thing, and the three scams that copy it

### 1. A way into your account: yours, or a copy of it

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

Before you type a password, read out a code or press Allow, check two things: what exactly you are being asked for, and whether you started it. Scammers copy things you do every day, and most people who get caught skip this check.

You sign in to your email. A code arrives when you pay online. An app asks whether it may see your photos. You do these things many times a week, and each is normal.

A scam does not break in. It gets you to hand over the way in yourself, and it makes the request look like the ones you already do. The page can be a copy, the code can be real, and the *permission screen* can come from your own email provider. So the way it looks will not tell you. Two questions will: what is it asking you to type or press, and did you start it?

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“What is it asking you to do right now?”** Its answers:

- **“Install something, open a file, or share your screen”**: give this answer when it asks you to install something, open or run a file, or let someone see or control your screen, or it warns of a device problem and gives you someone to call.
- **“Sign in, give a code, or allow an app”**: give this answer when it asks you to sign in, to give a one-time code by typing it, reading it out or passing it on, or to press Allow so that an app can use one of your accounts. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“Pay or send money”**: give this answer when it asks you to pay or send money, by any means, now or by a date.
- **“Tell them about yourself”**: give this answer when it asks for facts about you: a document, an ID or card number, your date of birth or address, or your work, home and family.
- **“Nothing: it only tells you something”**: give this answer when it tells you something has happened or will happen and asks you to do nothing, and anything it suggests uses only what you already had, like the number on your card.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. Real sign-in

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-realsignin`]

Scammers copy something you do every day, so the real thing comes first.

*The energy app*

> Marta wants to check how much electricity she used last month. ⟦She opens the energy company's app, which has been on her phone since last winter⟧. It asks for her email address and password, and she types them in.

Marta opened an app that was already on her phone, because she wanted to see her electricity use. It asked for her email address and password, and nothing else. She started it, using *a way you already had*, and she typed her password into the same app she opened.

That is the whole test. How the page looks and whose logo is on it tell you nothing, because a copy can look exactly the same, whether it is a sign-in page, a code or a *permission screen* with an Allow button. The one thing a copy cannot have is that you started it.

A real one can still come with a message, as long as the message answers something you did: a link to choose a new password after you tap "Forgot password", or a code after you start to sign in.

**How to spot it**

1. **Ask what you were doing before this appeared: Marta wanted to see her electricity use.** The real thing answers something you set out to do.
2. **Find where you started from: the energy app that was already on her phone.** A link in a message is never a place you started from.
3. **Check what it asks for: her email address and password, nothing more.** A real request asks only for what the job needs.

This is **Real sign-in**, and nothing is wrong. It comes in the same three forms as the scams: a password typed into a site or app you opened, a *one-time code* you asked for, and an Allow for an app you went looking for.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Ask yourself: did I start this, and from where?** The answer is in your own memory, so you need nobody's help.
2. **If you can say where you started from, go ahead.** You started it, so it is the real thing.
3. **If you cannot, close the page, then open the app or type the address yourself.** If it was real, it will be waiting for you there.

### 4. Phishing

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-phishing`]

The most common scam of all copies a password page.

*The locked streaming account*

> Sunita gets an email that says it is from her streaming service: 'Your account has been locked after a sign-in from another country. Tap here to unlock it.' The button opens a page with the service's logo. ⟦It asks her to type her email address and password⟧.

Sunita did not ask for this email. It came to her, and the page at the end of the link was built by a scammer, with the service's logo and layout copied. When she types her password, the page does not sign her in: it sends the password to the scammer, who uses it on the real site at once and can lock her out.

The scammer sends the same email to thousands of people, because a few will have an account there. Nothing was broken into: Sunita typed the password herself, into a page that looked right. A page can be made to look like any other, padlock and all, so how it looks is not what to check. How you got there is. Marta opened her own app, and Sunita followed an email she did not ask for.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find how you got there: an email Sunita did not ask for.** It always starts with something that came to you: a message, a call or a pop-up.
2. **Find the link or button: "Tap here to unlock it."** It leads to a page the scammer built.
3. **Find what the page asks you to type: her email address and password.** A page that wants a password, reached from a message, is the whole scam.

This is **Phishing**. The word is another spelling of "fishing": the message is the bait, and the password is what is caught.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Close the page and type nothing.** Whatever you type goes straight to the scammer.
2. **Open the app yourself, or type the address yourself.** A real problem with your account will be there too.
3. **If you already typed the password, change it now on the real site, and anywhere else you used it.** The scammer tries it on the real site at once.

You may also hear this called “fake login page” or “credential phishing”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Phishing**.

### 6. Phishing or Real sign-in: telling them apart

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 6 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-phishing-realsignin`]

Both can ask for a password on a page that looks the same.

**Story A**

> Dev opens the mail app he has used for years. A banner at the top says his mailbox is almost full. He taps 'Manage storage', and the app asks him to sign in again with his email address and password. He types them in.

**Story B**

> Dev gets a text from a number he does not know: 'Your mailbox is almost full. Sign in now to upgrade, or your emails will be deleted.' The link opens a page with his email provider's logo. It asks for his email address and password.

**What to compare.** Both stories are about Dev, his email provider and a nearly full mailbox, and in both a page asks for his email address and password. Compare one thing: how Dev got to the sign-in.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Yes: you started it through a way you already had, and it asks for nothing more”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story A.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A, Dev opens the mail app himself, and the sign-in comes from what he does next. That is **Real sign-in**.

In Story B, a text from a number he does not know sends him to a page with his provider's logo. It started with the message. That is **Phishing**.

The same words, a full mailbox and a page that wants the password, can be either. What differs is where each one began.

**How to tell them apart**

Did I start this myself, from an app, an address or a number I already had? Or did a message, a call or a pop-up bring me to the page?


### 7. One-time code scam

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-codescam`]

The first copy needed a fake page. This one needs no page at all, because the code it asks for is real.

*The phone-order call*

> Ewan's phone rings. A woman says she is from his cell phone company and that a new phone has been ordered on his account. 'To cancel the order I am sending you a code,' she says. ⟦A text arrives with six digits in it⟧. '⟦Read them out to me⟧,' she says.

The caller is already trying to sign in to Ewan's account. His phone company's system does what it is built to do: it texts a real *one-time code* to Ewan. The caller phones him with a story that fits and asks him to read the code out. She types it in, and she is in as Ewan.

This works on people who would never type a password into a strange page, because the code is real. It comes from the real company, in the same list of texts as the real ones. What is wrong is who asks for it. A code is for you to type into a page or an app that you opened, and nobody else ever needs to hear it.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the code that just arrived: a text with six digits.** It is a real code, sent by a real company.
2. **Find who contacted you first: the woman who phoned Ewan.** A real code answers something you did, and Ewan did nothing.
3. **Find where the code is meant to go: she asks him to read it out to her.** A real code goes only into a page or an app you opened.

This is **One-time code scam**: someone who reached you first wants a code read out or passed on.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Never read out or send on a code, whoever asks and whatever the reason.** With the code, they are in your account.
2. **End the call or the chat.** Nothing the caller says after this is worth hearing.
3. **Call the company at a number you already had, such as the one on your card. This is *the check*.** It is the only sure way to learn whether the request was real.
4. **If you already read a code out, call the company right away, ask them to lock the account, and change the password.** Until you do, the caller is inside.

You may also hear this called “OTP scam” or “verification code scam”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **One-time code scam**.

### 9. One-time code scam or Real sign-in: telling them apart

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 9 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-codescam-realsignin`]

Both can involve the same code, from the same bank, in the same list of texts.

**Story A**

> Hana wants to pay a bill, so she opens her banking app. To approve the payment of $60, the app says it has sent a code to her phone. The code arrives and she types it into the app.

**Story B**

> Hana's phone rings. A man says he is from her bank's fraud team and that someone is trying to pay $60 from her account. 'I have just sent a code to your phone,' he says. 'Read it out to me and I will stop the payment.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about Hana, her bank, a payment of $60 and a code that arrives on her phone. Compare one thing: who asks for the code, and where it goes.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Yes: you started it through a way you already had, and it asks for nothing more”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story A.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A, Hana opens her own banking app to pay a bill. The code arrives because of what she did, and she types it into the same app. Nobody else sees it. That is **Real sign-in**.

In Story B, a man calls her and asks her to read the code out. She started nothing, and the code is just as real as in Story A. That is **One-time code scam**.

The code does not tell you which story you are in. What differs is where it goes: into your own app, or into a caller's ear.

**How to tell them apart**

Who is going to see this code: only the page or the app I opened myself, or a person who contacted me?


### 10. A code that comes after a password

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 10 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `exception`, id `exc-both`]

A scam does not have to keep to one request. Here is one that asks for both.

*The held order*

> Gil gets an email that says it is from an online store: 'Your order is on hold. Sign in to release it.' The link opens a page that asks for his password. When he types it, a second window says: 'We have sent a six-digit code to your phone. Type it here to finish signing in.'

There is a code in this story, and a code that comes to your phone is what **One-time code scam** is about. Yet this story is **Phishing**.

**You are asked:** This looks like **One-time code scam**. Before you read why it is **Phishing**, tap the words in the story that settle it.

The pieces you can tap:
1. “Gil gets an email that says it is from an online store”
2. “Your order is on hold. Sign in to release it”
3. “The link opens a page that asks for his password”
4. “When he types it, a second window says: 'We have sent a six-digit code to your phone. Type it here to finish signing in.'”

**Shown as soon as you tap.** The words are “The link opens a page that asks for his password”.
- If you tapped “Gil gets an email that says it is from an online store”: This only says who the email claims to be. What the page asks for comes later.
- If you tapped “Your order is on hold. Sign in to release it”: This is the bait. What the page asks for is in the next sentence.
- If you tapped “When he types it, a second window says: 'We have sent a six-digit code to your phone. Type it here to finish signing in.'”: This code makes the story look like a code scam. But it comes second, after the page has already asked for his password.

**Why this is Phishing and not One-time code scam**

Look at the order. First a page asks Gil for his password. Only after he types it does a second window ask for the code. At that moment the scammer is signing in to the real store with Gil's password, the real store texts Gil a code, and the fake page asks for it so the scammer can use it.

When a story asks for two things, the answer is the first one, so here it is **“Your password”**. The code makes it a fuller **Phishing**, not a **One-time code scam**: nobody is asking Gil to read it out to them.

**How to tell them apart**

Am I being asked to type a password into a page, or to pass on a code that has just come to my phone?

When a story shows both **“A one-time code sent to your phone or email”** and a sign-in page that asks for your password first, the answer is **“Your password”**.

When a page asks for a password and then for a code, go by the password. The fake page is the thing to leave.


### 11. App permission scam

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 11 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-appscam`]

The first two copies asked you to type something. This one asks you to press a button, and needs no password and no code.

*The shared document*

> Rafa gets an email: 'A colleague has shared a document with you. Open it here.' The link opens his email provider's own permission screen: '⟦Docs Sync Pro would like to read, send and delete all your email, and see all your contacts⟧.' It has two buttons, Allow and Cancel.

Rafa did not ask for this email. Its link leads to a *permission screen* from his own email provider, with the real name and logo and a list of real things, so nothing here is copied. What is false is the reason for pressing Allow: a shared document. A scammer registered the app, Docs Sync Pro, for free and gave it an ordinary name.

Once Rafa presses Allow, the app can read his mail, send mail as him and delete it, without ever seeing his password. A document only needs to be opened. It does not need every email Rafa has ever received, so the gap between the reason given and what the *permission screen* asks for is what to read.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find what you are asked to press: Allow, on his email provider's screen.** This scam needs a button, not a password.
2. **Read what the app may do: read, send and delete all his email, and see all his contacts.** That list is the real price of pressing Allow.
3. **Compare it with the reason given: a shared document, which only needs to be opened.** A big gap between the two is the scam.

This is **App permission scam**: you give an app permission on a *permission screen*, and it uses it for far more than you were told.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Press Cancel, or close the page.** You lose nothing: if it was real, you can start again from your own app.
2. **If you already pressed Allow, open your account's security settings and find the list of connected apps.** The app stays connected until you remove it.
3. **Remove every app you do not know.** Changing the password does not remove it, because the app never used the password.

You may also hear this called “consent phishing” or “OAuth phishing”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **App permission scam**.

## Part 2 of 2: Two questions, one whole story, then the drill

### 14. The first question: “What does it want you to type in or press?”

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 14 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-A1`]

Each of the three scams has shown you this question, with the one answer that fits it. Here it is in one place.

**The question:** **“What does it want you to type in or press?”**

**Its answers**

- **“Your password”**
  - Give this answer when you are asked to type your password, or your username and password, into a sign-in page.
  - Keeps **Phishing** and **Real sign-in**. Rules out **One-time code scam** and **App permission scam**.
- **“A one-time code sent to your phone or email”**
  - Give this answer when a one-time code has just come to your phone or email, and you are asked to type it in, read it out or send it on.
  - Keeps **One-time code scam** and **Real sign-in**. Rules out **Phishing** and **App permission scam**.
- **“Allow, on an app’s permission screen”**
  - Give this answer when a permission screen asks you to press Allow so that an app can read or use your mail, files, contacts or another part of your account.
  - Keeps **App permission scam** and **Real sign-in**. Rules out **Phishing** and **One-time code scam**.

**Why it matters**

### 16. The second question: “Did you start this, and is that all it asks?”

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 16 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-A2`]

The first question told the three scams apart, but it could not say whether a request is real, because the real thing asks for the same three things. This one can.

**The question:** **“Did you start this, and is that all it asks?”**

**Its answers**

- **“Yes: you started it through a way you already had, and it asks for nothing more”**
  - Give this answer when you started the sign-in yourself through a way you already had, and your password or code goes only into that site, or the app asks only for what your task needs.
  - Keeps **Real sign-in**. Rules out **Phishing**, **One-time code scam** and **App permission scam**.
- **“No: it came to you, or it asks for more than you set out to do”**
  - Give this answer when the sign-in page, code request or app came to you in a message, call, link or pop-up, or the app asks for more of your account than your task needs.
  - Keeps **Phishing**, **One-time code scam** and **App permission scam**. Rules out **Real sign-in**.

**Why it matters**

A real sign-in page, a real code and a real permission screen can each be used against you. What tells them apart is whether you started it yourself and whether it asks only for what you set out to do, and you can answer that without knowing who is behind it.

With a real one, you started it, and it asks no more than the job needs. A copy came to you, or wants far more than you set out to do. Say you found a free tool yourself, but it wants to delete all your mail: you started it, but it asks for too much, so the answer is still no.

You can answer this on the spot, from what is in front of you and your own memory. You do not need to know who is behind it.

**How to answer it**

1. **Ask what you were doing before this appeared.** If you set out to sign in, pay or connect an app, you started it.
2. **Say where you started from: your own app, or an address you typed.** A link in a message is never where you started from, and neither is a number a caller gave you.
3. **Read what it asks for, and check that the job needs it.** A photo app needs your photos, not your whole mailbox.
4. **If you are not sure you started it, answer no: close it and start again from your own app.** If it was real, it will be waiting there.

### 19. What to carry away

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 19 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap`]

This card puts the unit in one place.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What does it want you to type in or press?
- Your password → Phishing · Real sign-in
- A one-time code sent to your phone or email → One-time code scam · Real sign-in
- Allow, on an app’s permission screen → App permission scam · Real sign-in

Did you start this, and is that all it asks?
- Yes: you started it through a way you already had, and it asks for nothing more → Real sign-in
- No: it came to you, or it asks for more than you set out to do → Phishing · One-time code scam · App permission scam

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Real sign-in**: a sign-in, a code or an Allow you started yourself, on a site or app you reached through a way you already had, and nothing asked beyond what you set out to do.
- **Phishing**: a message, a call or a pop-up you did not ask for, a link in it, and a sign-in page at the end of the link that asks for your password.
- **One-time code scam**: a one-time code that has just come to your phone or email, and someone who contacted you asking you to read it out or pass it on.
- **App permission scam**: a permission screen asking you to press Allow for an app, and an app that came to you in a message or wants far more of your account than your task needs.

**To carry away**

- Before you sign in, give a code or press Allow, ask two questions: what am I being asked to type or press, and did I start it? Find the words that show each.
- If it came to you, a page that wants a password is **Phishing**, a person who wants you to pass on a code is **One-time code scam**, and a *permission screen* where an app wants far more than its job needs is **App permission scam**. If you started it and it asks no more than the job needs, it is **Real sign-in**.
- The page, the code and the *permission screen* can all be real, even in a scam. How a request looks, whose name is on it, and where it sits among your messages tell you nothing. Whether you started it does.
- When you cannot say that you started it, stop, and start again from an app, an address or a number that you already had. That is *the check*, and a real company never minds it.
- If you have already given something away: change the password at once; for a code, call the company at once; for an Allow, remove the app, because changing the password does not take it away.
