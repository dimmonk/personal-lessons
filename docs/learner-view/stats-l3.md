# Statistical Claims: A risk, before and after

Revision 1, live. Part: A risk, before and after. Where the subject ends up: When a headline and its article, a chart, an ad or a post on your phone is about to make you believe, share or act on a number, you can say whether the number shows what it is used to claim, point to the line that decides it (or say what is missing), and say how big it really is in plain counts, such as from 3 in 1,000 to 2 in 1,000 over ten years.

## The why

“Cuts your risk by half” is a change, and a change is only as big as the risk it started from. Half of a big risk is a lot; half of a tiny risk is a few people in a thousand.
People who read health numbers for a living always want the risk before and after, in so many in 1,000, over a stated time. You learn to work it out from a headline and a line of the story.

## Step 1: Before and after

When a headline gives a change in risk, work out the risk before and after, in so many in 1,000 over the time stated.
First, find how common it was without the pill (or before the change): for example, 4 in 1,000 over five years. If the text gives counts out of another number, turn them into 1,000 as you did for a count: 150 of 500 is 150 ÷ 500 × 1,000 = 300 in 1,000. Second, if the text gives only the change as a percent, take it off: a 50% cut takes half off 4, which leaves 2; a 25% cut takes a quarter off. Third, say it as before and after: from 4 in 1,000 to 2 in 1,000 over five years, 2 fewer in every 1,000. Keep the time: “over five years” is part of the size.
Then check each size in the headline against that. A share of the risk (“halves”, “by a third”, “by 25%”) holds up when the before and after drop by that share. A number of people (“spares 1 in 1,000”, “keeps half of patients from …”) holds up when that many fewer people have it. A strong word with no number (“slashes”, “dramatically”) decides nothing: the numbers do. And a claim that the pill makes the difference stands only when a coin toss (or a lottery) split the groups; otherwise the text shows only that the two went together.
If the text does not give the risk before and after (or the start and the change), you cannot tell, unless it says in words that the claim cannot be right, such as that hardly anyone had it to begin with.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “50% less risk means half of the people are spared.”
The right line: It means the risk is half as big. If 4 in 1,000 people would have a stroke and the pill cuts that to 2, the pill spares 2 in every 1,000 people. A big percent of a small risk is still small.

## Step 2: A worked example

A news story, from "Eastbay Courier · Health":

> # New pill spares half of patients a stroke
>
> In a five-year study where a coin toss decided who got Zelvin and who got a dummy pill, people on Zelvin had 50% fewer strokes, the Eastbay University team reported. Without Zelvin, about 4 in every 1,000 people aged 50 to 60, like those in the study, have a stroke within five years.
> The pill is taken once a day.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**What is this claiming?**
(the options come in an order that depends on the seed of the question)
- Zelvin keeps half of the people who take it from having a stroke
- People who take Zelvin have fewer strokes
- Zelvin keeps half of the people who have had a stroke from having another

The working, one step at a time:
1. Find what the headline claims.: “Spares half of patients” says half of the people who take the pill are kept from a stroke.
1. Find where the risk started.: The second sentence: without the pill, about 4 in every 1,000 people have a stroke in five years.
1. Take the change off the starting number.: 50% fewer than 4 is 2. So from 4 in 1,000 to 2 in 1,000 over five years.
1. Check the headline’s size against that.: The headline’s size is a number of people: half of them. The pill spares 2 people in every 1,000, not half. It halves a small risk, which is not the same as sparing half the people.

Then the answer: Zelvin keeps half of the people who take it from having a stroke / Without Zelvin, about 4 in every 1,000 people aged 50 to 60, like those in the study, have a stroke within five years. / from 4 in 1,000 to 2 in 1,000 / Does not show it. “Half” is the size of the change, 50%. The risk was 4 in every 1,000 people, and the pill brings it to 2 in 1,000 over five years, which spares 2 people in every 1,000, not half of the people who take it.
What you would need to see: You would need a headline that says what the study found: from 4 in 1,000 to 2 in 1,000 over five years.
