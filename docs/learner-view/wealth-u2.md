## Part 1 of 3: Fees, and when a fee is worth paying

### 1. Before you fix it, find out what is taking the money

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 1 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

When money goes out of someone’s savings every year, find out what is taking it before you try to fix it. A fee, a tax bill and a spending habit each need a different fix, and sometimes the right fix is to leave it alone.

You have probably met all six of these without knowing they were different.

A man reads his 401(k) statement and finds that the funds and an adviser together take 2% of his money every year. A woman pays her accountant $3,000 a year and has never asked what it buys. A couple find that the bond fund in their ordinary account is taxed every year, though they have sold nothing. A man is about to sell *a fund* that has gone up, because a newsletter said to, and has not asked what the tax will be. A woman sold one fund at a profit and is angry about another that has fallen. A couple still take the $40,000 a year they chose when they retired, though their savings are much smaller now.

Each has a different cause and a different fix, and one needs no fix at all. Cheaper funds do not lower a tax bill, and a tax move does not change how much is spent. Find out which one you are looking at before you touch anything.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“What could lose this money?”** Its answers:

- **“Money going out every year”**: give this answer when money goes out of the pot every year: fees charged by funds or an adviser, tax on the investments, or a sum taken out to spend. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“One thing that could wipe it out”**: give this answer when most of the pot depends on one thing: one company or property that is most of it, a claim that could reach everything they own, or a lender who could force a sale.
- **“Prices falling at the wrong time”**: give this answer when a fall in prices would hurt because of what the money has to pay for or how it is split: living costs, a bill due on a set date, or a mix drifted from its plan.
- **“Handing it over”**: give this answer when the money passes to others when the owner dies, can no longer manage it or gives it away, and the question is who gets it, the tax on it, the papers, or the people.
- **“Nothing could lose it”**: give this answer when money is being kept, and none of the other four show up: no needless fee or tax, nothing most of it depends on, no bill or drifted mix, and no handover coming.

### 2. Funds that copy a list

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 2 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-indexfund`]

The first name is about paying someone to pick investments. To see the alternative, you need one word.

*Two funds, one list*

> Two funds each hold shares in about 500 companies. In the first, a team of managers meets every week to decide which shares to buy and which to sell, and the fund charges 1.1% a year. The second holds every company on a published list of about 500 of the largest companies in the US, in the same proportions as the list, and buys or sells only when the list changes. It charges 0.1% a year.

In the first fund, a team of managers decides every week what to buy and sell, and the team has to be paid. That is most of the 1.1%. In the second, nobody decides anything: the fund copies a published list, so there is no team to pay and the fee can be 0.1%.

On $100,000 that is $1,100 a year against $100. The first fund may beat its list or fall behind it, and nobody can say which in advance. The fee is the one thing that is certain.

**The word for this.** *An index fund*: a fund that simply holds every company on a published list, such as the largest companies in the US, with nobody choosing what to buy, so it charges very little.


### 3. Switch to index funds

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 3 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-feecore`]

The first of the six: a yearly fee that pays for picking investments and nothing else.

*Mara and the two fees*

> Mara, 46, has $250,000 in an IRA, all of it in one fund where managers pick the shares. ⟦The fund charges 1.2% a year. Her adviser's firm, which recommended the fund and has done nothing else for her since, takes another 0.8% a year⟧. Together that is 2% of the pot, $5,000, every year. A fund that simply holds every company on a published list of the largest companies charges 0.1% a year, $250 on the same money.

Mara pays two fees, and both pay for the same thing: picking. The fund’s managers pick the shares, and the adviser picked the fund and has done nothing since. Together that is 2% of *the pot*, $5,000 a year, against $250 for *an index fund*.

Over thirty years the gap is huge. If the money grows 4% a year before fees, $250,000 becomes about $452,800 at 2% and about $787,800 at 0.1%. The picking costs Mara about $335,000, and the managers would have to beat the list by 1.9 points every year to earn it back. The fee is certain. What it buys is a hope.

**How to spot it**

1. **Add up every fee on the money: Mara pays 1.2% to the fund and 0.8% to the adviser, 2% in all.** Fees stack, and statements often show them on separate lines.
2. **Ask what each fee pays for: the fund’s managers pick shares, and the adviser picked the fund.** If the answer is only picking, you are paying for a hope.
3. **Check whether anything else is done for the money: for Mara, nothing.** A fee that also pays for real work is a different story, met next.
4. **Compare with *an index fund*: 0.1%, which is $250 a year instead of $5,000.** The gap is what the picking costs you.

This is **Switch to index funds**. The fix is an index fund, where nobody is paid to pick.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **If the fees pay only for picking, move the money into *an index fund*.** Mara would keep about $4,750 a year.
2. **In an ordinary account, work out the tax on selling before you sell.** A sale can cost more in tax than the fee saves in a year or two.

You may also hear this called “a low-cost core” or “passive investing”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Switch to index funds**.

### 5. Two kinds of account

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 5 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-sheltered`]

The next name needs one more word: the kind of account the investments sit in.

*Leila’s two accounts*

> Leila, 45, has $40,000 in an IRA and $40,000 in an ordinary brokerage account. Both hold the same fund, which pays out $1,600 of income a year. In the IRA the law does not tax that income while the money stays in; it taxes what she takes out in retirement. In the brokerage account she pays 25% of it, $400, every year.

Leila holds the same fund in two places and pays different tax on it. In the IRA, the $1,600 the fund pays out each year is not taxed; the law taxes what she takes out in retirement. In the brokerage account, the same $1,600 is taxed every year, and she pays 25% of it, $400. Same fund, same income. Only the account differs.

In the US, sheltered accounts are the ones the law sets up for retirement and a few other purposes: a 401(k) at work, an IRA, a Roth IRA, a health savings account (HSA) and a 529 plan for college. Most have a yearly limit on what you can pay in, and Congress changes the rules often. In this unit, an IRA or a 401(k) is the sheltered account, and an ordinary brokerage account (also called a taxable account) is not. The 25% on interest and rent and the 15% on most dividends are examples that stand for federal and state tax together.

**The word for this.** *A sheltered account*: an account the law taxes less, or later, such as a 401(k), an IRA or a Roth IRA, with a yearly limit on what can be paid in. An ordinary brokerage account, taxed in full, is a taxable account.

If you have both kinds of account, you choose which investment goes where. A fund that pays out little costs almost nothing in an ordinary account, and one that pays out a lot costs the most. Choosing where each one sits is the one tax move that needs no sale.


### 6. Nothing to cut back

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 6 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-nocut`]

Not every yearly cost needs cutting. Here is a fee that is worth paying.

*Kamal and his planner*

> Kamal, 58, pays his planner ⟦$3,000 a year, a flat price agreed in writing that has not changed in five years, though his pot has doubled in that time. For it the planner prepares his tax return, checks that his will and forms are current, and updates his spending plan, none of which Kamal would do himself⟧. The planner takes no commission, and Kamal's money is in index funds.

Kamal’s planner costs $3,000 a year, and for it Kamal gets three jobs he would not do himself: his tax return, a check that his will and forms are current, and an update of his spending plan. Stop paying, and the return is late, the will goes out of date and the plan goes stale. Cutting this fee would save $3,000 and cost him all three.

Look at how the price behaves too. 1% of $300,000 is $3,000, and so is a flat $3,000. But when *the pot* doubles to $600,000, 1% becomes $6,000 and the flat price stays at $3,000. A flat price is tied to the work. A price that grows with *the pot* is not.

**How to spot it**

1. **Ask what would stop if you stopped paying: Kamal’s tax return, will check and plan.** A fee is worth paying when real work would stop without it.
2. **Check the price is flat: $3,000, in writing, unchanged in five years while his pot doubled.** A flat price pays for the work, not for the size of *the pot*.
3. **Check nobody is paid to pick: his planner takes no commission and his money is already in index funds.** A fee for picking would be **Switch to index funds**.

This is **Nothing to cut back**. It is a full answer: leave it alone.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Write down what the fee is for and what would stop without it.** You can show it to anyone who calls the fee too high.
2. **If someone offers something cheaper, ask what work the cheaper one would not do.** Cheaper often means less gets done.
3. **Leave it alone, and set a date to look again.** The work or the price may change.

You may also hear this called “a cost worth paying”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Nothing to cut back**.

### 8. Switch to index funds or Nothing to cut back: telling them apart

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 8 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-feecore-nocut`]

These two are easy to mix up: in both, a firm or an adviser is paid out of *the pot* every year.

**Story A**

> Gwen, 60, has $300,000 with a firm of advisers. The firm takes 1% of it each year, $3,000, to choose her funds, and has done nothing else for her since the day it chose them.

**Story B**

> Gwen's sister Ann, 60, has $300,000 with the same firm. It takes $3,000 a year from her too, a flat price that does not move when her money does. For it the firm prepares her tax return, reviews her will and her forms, and updates her plan each spring. Her money is in index funds.

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the same firm and the same $3,000 a year, for two sisters with the same pot. Compare one thing: what each $3,000 pays for.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Nothing more than it should”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A, Gwen’s firm takes 1% of her $300,000 and has done nothing since it chose her funds. The $3,000 pays for picking, and it would grow if her pot did. The answer is **“A yearly fee for picking investments”**, so this is **Switch to index funds**.

In Story B, the firm takes $3,000 from Ann too, but it is a flat price for a tax return, a check of her will and an update of her plan. Stop the firm and those stop. The answer is **“Nothing more than it should”**, so this is **Nothing to cut back**.

The size and the firm are the same, so neither tells you anything. What the money pays for does, and so does whether the price moves with *the pot*.

**How to tell them apart**

If the fee stopped, what important job would stop being done? Can you find that job in the story, and a price that stays the same when *the pot* grows?

## Part 2 of 3: Tax

### 9. Right account for each investment

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 9 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-location`]

Now tax. This one comes every year, and nothing has been sold.

*Ana and the bond fund*

> Ana, 44, has $50,000 in an IRA and $50,000 in an ordinary brokerage account. The IRA holds a fund of shares that pays out very little. ⟦The brokerage account holds a bond fund that pays out $2,400 of interest a year, and Ana pays 25% tax on it, $600, every year⟧.

Ana pays tax every year on the income her bond fund pays out, whether or not she sells anything. In her ordinary account the $2,400 of interest costs $600 a year. In her IRA, which is *a sheltered account*, the same interest would cost nothing.

Her two funds could swap places. The fund of shares pays out about $500 a year, which costs $75 in the ordinary account. Put the bond fund in the IRA and the shares in the ordinary account, and her yearly tax drops from $600 to $75. That saves $525 a year. An investment that pays out a lot gives the IRS something to tax every year. One that pays out little mostly grows in price, and that is taxed only when you sell.

**How to spot it**

1. **List each investment, its account and what it paid out last year: Ana’s bond fund paid out $2,400.** The 1099 form your brokerage sends shows it.
2. **Find the investment that pays out the most: her bond fund.** Its payouts are what get taxed every year.
3. **See which account holds it: the ordinary account, where Ana pays $600 a year on it.** In an IRA the same payout is not taxed that year.
4. **Check the IRA holds something that pays out little: Ana’s IRA holds shares that pay almost nothing.** That leaves room for the bond fund to move in.

This is **Right account for each investment**. The fix is to swap which account holds what.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Swap what the two accounts hold: the bond fund into the IRA, the shares into the ordinary account.** Switching funds inside an IRA is not taxed.
2. **In the ordinary account, work out the tax on selling before you sell.** If the tax is large, the next step avoids it.
3. **If the tax is large, pay each year’s new money into the sheltered account instead, up to its yearly limit.** New money shifts the balance with no sale.

You may also hear this called “asset location”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Right account for each investment**.

### 11. Right account for each investment or Nothing to cut back: telling them apart

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 11 of 26*

The same two funds can be a problem or fine. It depends on which account holds which.

**Story A**

> Ravi, 57, keeps a fund of company bonds in his ordinary brokerage account. It pays out $3,000 of interest a year, and he pays 25% tax on it, $750, every year. His IRA holds a fund of shares that pays out almost nothing.

**Story B**

> Ravi's brother Sunil, 57, has the same two funds, the other way round. His bond fund is in his IRA, where its $3,000 a year is not taxed. His fund of shares is in his brokerage account, where its $600 a year costs him $90 in tax.

**What to compare.** Ravi and his brother Sunil hold the same two funds, the other way round. Compare one thing: which account holds the fund that pays out the most.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Nothing more than it should”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A, Ravi’s bond fund pays out $3,000 a year and sits in his ordinary account, where he pays $750 a year on it. His IRA holds the fund that pays out almost nothing. The answer is **“Yearly tax on income in the taxable account”**, so this is **Right account for each investment**.

In Story B, Sunil has the same two funds the other way round. His bond fund is in the IRA, so its $3,000 is not taxed, and his fund of shares costs him $90 in the ordinary account. That is already about as low as it gets. The answer is **“Nothing more than it should”**, so this is **Nothing to cut back**.

Same funds, same sums, same accounts. Only which fund sits in which account differs, and it turns $750 of tax a year into $90.

**How to tell them apart**

Which account holds the fund that pays out the most each year: the taxed one or the sheltered one?


### 12. A profit on paper

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 12 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-gain`]

Two of the next names are about tax on a sale. First, one word for the profit that triggers it.

*Tomás and his shares*

> Tomás bought 1,000 shares in a company for $10 each, $10,000 in all. Today each share is worth $16, so his 1,000 shares are worth $16,000. He has not sold any.

Tomás paid $10,000 for his shares, and they are now worth $16,000. On paper he is $6,000 better off. But he has sold nothing, so nobody has paid him that $6,000.

Nothing is due while he holds the shares. If he sells them all, he has made $6,000 more than he paid, and tax is due on that $6,000: at 15%, $900. If he sells none, the tax is nothing. If the shares had fallen to $7,000 instead, he would be $3,000 below what he paid. That is a loss, not *a gain*.

**The word for this.** *A gain*: how far something has risen above what was paid for it. Tax on a gain is usually due only when it is sold; until then it is a gain on paper. Sold after more than a year it is a long-term gain, taxed at a lower rate than a short-term gain on something held a year or less.

The stories that follow assume everything sold was held for more than a year, and use 15% on a gain and 25% on interest and rent. Rates change, so these are examples. The idea holds: what has not been sold has not been taxed.


### 13. Delay the tax by not selling

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 13 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-defer`]

The last tax came every year without a sale. This one comes only if you sell.

*Imogen and the profit*

> Imogen, 57, bought a fund for $24,000 several years ago, and it is now worth $30,000. Her adviser says it has had a good run and suggests she 'lock in the profit' ⟦by selling it all and moving the money into another fund, which is much like the one she holds. Imogen has no bill to pay and no need for the cash. Selling would bring tax of 15% on the $6,000 gain, $900⟧.

Imogen’s adviser says to “lock in the profit”. But a profit on paper is already hers. Selling does not make it more hers, it only makes it taxable. She would move the money into a very similar fund, so nothing changes except that $900 goes to the IRS. Sell, and $30,000 becomes $29,100 to invest. Do not sell, and the full $30,000 keeps working.

Not selling does not make the $6,000 tax-free for ever. If she sells one day, the gain, maybe larger by then, is taxed. What she gains is time: she pays later, and the $900 keeps working in the meantime.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check that something has gone up: Imogen paid $24,000 and it is now worth $30,000.** With no gain there is no tax.
2. **Find the planned sale: her adviser wants her to sell it all and move into a similar fund.** The tax comes only if the sale happens.
3. **Work out the tax: a $6,000 gain at 15% is $900.** That is the price of the sale.
4. **Ask what the sale is for: Imogen has no bill to pay and no need for the cash.** A remark, a hunch or a wish to tidy up is not a reason.

This is **Delay the tax by not selling**. If nothing needs the sale, do not make it.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **If the reason is only a remark, a hunch or a wish to tidy up, do not sell.** The tax you do not pay keeps working for you.
2. **See whether new money could do the same job: if shares have drifted from 60% to 62% of your mix, put your next payments into bonds.** That restores your 60/40 split with no sale and no tax.
3. **If a sale is truly needed, sell the part with the smallest gain first.** That part brings the smallest tax bill.

You may also hear this called “deferring the tax on a gain”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Delay the tax by not selling**.

### 16. Use a loss to cut tax

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 16 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-harvest`]

The last name was about a sale nobody needs. This one starts after a sale has already happened.

*Sam and the fund that fell*

> Sam, 49, sold some shares in March, which he had held for years, for $5,000 more than he paid for them, ⟦so he will owe 15% tax on that gain, $750. In the same brokerage account he still holds a fund he has not sold. He paid $12,000 for it, and it is now worth $9,000⟧.

Sam will pay tax on a $5,000 gain: 15% is $750. But tax on gains is worked out on the year’s gains and losses added together, and Sam has a loss waiting. The fund he has not sold cost $12,000 and is now worth $9,000. If he sells it, that $3,000 loss cancels part of the gain. The taxed gain falls to $2,000, the tax from $750 to $300, and he saves $450 ($3,000 at 15%).

He does not have to give up the investment. He can put the $9,000 into a similar fund that is not the same one. Under the wash-sale rule, if he buys the same investment, or one almost identical, within 30 days before or after the sale, the loss does not count. In the US the tax year ends on December 31.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find a sale this year at a profit: Sam sold shares in March for $5,000 more than he paid.** Without a taxed gain, a loss has nothing to cancel.
2. **Find an unsold investment worth less than was paid: Sam’s fund cost $12,000 and is worth $9,000.** Without a loss, there is nothing to use.
3. **Check the fund sits in an ordinary account: Sam holds it in his brokerage account.** A loss inside an IRA cannot be set against the gain.

This is **Use a loss to cut tax**. Sell the fund that fell, and its loss cancels part of the gain.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Before December 31, list what you sold this year and the gain on each.** That is the gain you can cancel.
2. **List what you hold in an ordinary account that is worth less than you paid.** Each one is a loss you could use.
3. **Sell the one that fell and buy a similar fund that is not identical.** You stay invested, and the wash-sale rule does not cancel the loss.
4. **Keep the paperwork for both sales.** You need it to show the loss on your tax return.

You may also hear this called “tax-loss harvesting”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Use a loss to cut tax**.

### 19. Spend a percentage of the pot

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 19 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-burnrate`]

The last one is not a fee or a tax bill. It is the sum you take out to live on.

*Hugh and his $50,000*

> Hugh retired at 62 with $1,000,000. The pot has grown about 3% a year since. ⟦He decided to spend $50,000 a year, which was 5% of it, and he still spends $50,000 a year. Nine years on the pot is worth about $800,000, so the $50,000 is now 6.25% of what is left⟧.

Spending is what savings are for, so the sum is not wrong. What matters is its size against his pot. When Hugh retired, $50,000 was 5% of $1,000,000. Now his pot is about $800,000, so the same $50,000 is 6.25%. It feeds itself: his pot earns about $24,000 a year, Hugh takes $50,000, and a smaller pot earns even less next year.

The fix is to set the sum as a percentage of what *the pot* is worth at the start of each year, not a number of dollars. Say 3.5%: that is $35,000 on $1,000,000, and $28,000 if it falls to $800,000. The spending drops when your pot does, and that is what makes it last. The 3.5% is an example, not a promise: the right share depends on your age, your plans and other income such as Social Security.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the sum taken out each year: Hugh takes $50,000.** Everything else is measured against it.
2. **Check whether it has ever been reset: he still takes $50,000, the same as the year he retired.** A sum set in dollars never follows his pot.
3. **Divide it by what his pot is worth today: $50,000 ÷ $800,000 is 6.25%.** That is the share you are really taking now.
4. **Compare it with the share when you set it: 5% then, 6.25% now.** A bigger share of a smaller pot is what drains it.

This is **Spend a percentage of the pot**. Set the sum as a percentage of your pot, and work it out again each year.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Choose a percentage you can live on.** Examples often start between 3.5% and 4%, which is not a promise.
2. **Work it out again each January from what your pot is worth that day.** The sum then rises and falls with your pot.
3. **Decide now what you would cut first in a bad year.** A plan made before the bad year is easier to keep.

You may also hear this called “a withdrawal rate” or “percentage withdrawals”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Spend a percentage of the pot**.

### 22. The question to ask about any yearly cost

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 22 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-erosion`]

Here is the question and its six answers in one place.

**The question:** **“What is taking money out of it?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other five.

- **“A yearly fee for picking investments”**
  - Give this answer when a yearly fee for picking investments, far more than an index fund would take, pays for nothing else, or grows with the pot while the other work it pays for does not.
  - It leads to **Switch to index funds**.
- **“Yearly tax on income in the taxable account”**
  - Give this answer when investments that pay income every year, like rent or interest, are taxed in the taxable account while the sheltered account has room or holds ones that pay little.
  - It leads to **Right account for each investment**.
- **“Tax on a sale that does not have to happen”**
  - Give this answer when a sale is planned of something now worth more than was paid for it, it would bring a tax bill on the gain, and nothing needs the sale.
  - It leads to **Delay the tax by not selling**.
- **“Tax on a gain, with a loss left unsold”**
  - Give this answer when something was sold this tax year at a gain that will be taxed, and another investment in the taxable account, not yet sold, is worth less than was paid for it.
  - It leads to **Use a loss to cut tax**.
- **“The same yearly sum from a pot that has shrunk”**
  - Give this answer when a fixed sum is taken out to spend every year, it was set when the pot was worth more, and it is now a bigger share of a smaller pot.
  - It leads to **Spend a percentage of the pot**.
- **“Nothing more than it should”**
  - Give this answer when what comes out each year is worth it or already as low as it can be, like a flat fee for real work, and none of the other five show up.
  - It leads to **Nothing to cut back**.

**Why it matters**

Each answer has its own fix, and one fix does nothing for another: cheaper funds do not lower a tax bill, and moving investments between accounts does not change how much is spent. So look at what is taking the money out, not at how big the sum is or who is involved.

Two people can have the same pot and lose the same $3,000 a year, and need opposite things. One pays $3,000 to someone who picked investments and did nothing else. The other pays $3,000 for a tax return, a check of a will and a plan. The answer for the first is **“A yearly fee for picking investments”**. For the second it is **“Nothing more than it should”**.

Any fix you buy for a story that shows **“Nothing more than it should”** costs money and fixes nothing.

**How to answer it**

1. **Read the whole story first.** The words that decide it are often in the last sentence.
2. **If the money goes to a firm or an adviser, ask what it pays for.** Picking is **“A yearly fee for picking investments”**, and named work at a set price is **“Nothing more than it should”**.
3. **If it goes to the IRS, ask whether the tax comes every year or only with a sale.** Every year is **“Yearly tax on income in the taxable account”**, and with a sale is **“Tax on a sale that does not have to happen”** or **“Tax on a gain, with a loss left unsold”**.
4. **If it is the person’s own spending, ask whether the sum is ever reset.** Never reset is **“The same yearly sum from a pot that has shrunk”**, and reset each year is **“Nothing more than it should”**.
5. **Find the exact words that show your answer.** If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet, so do not invent a problem.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Some stories show two at once. Say your mix has drifted (**“Prices falling at the wrong time”**), and putting it back needs a sale that would bring a tax bill new money could avoid. The answer to the first question is then **“Money going out every year”**, and the answer to this one is **“Tax on a sale that does not have to happen”**. A sum fixed in dollars and left alone while a fall in prices shrinks *the pot* is handled the same way: the answer here is **“The same yearly sum from a pot that has shrunk”**.

- Switch to index funds or Nothing to cut back: If the fee stopped, what important job would stop being done? Can you find that job in the story, and a price that stays the same when *the pot* grows?
- Right account for each investment or Delay the tax by not selling: Does the tax arrive every year without anyone selling anything? Or would it arrive only if something were sold?
- Delay the tax by not selling or Use a loss to cut tax: Has something already been sold this year at *a gain*? And is another investment, not yet sold, worth less than was paid for it?
- Right account for each investment or Nothing to cut back: Which account holds the fund that pays out the most each year: the taxed one or the sheltered one?
- Spend a percentage of the pot or Nothing to cut back: Is the sum the same number of dollars as in earlier years, or worked out again each year from what *the pot* is worth now?

### 25. What to carry away

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 25 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap`]

This card puts the unit in one place.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What is taking money out of it?
- A yearly fee for picking investments → Switch to index funds
- Yearly tax on income in the taxable account → Right account for each investment
- Tax on a sale that does not have to happen → Delay the tax by not selling
- Tax on a gain, with a loss left unsold → Use a loss to cut tax
- The same yearly sum from a pot that has shrunk → Spend a percentage of the pot
- Nothing more than it should → Nothing to cut back

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Switch to index funds**: a yearly fee for picking investments, far more than an index fund charges, that pays for nothing else, or grows with the pot while the other work it pays for does not.
- **Nothing to cut back**: money going out every year that is worth it or already as low as it can be, like a flat fee for real work, or spending already set as a percentage of the pot.
- **Right account for each investment**: a taxable account holding investments that pay income every year, taxed each year, and a sheltered account with room to spare or holding ones that pay little.
- **Delay the tax by not selling**: something worth more than was paid for it, a planned sale that would bring a tax bill on the gain, and no real need to sell, since new money could do the same job.
- **Use a loss to cut tax**: something sold this tax year at a taxable gain, and another investment in the taxable account, not yet sold, now worth less than was paid for it.
- **Spend a percentage of the pot**: a fixed sum taken out to spend every year, set when the pot was worth more, and now a bigger share of a smaller pot.

**To carry away**

- Say what is taking money out of *the pot*, and find the words in the story that show it. If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.
- Ask what a fee pays for. A fee for picking investments and nothing else is a problem. A fee for named work that would not otherwise get done, at a price that does not grow with *the pot*, is not. Size alone settles neither.
- Tax comes in three shapes: on income every year, with nothing sold; on *a gain*, only if you sell; and on *a gain* already made this year, with a loss waiting beside it. Each has its own fix. Do not sell just because tax is in the story. Sell only to use a loss, or when the sale has a real job to do.
- A fixed number of dollars taken from a pot that has shrunk is a bigger share than it was. The fix is a percentage of what *the pot* is worth now, worked out again each year.
- **“Nothing more than it should”** is a real answer, and a common one. If you cannot find words that show a problem, do not invent one, and do not buy a cure for it.
