# Wealth Preservation: The spending rate

Revision 1, live. Part: The spending rate. Where the subject ends up: Run a planner's yearly check on your own US accounts: list everything, see what money you need soon is exposed and what single loss could ruin you, find the certain leaks (yearly costs in dollars, a missed match, tax in the wrong place, foreign filings) and the risks you did not choose (one big bet, a drifted mix), check who inherits and who can act, and change only what the check calls for.

Waits for a moment: the learner marks "I will stop working within a year" before the step on their own data opens.

## The why

Ruth stops working next month. She plans to spend $66,000 a year, with about $6,000 of tax on what she takes out; Social Security will pay $24,000, and her accounts the rest.
Take too much in the first years and the money may run out; too little and she lives smaller than she needs to. Here you check the rate against the research.

## Step 1: The rule

A year’s spending, plus the tax on what you take out of a 401(k) or IRA (the research’s rate is before tax), less the income that keeps coming (Social Security, a pension), is what your accounts must give. Divide it by your invested accounts (401(k), IRA, brokerage; the bank savings are your buffer) × 100: your spending rate.
At or under the rate looked up for this year: leave it. Over it: change it, by spending less, working longer, or planning to cut in bad years. The rate lasted 30 years in 90 of 100 tries in the research; it is an estimate that moves each year, not a promise.
Each later year, take last year’s amount raised by the rise in prices, not a new share of what is there. A fall in the first ten years hurts most: spending a bit less for a while lets the money last.

A value looked up, never memorized: "The highest starting spending rate, raised each year for prices, that would last 30 years in 90 of 100 simulated futures for a balanced mix, for someone retiring in 2026 (taxes come out of it)": 3.9%. It prints "Looked up 2026-10-22" and "Source: Morningstar, The State of Retirement Income, December 2025 (morningstar.com)", says to check the source, and after a year says to look it up again.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “I will take 4% of whatever is there each year, and Social Security is extra.”
The right line: Social Security pays part of the year, so the accounts give less; the tax on withdrawals makes them give more. And the amount follows prices, not this year’s balance.

## Step 2: A worked example

A statement, "Ruth’s accounts" (a tab named "Accounts", one of a household's papers: one open at a time, the first at the start, and the one with a marked line after the answer):

> Account  |  Balance  (the column heads)
> 401(k)  |  $620,000
> IRA  |  $280,000
> Brokerage account  |  $100,000
> Bank savings  |  $30,000

A plain table, "Ruth’s year, after work" (a tab named "A year", one of a household's papers: one open at a time, the first at the start, and the one with a marked line after the answer):

> What  |  A year  (the column heads)
> What Ruth spends  |  $66,000
> Tax on what comes out of the 401(k) and IRA  |  $6,000
> Social Security, coming every year  |  $24,000
> (Prices rose 3% this past year.)

The working, one step at a time:
1. Spending, plus the tax on withdrawals, less the income that keeps coming. $66,000 + $6,000 − $24,000 = $48,000.
1. Divide by the invested accounts (not the bank). $620,000 + $280,000 + $100,000 = $1,000,000. $48,000 ÷ $1,000,000 = 4.8%.
1. Hold it against the rate looked up for this year. 4.8% is over 3.9%. Change it.
1. Work out what the accounts can give at the line. 3.9% of $1,000,000 = $39,000, against $48,000 now.

Then the answer: $48,000 / 4.8% / Change it / $39,000. $66,000 of spending and $6,000 of tax, less $24,000 of Social Security, is $48,000 from the accounts: 4.8% of $1,000,000, over the 3.9% looked up for this year. At 3.9% the accounts give about $39,000: spend less, work longer, or plan to cut in bad years.
