# US Civics & History: Who is telling you

Revision 1, live. Part: Who is telling you. Where the subject ends up: Answer the citizenship interview’s civics questions aloud from memory, and, reading a news story about a US law, order or ruling, say who acted, whether it was theirs to do, who can stop it, and whether to take the story as it stands or check who is behind it first.

## The why

A story can look official and still come from someone who wants you to believe it. People who check facts for a living do not read the page harder. They leave it and see what others say about who is behind it.
The real move is made on your own phone, on the open web. Here you practice the first decisions, and then you mark that you did it.

## Step 1: Four moves

A plain table, "What fact checkers do":

> Move  |  What it means  (the column heads)
> Look for trusted work  |  Search the claim and find a report by people who have no stake in it.
> Find the original  |  Go to the law, the speech or the record itself, not someone’s telling of it.
> Investigate the source  |  Leave the page and see who is behind it. The source’s own pages say only what it wants you to read, even under a name like “independent” or “official”.
> Circle back  |  Return to the story and read it again, knowing more.

## Step 2: A wrong idea

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “It looks official, so I can trust it.” A page can have a flag, a serious name and an About page, and still be run by anyone.
The right line: A seal, a .org, “nonpartisan” or a paper’s kind of name can be put on any page. Do not judge a page by how it looks. Leave it and see what others say about who is behind it, or go to the original record.

## Step 3: When to check

You do not have to check everything.
Take a story as it stands only when both hold: you have already checked who is behind the source, or it is the original record you went to yourself, and what it says is ordinary. If you cannot place the source, or the story is alarming or sudden, check before you rely on it: leave the page and see what others say.
Alarming or sudden means a big change to many people’s lives, money or rights, out of nowhere: every bank account frozen, all cash ended. The usual work of government is ordinary, even when it starts soon: a vote, a new fee, a road closed, new benches in a park.

## Step 4: The same group, two pages

| A | B |
|---|---|
| The group’s own About page: “We are neighbors who want safe streets.” | A paper’s report on who pays for the group. |

Compare: The first is the group talking about itself. The second is someone else checking.

## Step 5: A worked example

A flyer in your mailbox says a group called Safe Streets Now wants a new state law. You have never heard of the group. You search its name and see these four results.

SafeStreetsNow.org: Who pays for us? Our funding, explained by us The Harbor Gazette: Who pays for Safe Streets Now? A look at its donors Safe Streets Now: Donate today Sponsored: Safe Streets Now, trusted by police

The working, one step at a time:
1. Say what you want to know: You want to know who is behind the group.
1. Leave the group’s own pages: The page titled “Who pays for us?” sits on the group’s own site, so its answer is the group’s word about itself. A report by people outside the group can say who pays for it.

Then the answer: The Harbor Gazette: Who pays for Safe Streets Now? A look at its donors. A report by people who do not work for the group can tell you who is behind it. The group’s own pages, even one titled “Who pays for us?”, say only what the group wants you to read.
