# Wealth Preservation: Your list, and what could lose it

Revision 1, live. Part: Your list, and what could lose it. Where the subject ends up: Run a planner's yearly check on your own US accounts: list everything, see what money you need soon is exposed and what single loss could ruin you, find the certain leaks (yearly costs in dollars, a missed match, tax in the wrong place, foreign filings) and the risks you did not choose (one big bet, a drifted mix), check who inherits and who can act, and change only what the check calls for.

## The why

Maya keeps a bank account, a brokerage account and a 401(k). She checks each one alone and never adds them up.
A careful planner starts with the whole list: every account, what is in it, and what pays if the bank or the firm fails. Nothing gets changed before that list exists.

## Step 1: The rule

List every account with its balance, then add them up. Next to each, write what pays if the bank or the firm itself fails. A bank’s papers say “Member FDIC”. A brokerage firm holds stocks and funds for you, your holdings; its papers say “Member SIPC”.
A 401(k) usually has neither: the law keeps its money in a trust, a pot held for you apart from your employer, so your employer failing does not take it. Its papers say “Not FDIC insured”.
FDIC covers up to its limit for each person, at each bank, for each kind of ownership. Accounts in your name alone at one bank are added up together. A second bank is counted on its own, and so is a joint account: with one other person, it is covered up to the limit for each of you. SIPC works the same way at a firm: your own account, a joint one and an IRA are each covered to its limit, of which $250,000 may be cash.
Over the limit? Change it: move the part over to another bank, or into another kind of ownership. Neither FDIC nor SIPC pays anything when prices fall.

A value looked up, never memorized: "FDIC insurance, per person, per kind of ownership, at each insured bank": $250,000. It prints "Looked up 2026-10-22" and "Source: FDIC, Understanding Deposit Insurance (fdic.gov)", says to check the source, and after a year says to look it up again.

A value looked up, never memorized: "SIPC protection when a brokerage firm fails, for each customer in each capacity (alone, joint, an IRA), and of it the most for cash alone; never a fall in prices": $500,000; $250,000. It prints "Looked up 2026-10-22" and "Source: SIPC, What SIPC Protects (sipc.org)", says to check the source, and after a year says to look it up again.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “My brokerage account is insured, so I cannot lose money in it.”
The right line: SIPC pays when the firm fails and your holdings are missing. When prices fall, the loss is yours.

The working, one step at a time:
1. Read each paper’s total, and its line on cover. Harbor Bank: $262,400, Member FDIC. Northpoint Brokerage: $60,000, Member SIPC. 401(k): $94,500, neither: Not FDIC insured, held in a trust.
1. Add up everything. $262,400 + $60,000 + $94,500 = $416,900.
1. Match what happened to its cover. The $6,300 fall is Maya’s loss. FDIC and SIPC pay only when a bank or a firm fails.
1. Add up each bank, and each kind of ownership at it, against the FDIC limit. Checking and savings are both in Maya’s name at one bank, so FDIC counts them together: $262,400, over $250,000.
1. Work out the part with no cover. $262,400 − $250,000 = $12,400.
1. Say where it could go. To an account at another bank: FDIC counts each bank on its own.

Then the answer: Everything at Harbor Bank: $262,400; What protects it: FDIC, if the bank fails; Everything at Northpoint Brokerage: $60,000; What protects it: SIPC, if the brokerage firm fails; The 401(k): $94,500; What protects it: Neither / $416,900 / Nobody / Change it / $12,400 / An account in Maya’s name at another bank. Everything comes to $416,900. The two accounts at Harbor Bank are in one name, so FDIC counts $262,400 against its $250,000: $12,400 has no cover if the bank fails. Moving it to another bank covers it.
