## Part 1 of 3: Fees, and prices that fall at the wrong time

### 1. Before you change anything about your money, find what could lose it

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 1 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient-gate`]

Before you buy a fix for your money, move it, or take anyone’s advice about it, find out what could actually lose it. It is always one of five things, and each one needs a different fix.

You have probably heard some version of these four stories.

A man in his fifties reads his 401(k) statement for the first time in twenty years. The fund has been taking 1.7% of his money every year to run it: $3,400 a year on $200,000, whether the fund did well or badly.

A farmer is sued after a worker is hurt on her land. Her farm, her house and her savings are all in her own name, and the demand for payment is bigger than her insurance.

Two parents sell shares in a stock-market crash to pay their son’s college tuition, because the bill is due in September and the money was in shares. The prices are back up the next year. What they sold is not.

A man dies and the life insurance from his job goes to his first wife, because the form he signed twenty years ago still names her. His widow gets nothing from it.

Each loses money in a different way: a little every year, all at once through one thing, in a fall in prices, or when the money is passed on. A fifth story is as common as any of them: money that is simply sitting there, with nothing that could lose it. Most people reach for a fix before they know which of these they are dealing with. This unit teaches you to tell them apart first.

What could lose this money?
- Money going out every year → fees, tax or spending that take a bite out of the pot every year
- One thing that could wipe it out → one stock, property, business, lawsuit or loan that could take most of the pot at once
- Prices falling at the wrong time → share and fund prices falling just when the money is needed
- Handing it over → what happens when the owner dies, gets too ill to act, or gives money to family
- Nothing could lose it → money put away, with nothing in the story that could lose it

The unit has three parts, and you can stop after any of them.

1. Fees, and prices that fall at the wrong time
2. One big risk, what happens at death, and nothing wrong
3. One whole story, then the drill

Each starts from a real story. After each one you answer a quick question, and the reason is shown right away. Nothing here is graded. A miss only decides what comes back.

### 2. What counts as your money

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 2 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-pot`]

One word first, so that every story means the same thing by it.

*Nadia’s total*

> Nadia is 52. She has $40,000 in a savings account, $150,000 in her 401(k), a condo worth $220,000 with nothing owed on it, and a one-fifth share of her brother's bakery, which the family values at $60,000. She also earns $5,000 a month.

Add up everything Nadia owns: $40,000 + $150,000 + $220,000 + $60,000 = $470,000. That is what she has built up and wants to keep. Her $5,000 a month in pay is not in it: it arrives and it is spent.

**The word for this.** *The pot*: all the money a person has built up and wants to keep: savings, investments, property, a share of a business. Not the pay that arrives each month.

From here on that total is *the pot*. Every question in this unit is about it, never about the pay that arrives each month.


### 3. A basket of investments bought in one go

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 3 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-fund`]

The second word is about how most people end up owning shares without choosing them one by one.

*Lena and the fund*

> Lena puts $10,000 into one fund that holds shares in 500 companies. Each year the company that runs the fund takes 0.2% for doing so, $20. In a bad year the price of the fund falls by 20%, because most of what is in it has fallen.

Lena has not chosen 500 companies. She has bought one thing that holds a little of each, so if a few do badly the rest cover for them.

Two numbers matter. The first is the fee: $20 a year, taken whether the fund does well or badly, so it is easy never to see it. The second is the price: when most of the companies fall, the fund falls with them, and 20% off $10,000 is $2,000.

**The word for this.** *A fund*: a basket of many investments bought in one go, for a yearly fee, whose price rises and falls with what is in it.

This is *a fund*. Most 401(k)s hold one, so a yearly fee, and a price that drops when the market drops, show up in many stories.


### 4. Money going out every year

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 4 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-erosion`]

First: the answer that is easiest to miss, because nothing dramatic ever happens in it.

*Colin and the 401(k)*

> Colin is 54. He has $200,000 in a fund in his 401(k), and for twenty years he has never read the yearly statement. This year he reads it and finds that ⟦the fund takes 1.7% of the money in it every year for running it, whether prices rose or fell⟧.

Colin has paid this fee for twenty years without knowing. 1.7% of $200,000 is $3,400. Taken once, that is hardly noticeable. Taken every year for twenty years it is $68,000, before counting the growth that money would have earned if it had stayed. Nobody sends a bill, which is why it goes unnoticed.

Money can leave like this in three forms: a fee, paid to the firm that runs your investments or to an adviser; tax, on investments that pay out income every year; and a sum you take out to spend. None is wrong in itself. The only question here is whether money is leaving.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the sum that leaves every year: Colin’s 1.7% fee.** It comes out again next year, and the year after.
2. **Check that it leaves whatever prices do: “whether prices rose or fell”.** A sum that only appears when prices fall is a different answer.
3. **Work out the yearly amount: 1.7% of $200,000 is $3,400.** A small percentage hides a big sum.
4. **Check nothing else is going on: no big sale, no bill, no death.** Then the yearly sum is all there is to name.

This is **“Money going out every year”**. It does not say the sum is too big or unfair, only that money leaves every year.

You may also hear this called “a slow leak”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **“Money going out every year”**.

### 6. A loan with a fixed payout

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 6 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-bond`]

The next answer is about falling prices, and prices fall for some things and not for others. One more word, for something whose price wobbles but whose payout does not.

*Omar’s loan*

> Omar lends $1,000 to a government for five years. Each year it pays him $30 in interest, and at the end it repays his $1,000. In the third year, if he wanted to sell the loan to someone else, the price might be $950 on one day and $1,040 on another.

Omar did not buy a piece of a company. He lent money, and the loan comes with a promise: $30 a year, and his $1,000 back on a set date.

The price of the loan does move, but that only matters if he has to sell early. If he waits for the date, he gets his $1,000.

**The word for this.** *A bond*: a loan to a government or a company that pays interest and repays a set amount on a set date. Held to that date, it pays exactly that amount, whatever its price did in between.

This is *a bond*. Companies borrow this way too, not just governments.


### 7. The split between shares, bonds and cash

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 7 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-mix`]

One last word before the next answer: how a person has divided their money between the things it can be held in.

*Hana’s split*

> Hana chose to keep her money half in shares and half in bonds. That was twelve years ago. Since then shares have done well, and today $300,000 of her $400,000 is in shares and $100,000 is in bonds. She retires in three years.

Shares rise and fall more than bonds, so the split decides how rough a ride the money gets. Hana planned half and half, but her shares grew and are now three-quarters of her money.

If shares fall 30%, she loses $90,000, which is 22.5% of everything. On her plan, with $200,000 in shares, the same fall would cost $60,000, which is 15%. Nobody decided to take that extra risk. It built up while she was not looking.

**The word for this.** *The mix*: how the pot is split between shares, bonds and cash, such as 60% shares and 40% bonds. The plan is the split the person chose.

This is *the mix*. When it drifts away from the one the person chose, a fall takes more of their money than they meant to risk.


### 8. Prices falling at the wrong time

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 8 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-timing`]

Second: money leaves in this one too, so it is easy to mix up with the first. What matters this time is not how much leaves, but when.

*Pete and Jean*

> Pete and Jean are both 67. Everything they own is $400,000 in shares and funds, and ⟦every month they sell about $1,700 of them to pay their bills⟧. ⟦They have no cash set aside⟧. ⟦This spring prices fell by 30%⟧.

Pete and Jean pay about $1,700 a month in bills, and they have no cash, so every bill is paid by selling shares. Say each share costs $10. Before the fall, $1,700 means selling 170 shares. After it, at $7, the same bills mean selling 243: 73 more. If prices come back, those 73 are not there to rise with them.

A fall does nothing to a person who is not selling. It hurts a person who has to sell on a day the market does not care about. Someone with cash set aside to live on until prices recover hardly notices the same fall.

Two other situations belong here. One is a bill of a known size on a known day, with the money for it still in shares or funds that can fall. The other is *the mix* drifting away from its plan, so that a fall takes more than the person chose.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find what the money is held in: shares and funds, $400,000.** Their price can fall. Cash does not, and neither does *a bond* held to its date.
2. **Find what the money has to pay for: $1,700 a month in bills, with nothing set aside in cash.** A fall only hurts when something is waiting for the money.
3. **Check whether prices are down on the days it is needed: this spring they fell 30%.** Each sale is at a lower price, and what is sold is not there when prices recover.

This is **“Prices falling at the wrong time”**. It does not say prices will fall, only what a fall would do to money that nothing has been set aside to protect.

You may also hear this called “bad timing”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **“Prices falling at the wrong time”**.

### 10. Money going out every year or Prices falling at the wrong time: telling them apart

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 10 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-erosion-timing`]

Money leaves the owner in both of these, and a fall in prices may be somewhere in the story. They are easy to mix up.

**Story A**

> Greta and Sam are retired and have $300,000. Every December their adviser's firm takes 1.1% of it, $3,300, however the funds did that year.

**Story B**

> Greta and Sam are retired and have $300,000. They pay their bills by selling about $1,500 of their funds each month, with nothing set aside in cash, and this year prices are down 25%.

**What to compare.** Both stories are about Greta and Sam, who are retired and have $300,000. Compare one thing: does the same sum leave every year whatever prices do, or does money have to be raised on certain days because prices have fallen?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Prices falling at the wrong time”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the adviser takes the same $3,300 in a good year and in a bad one, and nothing is sold on a bad day. That is **“Money going out every year”**.

In Story B nobody takes a fee. The bills are paid by selling funds with nothing in cash, in a market that has fallen, so each sale is at a lower price and what is sold is not there when prices come back. That is **“Prices falling at the wrong time”**.

**How to tell them apart**

Does a sum leave *the pot* every year, whatever prices do? Or does money have to be raised on a certain day, with prices down?

## Part 2 of 3: One big risk, what happens at death, and nothing wrong

### 11. Someone says you owe them for harm

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 11 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-claim`]

The next answer uses one more word. It is about what happens when somebody says you owe them money for harm you are said to have caused.

*Mick’s letter*

> Mick runs a small moving company. A delivery driver slips on ice in his yard and breaks her leg. Her lawyer writes to Mick, saying that the yard was not safe and that he must pay $400,000 for her injury, her lost pay and her pain. Mick's insurance pays up to $250,000 for an accident like this.

The lawyer’s letter is not a bill for something Mick bought. It is a demand that he pay for harm he is said to have caused. If he refuses and the driver wins, a court can order him to pay, and what he owns can be taken.

The amount does not depend on what Mick has saved or what his insurance covers. The insurance pays $250,000, so he would have to find the other $150,000 from whatever he owns.

**The word for this.** *A claim*: a demand, backed by the courts, that someone pay for harm they are said to have caused, such as an injury on their property or in a crash they caused.

This is *a claim*. Watch the gap: how much could be demanded, against how much the insurance would pay.


### 12. One thing that could wipe it out

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 12 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-shock`]

Third: most of someone’s money resting on one thing. It looks nothing like the first two.

*Karim and the company shares*

> Karim, 45, has $500,000 in all. ⟦$350,000 of it is shares in the company he works for⟧, which he has built up over twenty years. The rest is in a savings account and a small 401(k).

Karim has $350,000 of his $500,000 in one company, the one he works for. That is 70%. If its price halves, he loses $175,000, which is 35% of everything he has. If the company fails, he loses 70%.

Had the same $350,000 been in *a fund* that holds 500 companies, a fall of a fifth would cost him $70,000, and no single company could take much more than a sliver. A bad year hurts. One company failing can end a working life’s savings.

The one thing does not have to be shares. It can be a building, a business you run, *a claim* that could reach everything you have, or a loan whose lender could force a sale. In each, one thing could take most of your money at once, even while every other price stays put.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the biggest single thing: $350,000 in the shares of the company Karim works for.** If most of the money is there, it is not spread out.
2. **Work out its share of the total: $350,000 out of $500,000 is 70%.** The bigger the share, the more one failure can take.
3. **Ask what he would lose if that one thing failed: 70%, while every other price stays put.** Nothing the rest of the market does would help him.
4. **Look for the other forms of one big thing: a building, a business, a demand for payment, a loan that can be demanded back.** Each can take most of the money in one blow.

This is **“One thing that could wipe it out”**. It does not say the one thing will fail, only that if it did, most of the money would go with it.

You may also hear this called “concentration risk”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **“One thing that could wipe it out”**.

### 14. One thing that could wipe it out or Prices falling at the wrong time: telling them apart

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 14 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-shock-timing`]

In both of these a large part of everything could be lost, and both can be about shares. They are easy to mix up.

**Story A**

> Lars has $500,000. $350,000 of it is shares in the company where he works. Last month a rival won the company's biggest contract, and its price fell by 40%. Prices of other companies did not move.

**Story B**

> Lars has $500,000, all of it in funds that hold shares in thousands of companies. He must pay $120,000 for a house on March 1, four months away, and prices across the whole market are down 25% this year.

**What to compare.** Both stories are about Lars, who has $500,000. Compare one thing: does one thing do the damage whatever the rest of the market does, or does the whole market fall on a day when he needs money?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“One thing that could wipe it out”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story A.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A one event hit one company: a rival won its biggest contract. Every other price stayed put, and Lars still lost 40% of $350,000, which is $140,000. That is **“One thing that could wipe it out”**.

In Story B nothing is concentrated, because his money is in funds that hold thousands of companies. What hurts is that the whole market is down 25% in a year when he must pay $120,000: to raise it he has to sell funds at the lower price, so he gives up more of them. That is **“Prices falling at the wrong time”**.

**How to tell them apart**

Would one thing do the damage even if every other price stayed where it is? Or do prices fall everywhere, on a day when the money is needed?


### 15. Handing it over

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 15 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-handover`]

Fourth: what happens to the money when its owner dies, or can no longer act for themselves.

*Gerald and the old form*

> Gerald is 68. He has life insurance through his job that would pay $300,000. The form he signed with the insurer twenty years ago ⟦names his first wife as the person who should receive it if he dies⟧. He married again fifteen years ago, and ⟦has never changed the form⟧.

Gerald’s $300,000 of life insurance will be paid to whoever the form names, and the form names his first wife. If he died tomorrow, the money would go to a woman he is no longer married to, and his widow would get nothing. The loss comes once, and Gerald will not be there to fix it.

The same goes any time money passes to other people, or someone else has to act for its owner: a death, an illness that stops the owner acting, or gifts to family. Three things can go wrong. The papers can be out of date or missing, as here. Tax can take a part of a large inheritance before the family gets it: the federal estate tax takes 40% of what a person leaves above a tax-free limit, so it only hits large estates. And the people who get or run the money can lose it by what they do.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the moment it changes hands: Gerald’s death.** What is at stake comes once, not every year.
2. **Find the paper that decides who gets it: the insurance form he signed twenty years ago.** The insurer pays whoever the form names.
3. **Check the paper against real life: it names his first wife, and he has married again.** A paper that is out of date is the commonest way to lose money here.
4. **If the papers are fine, look at the tax and the people: a very large estate, or heirs who do not get on.** Those are the other two ways it can go wrong.

This is **“Handing it over”**. It does not say something will go wrong, only that the money is about to pass to other people.

### 17. Nothing could lose it

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 17 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-none`]

Last: the answer for a story where nothing could lose the money.

*Aisha and the 401(k)*

> Aisha, 35, ⟦pays $400 a month into a 401(k), and she will not touch it for thirty years⟧. She also keeps six months of her pay in a savings account. She asks a friend, 'Is there something I should be doing about all this?'

Check Aisha’s story against the other four answers. Nothing comes out of her money that the story mentions. No one thing is most of it. She will not need the 401(k) for thirty years, so prices have time to come back from any fall. And nothing is said about a death, an illness or a gift. What is left is money being kept, with nothing that could lose it.

This is a real answer, and a common one. Without it you would find a problem in every account of money and buy a fix for it, and a fix for a problem that is not there costs money and effort for nothing.

**How to spot it**

1. **Look for a fee, a tax bill or spending that comes out each year: Aisha’s story has none.** If you cannot find the words, do not invent them.
2. **Look for one thing that is most of the money: Aisha has a 401(k) and savings.** No single thing could take most of it.
3. **Look for money needed soon: she will not touch her 401(k) for thirty years.** A fall has thirty years to recover.

### 21. The question to ask before anything else

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 21 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-gate`]

This card puts the question and its five answers in one place.

**The question:** **“What could lose this money?”**

**Its answers**

- **“Money going out every year”**
  - Give this answer when money goes out of the pot every year: fees charged by funds or an adviser, tax on the investments, or a sum taken out to spend.
- **“One thing that could wipe it out”**
  - Give this answer when most of the pot depends on one thing: one company or property that is most of it, a claim that could reach everything they own, or a lender who could force a sale.
- **“Prices falling at the wrong time”**
  - Give this answer when a fall in prices would hurt because of what the money has to pay for or how it is split: living costs, a bill due on a set date, or a mix drifted from its plan.
- **“Handing it over”**
  - Give this answer when the money passes to others when the owner dies, can no longer manage it or gives it away, and the question is who gets it, the tax on it, the papers, or the people.
- **“Nothing could lose it”**
  - Give this answer when money is being kept, and none of the other four show up: no needless fee or tax, nothing most of it depends on, no bill or drifted mix, and no handover coming.

**Why it matters**

Each of the four loses money a different way and has a different fix, so this answer decides what to ask next. It tells you where to look, not that something is wrong: in each of the four, the risk may already be taken care of.

Get this wrong and you fix the wrong thing. Take a yearly fee for a fall in prices and you go looking for what to sell and when, when the real problem is a sum that leaves whatever prices do. A fix answers one particular way of losing money, so you need to know which one first.

**How to answer it**

1. **Read to the end before you answer.** The day, or the one thing that matters, is often in the last sentence.
2. **Look in the words for each of the four problems, one at a time.** For each, you must be able to find the words that show it.
3. **Found none? The answer is “Nothing could lose it”, and you stop.** A problem that is not in the words is one you invented.
4. **Put your finger on the words that show your answer: the sum and how often, the day and what the money is held in, the one thing and how much of everything it is, the death or the paper.** If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Some stories show two answers at once, and one of the two wins. Three pairs have no card of their own, and each is easy to mix up: **“Nothing could lose it”** and **“Money going out every year”**, **“Handing it over”** and **“Money going out every year”**, **“Nothing could lose it”** and **“One thing that could wipe it out”**. The test for each pair is printed below.

- Money going out every year or Prices falling at the wrong time: Does a sum leave *the pot* every year, whatever prices do? Or does money have to be raised on a certain day, with prices down?
- One thing that could wipe it out or Prices falling at the wrong time: Would one thing do the damage even if every other price stayed where it is? Or do prices fall everywhere, on a day when the money is needed?
- Nothing could lose it or Prices falling at the wrong time: When is the money needed? Is something waiting for it, such as living costs, a bill on a date or a drifted mix? Or is nothing needed for years?
- Nothing could lose it or Money going out every year: Does the story say anything about a fee, a tax bill or money taken out? If you cannot find the words, it does not.
- Handing it over or Money going out every year: Does it come out every year while the owner is alive? Or does it come up once, at a death, an illness or a gift?
- Nothing could lose it or Handing it over: Does the story say anything about a death, a will, a form, an illness or a gift? If you cannot find the words, it does not.
- Nothing could lose it or One thing that could wipe it out: Is there one company, property, business, demand for payment or loan that is most of the story? Or is the money spread out, with nothing that could reach all of it?

### 23. What to carry away

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 23 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap-gate`]

You have now answered the first question on your own.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What could lose this money?
- Money going out every year
- One thing that could wipe it out
- Prices falling at the wrong time
- Handing it over
- Nothing could lose it

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **“Money going out every year”**: money going out of the pot every year: fees for funds or advice, tax on the investments, or a sum taken out to spend.
- **“Prices falling at the wrong time”**: shares or funds whose prices can fall, and something a fall would hit: living costs paid from them, a bill due on a set date, or a mix drifted from its plan.
- **“One thing that could wipe it out”**: one thing that could take most of the pot at once: one company or property that is most of it, a claim that could reach everything, or a lender who could force a sale.
- **“Handing it over”**: a time when the money passes to other people or someone else has to handle it: a death, an illness that stops the owner acting, or gifts to family.
- **“Nothing could lose it”**: money being kept, and none of the other four: nothing going out that should not, nothing most of it depends on, nothing to pay soon, and no handover coming.

**To carry away**

- Before you fix anything, ask what could lose the money, and find the words that show it. If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.
- The answer tells you where to look. It does not say something is wrong: a fee can pay for real work.
- A fall in prices only hurts money that something is waiting for: bills, living costs, or a mix that has drifted. Money that nothing is waiting for can wait for prices to come back.
- A story where all the papers are in order is still **“Handing it over”**. **“Nothing could lose it”** is for a story that raises none of the four: if you cannot find the words, do not invent them, and do not buy a fix for a problem that is not there.
- The question is about *the pot*, everything someone has built up and wants to keep, never about the pay that arrives each month.
