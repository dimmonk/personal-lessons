# Psychology: Anchors and hindsight

Revision 1, draft (the screen carries the draft line). Part: Anchors and hindsight. Where the subject ends up: After this subject you can hear a story about someone, or catch one in your own head, and say what is going on, whether it is one moment or a lasting pattern, the name that fits or that there is too little to tell, and what to do about it, when someone reaches for "narcissist", "gaslighting" or "toxic".

## The why

A recruiter says, "We start people at $52,000," and the $75,000 you planned to name suddenly feels greedy. A friend's shop closes, and you say you always knew, though you cheered at the opening.
In about 18 minutes: two slants, each beside its fair twin, and what to do about each. In the check, a story counts as right only when every answer is right.

## Step 1: The five questions, on numbers and the past

The five questions from before: 1. What is going on? 2. One moment, or the same shape over and over? 3. What does it do for them? 4. What is the nearest look-alike, and what one detail separates them? 5. What would prove this read wrong?
Most stories today are one person judging one number or one past event, once. For those, Questions 1 and 2 are settled, and the work is in 3 to 5.
A name needs its telltale signs together. The names: anchoring and hindsight, new today; the five from before for a mind talking itself into something; "ordinary", when the facts show a normal reason; and "too little to tell", when the facts are too few to see what is going on.

## Step 2: Anchoring: the first number sticks

People watched a wheel stop on a random number, then estimated something unrelated. Their estimates followed the wheel: people start from the number they are handed and do not move far enough from it, partly because it brings to mind reasons that fit it (Strack and Mussweiler 1997). A listing price pulled real estate agents as much as students (Northcraft and Neale 1987).
The fair twin is a fair reference price: a number backed by facts. Question 4: what backs the number? Question 5: would a different first number change your estimate?

| A | B |
|---|---|
| A used truck is listed at $18,000. Teo thought $12,000 was fair, and offered $16,000 "to meet in the middle." | A used truck is listed at $18,000. Teo found four like it that sold last month for $17,500 to $18,500, and offered $17,000. |

Compare: First, the listing pulled Teo from his own figure. Second, real sales back it: a fair reference price.

## Step 3: Hindsight: "I knew it all along"

Some people were told how a historical event ended. They rated that ending far likelier than people not told, without noticing. A close relative, outcome bias, judges a decision only by how it turned out (Baron and Hershey 1988); both go under hindsight here, since the fix is the same.
The fair twins: a prediction recorded beforehand, and criticism the facts of the day backed. Question 4: what could be known then?

| A | B |
|---|---|
| The coach called a pass, the play that had worked all season. It was intercepted. "Terrible call," said Dale. | Before the game Dale texted, "If they pass late, it gets picked off." It was intercepted. "Terrible call," he said. |

Compare: First, judged by the ending. Second, a prediction recorded beforehand.

## Step 4: The telltale signs, and what to do

Anchoring: a first number, even a random or self-serving one, pulls the estimate toward it; the guess moves too little away from where it started.
Hindsight: once the outcome is known, it feels like it was obvious all along, and the people who missed it look foolish.
What to do. Anchoring: make your own estimate from the facts before you hear theirs, and name it first when you can. Hindsight: judge by what could be known then, and write predictions down. Ordinary: nothing to fix. Too little to tell: find out more.
The wrong idea: "A random number would never fool me." Experts are moved too. Write your own figure down first.

## Step 5: A worked example

Before her interview, Renata looked up the pay for the job and found $70,000 to $85,000 posted by four employers in town. The recruiter opened with, "We usually start people at $52,000." Renata had planned to say $75,000. She said, "Could you do $58,000?" and was pleased when they agreed.

The working, one step at a time:
1. Check the telltale signs, then name it. She is one person judging one number, so the menu fits: a name for a mind talking itself into something, ordinary, or too little to tell. The signs: she had her own facts, $70,000 to $85,000. Then a first number arrived, and the figure she named landed just above it, far below her facts. Question 3, what it does for her: taking their number spares her judging from scratch. Question 4, the look-alike: a fair reference price would be backed by facts, and here her facts say the opposite. Anchoring. Question 5, what would prove it wrong: the posted pay really being near $52,000.
1. Pick the response that fits. The pull came from hearing their number before she had fixed hers. So: make your own estimate from the facts before you hear theirs, write it down, and name it first when you can.

Then the answer: Anchoring / Make your own estimate from the facts before you hear theirs. Her facts said $70,000 to $85,000, yet the figure she named sat just above the recruiter's first number. Anchoring: make your own estimate from the facts before you hear theirs, and name it first.
