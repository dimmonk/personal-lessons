## Part 1 of 2: Four ways a figure goes wrong, one story each

### 1. Before you trust a figure, check who is in it

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

Before you believe a figure like “four in five workers want a four-day week”, check who it was worked out from and how they got in. Four things go wrong here, and each one is easy to spot once you know what to look for.

A magazine says, "Four in five workers want a four-day week." An ad says, "Nine in ten people who try our studio say it helps them." Each figure was worked out from some particular people, and the claim speaks for a much bigger group.

Some people are in a figure because they were picked fairly. Others are in because of what happened to them: they stayed, they spoke up, they wrote back, or there were only a few of them. A figure from some of a group is not wrong for that reason alone, and you will practice telling the difference.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“Where does this claim first go wrong?”** Its answers:

- **“Who was counted”**: give this answer when the people or things the figure came from do not match the group the claim is about, or are too few to trust. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“What the number counts”**: give this answer when the figure could rise, fall or differ without the real thing it is taken to show doing the same.
- **“What it is compared with”**: give this answer when the figure is a percentage change, a test’s accuracy, or a total beside another total, and the claim leaves out what you need beside it to read it fairly.
- **“What caused what”**: give this answer when the claim says one thing made another happen, and the same result could have come about another way.
- **“Nothing goes wrong”**: give this answer when every part the claim uses checks out in order, and none of them goes wrong.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. Survivorship bias

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-survivor`]

First: a list of the ones that made it, with a conclusion drawn from what they share.

*The Mill Street restaurants*

> Forty restaurants opened on Mill Street in 2014. The street's business newsletter now ⟦lists the 12 that are still open⟧, and notes that all 12 open on Sundays. 'Open on Sundays and your restaurant will last,' the newsletter says.

The list is correct. The problem is who is on it: only the 12 restaurants still open. The 28 that closed are missing, so the list cannot tell you what the ones that did not last were like.

Say Sunday opening makes no difference, and 38 of the 40 restaurants opened on Sundays. Then nearly every restaurant still open would open on Sundays too, so a list of 12 that all open on Sundays is just what you would see if it did nothing. The ones that closed are missing because of what happened to them, and that is usually tied to what the figure is about.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find how many started: 40 restaurants.** A figure only means something next to the number that began.
2. **Find who is in the figure: the 12 still open.** A list made at the end holds only the ones that are still there.
3. **Ask what happened to the rest: 28 closed.** They are missing for a reason, and it is tied to what the figure is about.
4. **Look for a conclusion drawn from the ones that lasted: “Open on Sundays and your restaurant will last.”** The ones that closed may have done the same, and the list cannot show it.

This is **Survivorship bias**. A “survivor” is one still there at the end, and “bias” is a lean in one direction: here, toward the ones that made it.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Ask how many started and what happened to the ones who are not here.** The answer is hidden in the missing ones.
2. **Say what the figure shows for the ones that lasted, and no more.** It is exact for them and silent about everyone else.
3. **Do not copy what the survivors did until you know whether the ones that closed did it too.** What they share may be the same in both groups.

You may also hear this called “looking only at the winners”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Survivorship bias**.

### 4. Survivorship bias or A fair count: telling them apart

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-survivor-samp`]

The same trial can be reported two ways, and the two headlines can look alike.

**Story A**

> A seed company says: 'Our tomato plants give 14 kilograms each.' It planted 50 seeds in a trial garden. 30 plants were still alive in September, and the 14 kilograms is their average. The 20 that died early are not counted.

**Story B**

> A seed company reports on its trial garden: 'We planted 50 tomato seeds, and every one is counted. 30 plants lived to September and 20 died early. All together the 50 gave 420 kilograms, which is 8.4 kilograms for every seed planted.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the same seed company, the same trial garden and the same 420 kilograms of tomatoes. Compare one thing: how many of the seeds planted are in the figure, and what is said about the ones that died.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Nothing goes wrong”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the 14 kilograms is the average of the 30 plants that were alive in September, and the 20 that died early are not in it. Spread over all 50 seeds, 420 kilograms is 8.4 kilograms each, not 14. That is **Survivorship bias**.

In Story B the company counts every seed it planted, the 30 that lived and the 20 that died, and reports 8.4 kilograms for every seed planted. Nothing is left out, and the claim stays with the 50 seeds of the trial. That is **A fair count**.

The harvest is the same in both. What separates them is who is in the average.

**How to tell them apart**

How many started, and are all of them in the figure? If some are missing, are they missing because of what happened to them?


### 5. Self-selection bias

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 5 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-selfselect`]

Second: nobody was asked, and the figure comes from whoever chose to answer.

*The four-day-week poll*

> A trade magazine ⟦put a one-click poll on its website⟧: 'Would you like a four-day work week?' 3,200 readers clicked, and 2,560 of them said yes. The headline read: 'Four in five workers want a four-day week.'

The count is right: 2,560 out of 3,200 is 80 in every 100. The problem is who answered. Nobody picked them, they picked themselves, and people who choose to answer a poll are usually the ones who feel most strongly about it.

The poll cannot say how the readers who did not click would have answered. If the magazine has 100,000 readers, those 2,560 yes answers fit anything from 3 in every 100 readers to 99 in every 100. A big count does not fix this: 3,200 people who chose themselves are as one-sided as 32.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find how people were asked: a one-click poll on the website.** If nobody was picked or asked by name, anyone could join in.
2. **Find who is in the figure: the 3,200 readers who clicked.** They decided for themselves to take part.
3. **Find who the claim speaks for: “workers” in general.** A claim for a bigger group than the ones who clicked is where it goes wrong.

This is **Self-selection bias**. “Self-selection” means choosing yourself: the people in the figure picked themselves into it.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Say who the figure is for: “of the 3,200 readers who clicked, four in five said yes”.** It is true of them and of nobody else.
2. **To learn what a wider group thinks, look for a figure from people picked by lottery from a full list, with most of them answering.** Then nobody chose themselves in.

You may also hear this called “a self-selected sample” or “a voluntary poll”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Self-selection bias**.

### 7. Volunteers, split by lottery

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `exception`, id `exc-volunteers`]

People can also volunteer for a study, and the claim from it can still hold. This is a story where it does.

*The sleep lab’s pillow*

> A sleep lab asked for volunteers through a newspaper ad, and 600 people came forward. The lab drew names by lottery: 300 were given a new pillow, and 300 kept their own. After a month, the people with the new pillow slept 25 minutes longer a night on average. The lab says: 'The new pillow adds 25 minutes of sleep.'

These volunteers put themselves forward, like the people in a website poll. Yet this claim holds: it is **A fair test**, and not **Self-selection bias**.

**You are asked:** This looks like **Self-selection bias**. Before you read why it is **A fair test**, tap the words in the story that settle it.

The pieces you can tap:
1. “A sleep lab asked for volunteers through a newspaper ad, and 600 people came forward”
2. “The lab drew names by lottery: 300 were given a new pillow, and 300 kept their own”
3. “the people with the new pillow slept 25 minutes longer a night on average”
4. “The new pillow adds 25 minutes of sleep”

**Shown as soon as you tap.** The words are “The lab drew names by lottery: 300 were given a new pillow, and 300 kept their own”.
- If you tapped “A sleep lab asked for volunteers through a newspaper ad, and 600 people came forward”: This is what makes it look like a poll anyone could answer. It says how people joined, and you want how they were put into groups.
- If you tapped “the people with the new pillow slept 25 minutes longer a night on average”: That is the result. It says how big the difference was, not how the groups were formed.
- If you tapped “The new pillow adds 25 minutes of sleep”: That is the claim. You want the words that say how the groups were formed.

**Why this is A fair test and not Self-selection bias**

What is the claim about? Not what volunteers think. It is about a difference between two groups: the 300 who got the new pillow and the 300 who kept their own. The lab drew names by lottery to decide who went in which group.

The volunteers are probably not like most people: maybe they sleep worse, or care more about sleep. But that is as true of the pillow group as of the other group, so being a volunteer cannot be why one group slept 25 minutes longer.

Volunteers alone do not make **Self-selection bias**. Look for a figure read as true of people who never took part.

**How to tell them apart**

Does the claim speak for a wider group than the people who came forward, or is it about a difference between two groups that a lottery formed?


### 8. Non-response bias

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 8 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-nonresp`]

Third: everyone on a list was asked by name, and many did not answer.

*The Sunday library survey*

> A city library ⟦mailed a questionnaire to all 2,400 people with a library card⟧: 'Should we open on Sundays?' 336 mailed it back, and 300 of those said yes. The library announced: 'Nine in ten of our members want Sunday opening.' It ⟦did nothing to hear from the other 2,064⟧.

This is different from the poll on a website. Here nobody chose themselves in: the library asked everyone on its list. What went wrong came afterward. Most did not answer, and nothing was done about it.

People who mail a form back are not a typical slice of the list. They are likelier to care, and silence is not agreement. If all 2,064 silent members would have said no, only 300 of 2,400, one in eight, want Sunday opening. If all would have said yes, nearly everyone does. The announcement picks one point in that wide range.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find who was asked: all 2,400 people with a library card.** Everyone on a known list was asked, so nobody chose themselves in.
2. **Count how many answered: 336 of 2,400.** That is only 14 in every 100.
3. **Check what was done about the rest: nothing, for the other 2,064.** If the library had phoned them until most answered, this would not be a problem.
4. **Check who the claim speaks for: “our members”, all of them.** The replies are being read as the whole list.

This is **Non-response bias**. “Non-response” means not responding: the figure leans toward the few who wrote back.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Find the two numbers: how many were asked, and how many answered.** The gap is how much of the list is missing.
2. **Ask what was done to hear from the rest.** A follow-up that reaches most of the list changes the answer.
3. **Say the figure for the ones who answered, and look for one from most of the list.** Only that can speak for everyone.

You may also hear this called “a low response rate”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Non-response bias**.

### 10. Too few to trust

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 10 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-smalln`]

Fourth: nobody was left out, but there are so few that luck moves the figure.

*The reading ranking*

> A county newspaper ranked its elementary schools by the share of sixth graders at the top reading level. Fenwick Elementary came first: ⟦9 of its 10 sixth graders reached the top level⟧, which is 90 in every 100. The paper wrote: 'Fenwick is the best school in the county for reading.' Dalton Elementary, the county's biggest, had 150 of its 200 sixth graders at the top level, which is 75 in every 100.

The sums are right and nobody is missing. The problem is how few children the top figure rests on. With only 10 sixth graders, one child is 10 points: if one more had missed the top level it would be 80 in every 100, and if the one who missed had reached it, 100. At Dalton, one child moves it by half a point.

That is why the smallest groups crowd both ends of any ranking: their figures swing the most. A school that is first this year because a few strong readers happened to be in that year may be near the bottom next year, and the newspaper reads that luck as quality.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find how many are in the figure: 10 sixth graders at Fenwick.** Nobody is left out here, so the only question is how many.
2. **Try one child more or fewer: 9 of 10 becomes 8 of 10, or 10 of 10.** If that moves the figure a lot, luck can move it too.
3. **Check what the newspaper makes of it: “the best school in the county”.** A high figure from a handful is being read as meaning something.

This is **Too few to trust**. Luck can move a figure this small, so a high or low result from it means little.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Ask what the figure would be with one or two more or fewer.** That shows how far luck could move it.
2. **Look for the same figure over more years, places or people, and go by that.** Luck evens out over more.
3. **Say what the figure shows for the group it came from, and no more.** It is true of them and says little beyond.

You may also hear this called “a small sample” or “small numbers” or “the law of small numbers”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Too few to trust**.

### 12. Self-selection bias or Non-response bias: telling them apart

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 12 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-selfselect-nonresp`]

These two look alike: a lot of people had their say, and the rest stayed silent.

**Story A**

> A school emailed a survey to each of its 1,200 families by name: 'Is there too much homework?' 240 families replied, and 204 said yes. The school did nothing to hear from the other 960. The principal says: 'Parents say there is too much homework: 85 in every 100.'

**Story B**

> A school put a link to a survey in its newsletter and on its website: 'Is there too much homework?' Anyone who saw the link could answer. 240 people did, and 204 said yes. The principal says: 'Parents say there is too much homework: 85 in every 100.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the same school, the same question and the same figure: 240 answers, 204 of them yes. Compare one thing: was everyone on a known list asked by name, or could anyone who saw the call answer?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Everyone on a list was asked, and many did not reply”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story A.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the school emailed each of its 1,200 families by name. 240 replied, and the school did nothing about the other 960. Everyone was asked and most did not answer. That is **Non-response bias**.

In Story B the school put a link in its newsletter and on its website. Nobody was asked by name, and anyone who saw the link could answer. 240 did. That is **Self-selection bias**.

In Story A the silent 960 were asked, so you can name them and ring them. In Story B nobody was asked, so there is no list of the silent ones.

**How to tell them apart**

Was everyone on a known list asked by name, or could anyone who saw the call answer?


### 13. Non-response bias or A fair count: telling them apart

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 13 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-nonresp-samp`]

A figure from replies to a list can also hold, when most of the list has answered. The same list can be reported both ways.

**Story A**

> A town swimming pool mailed a questionnaire to the 1,500 households on its members' list: 'Should the pool open at 6 a.m. on weekdays?' 150 replied, and 108 said yes. The pool did nothing to reach the other 1,350. Its notice says: 'Most members want a 6 a.m. opening.'

**Story B**

> A town swimming pool mailed a questionnaire to the 1,500 households on its members' list: 'Should the pool open at 6 a.m. on weekdays?' It then rang every household that had not replied, and in the end 1,350 of the 1,500 gave an answer. 972 said yes. Its notice says: 'Most households on our members' list want a 6 a.m. opening: 72 in every 100.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the same swimming pool, the same list of 1,500 households and the same share, 72 in every 100, saying yes. Compare one thing: how many of the list answered, and what was done about the ones who did not.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Nothing goes wrong”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the pool mailed the questionnaire and 150 of the 1,500 replied, which is 10 in every 100. It did nothing about the other 1,350. The 108 yes answers are 72 in every 100 of the 150, but only 7 in every 100 of the list. That is **Non-response bias**.

In Story B the pool asked the same list, then rang every household that had not replied, and in the end 1,350 of the 1,500 answered, which is 90 in every 100. Nine in ten of the list are in the figure, and the claim speaks only for the list. That is **A fair count**.

The share is the same in both. What separates them is how much of the list is in it.

**How to tell them apart**

How many of the list answered, and what was done about the ones who did not?


### 14. Too few to trust or A fair count: telling them apart

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 14 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-smalln-samp`]

A figure from a small group goes wrong when it is read as meaning something. A bigger group about the same thing can hold, and the same player can be reported both ways.

**Story A**

> Dana plays for her school's soccer team. She has taken 4 penalty kicks this year and scored all 4. The coach says: 'Dana never misses a penalty.'

**Story B**

> Dana plays for her school's soccer team. Over six seasons she has taken 80 penalty kicks and scored 68. The coach says: 'Dana scores 85 penalties in every 100.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the same player, Dana, and her penalty kicks. Compare one thing: how many kicks are in the figure, and what one or two more or fewer would do to it.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Who was counted”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story A.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A Dana has taken 4 penalties and scored all 4, and the coach says she never misses. Every penalty she took is counted, but one miss would turn 4 out of 4 into 3 out of 4. That is **Too few to trust**.

In Story B Dana has taken 80 penalties over six seasons and scored 68, which is 85 in every 100. One miss more or fewer moves it by about one point, and the coach says no more than the figure. That is **A fair count**.

Both are correct counts. What separates them is how many kicks are in the figure, and so how far luck could move it.

**How to tell them apart**

What would the figure be with one or two more or fewer, and does the claim say more than the group can show?


### 15. The one question to ask about the people in a figure

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 15 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-how`]

You have met this question at the foot of each new name. Here it is with its four answers in one place.

**The question:** **“How did they end up in the count?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other three.

- **“Only the ones that lasted were counted”**
  - Give this answer when the figure was taken at the end from the ones still there, like shops still open, so the ones that closed, quit or failed are not in it.
  - It leads to **Survivorship bias**.
- **“They chose to answer, when anyone could”**
  - Give this answer when anyone who wanted to could answer, like a website vote or a call-in, and the answers are taken as true of more people than the ones who chose to.
  - It leads to **Self-selection bias**.
- **“Everyone on a list was asked, and many did not reply”**
  - Give this answer when a known list of people was asked (every member, every patient, every household), many of them did not reply, and nothing was done to hear from the rest.
  - It leads to **Non-response bias**.
- **“All were counted, but there are only a handful”**
  - Give this answer when nobody was left out, but there are so few that one or two more would move the figure a lot, and a high or low figure from them is taken to mean something.
  - It leads to **Too few to trust**.

**Why it matters**

The four names differ by how the people or things got into the figure. Each leaves out a different group, or leaves too few, so knowing which one tells you which way the figure leans and what you would need to see to fix it.

Ask it when the answer to **“Where does this claim first go wrong?”** is **“Who was counted”**: the people or things in the figure are not a fair picture of the group, or there are too few of them.

Two pairs are easy to mix up. In **Survivorship bias** everyone was there at the start and the ones who left are missing. In **Self-selection bias** nobody was asked by name, and the people in the figure chose to answer. In **Non-response bias** the few who replied are a small part of a long list. In **Too few to trust** the few are everyone there is.

**How to answer it**

1. **Read the whole claim and find the words about how the people or things got in.** The answer is in those words.
2. **Look for “still open”, “still playing”, “remaining”, or a number that started bigger than the number left, and give “Only the ones that lasted were counted”.** The ones that closed, quit or left are not in the figure.
3. **Look for a link, a box, a vote or a call-in line, with nobody asked by name, and give “They chose to answer, when anyone could”.** Anyone could answer, so the ones who did chose to.
4. **Look for a number sent and a smaller number returned, and give “Everyone on a list was asked, and many did not reply”.** A known list was asked and many stayed silent.
5. **Look for a count of ten or so behind a high or low figure, and give “All were counted, but there are only a handful”.** Everyone was counted, but luck can move a figure this small.
6. **Find the exact words that show your answer.** If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.
7. **If none of the four fits, go back to the first question.** When the people in the figure are a fair picture and there are enough of them, the answer is **“Nothing goes wrong”**.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Sometimes two of the answers seem to fit. Each pair below has one question that tells it apart.

- Survivorship bias or Self-selection bias: Did the people or things in the figure get in by lasting to the end, or by choosing to answer when nobody asked them by name?
- Self-selection bias or Non-response bias: Was everyone on a known list asked by name, or could anyone who saw the call answer?
- Non-response bias or Too few to trust: Out of how many were the few counted: a much bigger list of people who did not reply, or everyone there is?

### 18. What to carry away

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 18 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap`]

You have now run the questions on your own. Here is the unit in one place.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

How did they end up in the count?
- Only the ones that lasted were counted → Survivorship bias
- They chose to answer, when anyone could → Self-selection bias
- Everyone on a list was asked, and many did not reply → Non-response bias
- All were counted, but there are only a handful → Too few to trust

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Survivorship bias**: a figure from only the ones still around at the end, the ones that quit or failed left out, and it taken as true of all who started, or as why they lasted.
- **Self-selection bias**: a poll anyone could answer, nobody picked by the people running it, and the answers of those who chose to join in taken as true of a wider group.
- **Non-response bias**: a known list of people who were all asked, many of them never replying, no effort to hear from the rest, and the replies taken as true of the whole list.
- **Too few to trust**: a group so small that one or two more or fewer would move the figure a lot, and a high or low figure from it taken to mean something.

**To carry away**

- Before you name anything, find how the people or things got into the figure, and find the words that show it. If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.
- A figure can be added up correctly and still say little about the group. A big count only tells you how exact it is for the people in it, never whether they are a fair picture of the group.
- Few replies, a small group or a poll anyone could answer do not settle it alone. Look for what each name needs: a bigger group missing from the figure, or a high or low figure read as meaning something.
- A figure is fine when everyone who started is counted, or people were picked by lottery from a full list and nearly all answered, or the group is big enough that one or two more or fewer barely move it. Then the answer to the first question is **“Nothing goes wrong”**.
- Finding that a figure leans does not make the claim false. It tells you whom the figure leaves out, and what you would need to see before you could rely on it. Ask the same question about figures you like as about ones you do not.
