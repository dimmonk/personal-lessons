## Part 1 of 2: The three things a figure can be missing

### 1. A figure needs something beside it: what, and is it there?

*Unit Five · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient-compare`]

After this unit you can read a claim built on a percentage, a test result or a ranking of two totals, and say which of three things is missing from beside the figure. You will be able to point to the words that show it, and to tell the three apart.

You meet figures like these every week. An ad says, 'Cuts your risk by 50%.' A poster says, 'This test is 99% accurate.' A ranking says, 'Hospital A saves 90 of every 100 patients, and Hospital B only 80.' In each one the figure is true as far as it goes, and in each something is left out that you would need before you could tell what the figure means.

A figure on its own is like a length with no unit. '50%' is half of something, and the ad does not say of what. '99% accurate' says how often the test is right, and what you want to know is how likely it is that you have the thing when the test says yes. A ranking of two hospitals sets two totals side by side, and a total can hide what it is made of.

Two words are used all the way through. A count is an actual number of people or things, such as 3 out of 40. A percentage is a count turned into a share of 100, and it cannot give the count back: 3 out of 40 and 30 out of 400 are both 7.5%.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“Which part of the claim goes wrong first?”** Its answers:

- **“Who was counted”**: give this answer when the people or things the figure was worked out from are not a fair picture of the group the claim is about, or are too few to trust.
- **“What the number counts”**: give this answer when the figure could rise, fall or differ without the real thing it is read as showing doing the same.
- **“What it is compared with”**: give this answer when the figure is given as a percentage of what it was, as a test’s accuracy, or as totals set side by side, and the claim leaves out something you would need beside it to read it fairly. **This unit is about these cases.**
- **“What it says caused what”**: give this answer when the claim says one thing made another happen, and the case shows another way the same result could have come about.
- **“Nothing goes wrong”**: give this answer when every part the claim makes holds up when it is checked in order, and none of them goes wrong.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a case, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. A percentage that hides how many it is

*Unit Five · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-relrisk`]

Start with the one you see most often: a headline that gives a percentage and nothing else.

*The jogging headline*

> A health website runs the headline '⟦Joggers are 40% more likely to hurt an ankle⟧.' The article does not say how many joggers were hurt, how many people it looked at, or what the 40% is more than.

- There is a figure, and it is a percentage: 40% more likely.
- The 40% is a share of an amount the headline does not tell you: how likely people were to hurt an ankle before the "more" was added.
- No count is given: not how many joggers were hurt, and not how many people the study looked at.

A percentage is a share of something, so it always rests on an amount underneath. "40% more likely" means the old chance, plus 40% of the old chance. The headline hides the old chance, and the old chance is what tells you how big the news is.

Suppose 5 of every 1,000 people who do not jog hurt an ankle in a year. 40% more is 7 in 1,000: 2 more people in every 1,000. Now suppose 25 of every 100 do. 40% more is 35 in 100: 10 more people in every 100. The headline is true both times, and the news is very different.

To tell them apart you need the two counts the percentage was worked out from: how many it was before and how many after, each out of how many.

**What you must be able to point to.** A change or a risk given as a percentage of what it was, and no word on how many it was before and after.

The name for this is **A percentage without the numbers**. The claim gives the percentage and no word on how many it was before and after. The percentage is usually right; the name points at what it leaves out.

**What to do when you meet it**

Do not decide anything from the percentage alone. Ask "Out of how many, before and after?" and look for the two counts, in the report behind the headline or the small print. If you cannot find them, say so before you share it: "Up 40%, but from what?"

You may also hear this called “relative risk” or “a big percentage of a small number”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **A percentage without the numbers**.

### 4. A test that says yes when the answer is no

*Unit Five · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-falsealarm`]

The second name rests on a word you may not have met. Start with something you probably have.

*The toast alarm*

> Priya's smoke alarm goes off every time she makes toast. Each time she opens a window, and each time there turns out to be no fire.

Priya’s smoke alarm said "fire" and there was no fire. The alarm is not broken. It is made so that it will never sleep through a real fire, and the price is that it sometimes goes off for toast or steam.

**The word for this.** *False alarm*: a test or an alarm saying yes when the thing it looks for is not there.

Almost every test or alarm works like this: a test for an illness, a scanner at an entrance, a fraud alarm on a card payment. Each says yes to some people or things that do not have what it looks for, and any figure about a test has to be read with that in mind.


### 5. A test’s accuracy read as the chance its yes is right

*Unit Five · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 5 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-baserate`]

The first name was about a change. The second is about a test: the claim gives how often a test is right, and then reads a yes from it.

*The skin test poster*

> A clinic's poster says: 'Our new test for a skin condition is 99% accurate. ⟦If your test says yes, you almost certainly have it⟧.' About 1 person in every 100 has the condition. The poster does not mention that.

- There is a test, and a figure for it: 99% accurate, which here means right 99 times in 100.
- The poster reads that as the chance that a yes is right: "you almost certainly have it".
- The condition is rare: about 1 person in 100 has it, and the poster does not mention that.

"99% accurate" and "if it says yes, you almost certainly have it" are two different numbers. To get from the first to the second you need one more, which the poster leaves out: how common the condition is among the people tested.

Take 10,000 people. 100 have the condition, and the test says yes to 99 of them. The other 9,900 do not, and the test wrongly says yes to 1 in every 100 of them: 99 people, each a *false alarm*.

So the test says yes to 198 people, and only 99 of them have the condition. A yes is right 99 times in 198: half, and not 99%. Nothing is wrong with the test. What went wrong is reading a yes without knowing how rare the condition is.

**What you must be able to point to.** A test or an alarm that is right most of the time, a yes from it, the chance that the yes is right read straight off its accuracy, and the thing being rare among the people tested.

The name for this is **Base rate fallacy**. "Base rate" means how common the thing is among the people tested, before anyone is tested, and the mistake is leaving it out.

**What to do when you meet it**

Do not read a yes from a usually-right test as being right just as often. Find out how common the thing is among the people tested, then count it out for 10,000 people: the right yeses divided by all the yeses. If the result is yours and it matters, ask for a second test before you decide anything.

You may also hear this called “base-rate neglect” or “the false positive paradox” or “ignoring how common it is”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Base rate fallacy**.

### 7. Totals that hide a different mix of cases

*Unit Five · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-simpson`]

The first two names were about a figure for one thing. The third is about two totals set side by side, and what each of them is made of.

*The two tutors*

> A tutoring website compares two math tutors. It lists: 'Ms. Hale: 75 of her 100 students passed the exam. Mr. Ruiz: 54 of his 100 students passed.' It tells families to choose Ms. Hale. The site's own records show that ⟦Ms. Hale's students were mostly ones who were already doing well, and Mr. Ruiz's were mostly ones who were already failing⟧.

- There are two totals, set side by side as a ranking: 75 of 100 students passed with Ms. Hale, 54 of 100 with Mr. Ruiz. Families are told to choose Ms. Hale.
- Each total is made of two kinds of student: those already doing well, who pass easily, and those already failing, who pass with difficulty.
- The tutors have very different mixes: Ms. Hale has mostly the first kind and Mr. Ruiz mostly the second.

Hale looks far better, but ask what each total is made of. Ms. Hale taught 90 students who were already doing well, and 72 passed (80 of every 100), and 10 who were already failing, and 3 passed (30 of every 100). Mr. Ruiz taught 10 who were doing well, and 9 passed (90 of every 100), and 90 who were failing, and 45 passed (50 of every 100).

Kind of student by kind of student, Mr. Ruiz does better with both: 90 against 80, and 50 against 30. He still has the lower total, because 90 of his 100 students were the kind who rarely pass. A total counts each group in proportion to how many are in it, and what decided these totals was how many of each kind each tutor had.

So the totals cannot rank the tutors, because the tutors did not teach the same mix. To read them fairly you need each tutor’s figure split by kind of student.

**What you must be able to point to.** Two totals set side by side, each made of easier and harder cases (mild and severe illness, strong and weak students), the hard ones a much bigger share of one total than of the other, and the totals read as a fair ranking.

The name for this is **Simpson’s paradox**. A "paradox" is something that seems to contradict itself, and here one tutor does better with every kind of student and still has the lower total.

**What to do when you meet it**

Do not choose between two things by their totals alone when each deals with different kinds of people or jobs. Ask what each total is made of, and look for the totals split by kind, easy against easy and hard against hard. If you cannot get the split, say so: "Better overall, but is it the same mix?"

You may also hear this called “totals that hide the groups”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Simpson’s paradox**.

### 9. A percentage without the numbers or A fair comparison: telling them apart

*Unit Five · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 9 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-relrisk-compok`]

The same percentage can appear in a claim that holds and in one that does not. Here are two claims about the same bus lines. One is **A percentage without the numbers**, and the other is **A fair comparison**.

**Case A**

> The city transit blog says: 'Riders on Line 12 are 50% more likely to arrive late than riders on Line 9.' The post gives no counts.

**Case B**

> The city transit blog says: 'Riders on Line 12 are more likely to arrive late than riders on Line 9.' In March, 15 of 300 trips arrived late on Line 12 and 10 of 300 trips on Line 9. Both lines are weekday commuter lines of the same length, and every trip was timed the same way.

**What to compare.** Both claims are about Line 12 and Line 9, and in both Line 12 comes out likelier to be late. Compare one thing: whether you can find the counts the percentage was worked out from.

**You are asked:** Which case gives the answer **“Nothing goes wrong”**? (Case A / Case B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Case B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Case A the claim gives "50% more likely" and no counts. It could be 4 late trips in every 100 against 6, or 40 against 60, and the claim does not let you tell which. The answer is **“What it is compared with”**, and the case is **A percentage without the numbers**.

In Case B the counts are given: 15 of 300 trips late on Line 12 and 10 of 300 on Line 9. 15 is 10 plus half of 10, so the same 50% is now backed by the numbers, and both lines were timed the same way. The answer is **“Nothing goes wrong”**, and the case is **A fair comparison**.

The percentage is the same in both. What differs is whether the counts are beside it.

**How to tell them apart**

Can you find the two counts the percentage was worked out from, each out of its own total? If you can, a percentage beside them is fine. If you cannot, the percentage is all you have.

### 10. Base rate fallacy or A fair comparison: telling them apart

*Unit Five · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 10 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-baserate-compok`]

Results from the same kind of test can be reported in a way that holds. Here is one health office telling the story two ways.

**Case A**

> A city health office says: 'Our home test for Rudd fever is 98% accurate, so if yours says yes, you very likely have it.' About 1 person in 200 has Rudd fever.

**Case B**

> The same health office reports on 10,000 people who took the home test for Rudd fever: 'People who test yes are far likelier to have Rudd fever than people who test no. Of the 248 who tested yes, 49 had it. Of the 9,752 who tested no, 1 had it.' Everyone took the same test in the same month.

**What to compare.** Both claims are about the same home test for Rudd fever. The test is 98% accurate, and about 1 person in 200 has Rudd fever. Compare one thing: whether the claim reads a yes with how common the fever is in view.

**You are asked:** Which case gives the answer **“Nothing goes wrong”**? (Case A / Case B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Case B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Case A the claim gives the accuracy, 98%, and reads a yes as "very likely" right. Of 10,000 people, 50 have the fever and the test says yes to 49 of them. Of the 9,950 who do not, it says yes to 2 in every 100: 199. So 49 of the 248 yeses are right: about 1 in 5, and not 98 in 100. The answer is **“What it is compared with”**, and the case is **Base rate fallacy**.

In Case B the claim gives those same counts and reads them as they stand: of the 248 who tested yes, 49 had it, and of the 9,752 who tested no, 1 had it. It does not say a yes is nearly certain. The answer is **“Nothing goes wrong”**, and the case is **A fair comparison**.

The test and the people are the same. What differs is whether the reading of a yes uses how common the fever is.

**How to tell them apart**

Does the claim tell you how common the thing is among the people tested, and does it read a yes with that in view?

**Case A**

> A swim club's newsletter says: 'Coach Dana's swimmers: 80 of 100 improved their time this season. Coach Eli's: 60 of 100.' It names Dana the better coach. Dana's swimmers are almost all beginners, who improve fast. Eli's are almost all national-level swimmers, who improve slowly.

**Case B**

> A swim club's newsletter says: 'Coach Dana's swimmers: 80 of 100 improved their time this season. Coach Eli's: 60 of 100.' Each coach has 50 beginners and 50 national-level swimmers, and every swimmer was timed the same way in the same pool.

**What to compare.** Both newsletters give the same two totals, 80 of 100 and 60 of 100, and both call Dana the better coach. Compare one thing: whether each coach has the same mix of beginners and national-level swimmers.

**You are asked:** Which case gives the answer **“What it is compared with”**? (Case A / Case B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Case A.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Case A Dana’s swimmers are almost all beginners and Eli’s are almost all national-level. Beginners improve fast and national-level swimmers improve slowly, so Dana’s total is high partly because of who she coaches. The totals cannot say whether she coaches better. The answer is **“What it is compared with”**, and the case is **Simpson’s paradox**.

In Case B each coach has 50 beginners and 50 national-level swimmers, timed the same way. The totals are made of the same mix, so they can be set side by side as they stand. The answer is **“Nothing goes wrong”**, and the case is **A fair comparison**.

The totals are the same in both. A total is a fair ranking only when the two sides deal with the same mix.

**How to tell them apart**

Does each total hold the same mix of easy and hard ones, or does one hold far more of the hard ones than the other?

### 12. A headline percentage built on a handful

*Unit Five · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 12 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `exception`, id `exc-handful`]

Some claims give a big percentage and the counts as well. This one shows what changes when the counts are given and they are tiny.

*The shop owner's 300%*

> A shop owner posts: 'Shoplifting at my store is up 300% this month!' The store's own log shows one theft last month and four this month.

The shop owner’s post gives a percentage, up 300%, and a percentage is what you point to for **A percentage without the numbers**. Yet this case is **Too few to trust**.

**You are asked:** This looks like **A percentage without the numbers**. Before you read why it is **Too few to trust**, tap the words in the case that settle it.

The pieces you can tap:
1. “A shop owner posts”
2. “Shoplifting at my store is up 300% this month!”
3. “one theft last month and four this month”

**Shown as soon as you tap.** The words are “one theft last month and four this month”.
- If you tapped “A shop owner posts”: That tells you who is speaking. It is not the figure.
- If you tapped “Shoplifting at my store is up 300% this month!”: That is the percentage, and it is the part that catches the eye. A percentage with no counts would be one thing. Here the counts are given in the next sentence, and they are what the case turns on.

**Why this is Too few to trust and not A percentage without the numbers**

Ask what the percentage is built on. The log gives it: one theft last month and four this month. The sum is right: 3 more on 1 is 300%. But with so few, one theft more or fewer moves the percentage a long way. If next month there are two thefts, the same owner could post "down 50%", and nothing about shoplifting would have changed.

So the answer is **“Who was counted”**, and the name is **Too few to trust**. The questions ask about the people or things in the figure before they ask what the figure is set beside, because everything after rests on them.

**How to tell them apart**

Can you find the two counts behind the percentage? If you can, are they so small that one more or one fewer would change the percentage a long way?

When a case shows both **“What it is compared with”** and people or things that are not a fair picture of the group, or too few to trust, the answer is **“Who was counted”**.

### 13. The question you have been answering all along

*Unit Five · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 13 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-compare`]

This card puts the question and its three answers in one place, as they are always asked, and says why it is asked.

**The question:** **“What would you need to see to read the figure fairly?”**

**What it is for.** Tells apart three things a claim can leave out that a figure has to be set beside before it means anything.

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other two.

- **“The numbers behind the percentage”**
  - Give this answer when a change or a risk is given only as a percentage of what it was (up 300%, risk cut by half, 18% higher), with no word on how many it was before and after.
  - It leads to **A percentage without the numbers**.
- **“How common the thing is to begin with”**
  - Give this answer when a test’s or an alarm’s accuracy is read as the chance that its yes is right, and the claim leaves out, or does not use, how rare the thing is among the people tested.
  - It leads to **Base rate fallacy**.
- **“The totals split back into their groups”**
  - Give this answer when two totals (two hospitals, two tutors, two shops) are set side by side as if they dealt with the same kind of case, and the case shows that each deals with a different mix of easy and hard ones.
  - It leads to **Simpson’s paradox**.

**Why it decides**

The three names are defined by what is missing beside the figure. Each makes the figure look bigger, surer or fairer than it is, and each is put right by a different piece of arithmetic.

Each of the three names is a different thing missing from beside a figure. **A percentage without the numbers** needs the counts before and after, **Base rate fallacy** needs how common the thing is, and **Simpson’s paradox** needs each total split by kind. Asking for the wrong one gets you nowhere: the counts behind a percentage do not help you read a test, and the split of two totals does not help you read a percentage. So the answers are things to ask for: "out of how many?", "how common is it?", "what is each total made of?".

**How to answer it from a case**

Read the whole claim, the last sentence included, because the words that show the mix or the rare thing are often last. Whichever answer you give, point to the words that show it. If you cannot point, you do not have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Some claims look as if they need two of these, or as if nothing is missing at all. Each pair below has been set side by side in this unit, and each has one question that tells it apart.

- A percentage without the numbers or Base rate fallacy: Is the figure a change or a risk, or is it how often a test is right? For a change or a risk, ask for the counts before and after. For how often a test is right, ask how common the thing is among the people tested.
- Base rate fallacy or Simpson’s paradox: Is the figure how often a test is right, or two totals set side by side? For a test, ask how common the thing is among the people tested. For two totals, ask what mix of easy and hard ones each total is made of.
- A percentage without the numbers or Simpson’s paradox: Are the counts behind the figures missing, or are they there, with each total hiding a different mix of easy and hard ones?

*The county report*

> A county report says: 'Deaths after heart surgery are 20% lower at St. Mark's than at County General: 120 deaths in 1,000 operations at St. Mark's, against 150 in 1,000 at County General.' The report adds that County General is the only hospital in the county that operates on the sickest patients, and that St. Mark's turns most of them away.

**Question 1 of 2: Which part of the claim goes wrong first?**

What it is for: sorts a claim made with numbers by the first of its four parts that goes wrong, in the order a claim is put together, or finds that none of them does. A claim made with numbers is put together in order. Some people or things are counted; the figure from them stands for something; it is set beside something else to give it meaning; and it may be said to show that one thing caused another. Each part rests on the ones before it, so the first part that goes wrong spoils everything built on it, and what you ask next depends on which part that is. A claim in which no part goes wrong holds, as far as it goes.

> A county report says: '⟦Deaths after heart surgery are 20% lower at St. Mark's than at County General⟧: 120 deaths in 1,000 operations at St. Mark's, against 150 in 1,000 at County General.' The report adds that County General is the only hospital in the county that operates on the sickest patients, and that St. Mark's turns most of them away.

Answer: **“What it is compared with”**

Take the parts in order. The people or things in the figure: 1,000 operations at each hospital, all of them counted. That part holds. What the figure counts: deaths after heart surgery, counted the same way at both. That part holds.

Next, what the figure is set beside. The claim gives a percentage, “Deaths after heart surgery are 20% lower at St. Mark's than at County General”, and two totals set side by side as a ranking. It does give the counts, but the case goes on to say that the two hospitals do not operate on the same patients, and a ranking that leaves out what each total is made of leaves out something you would need beside it. The answer is **“What it is compared with”**.

Still possible: all three names this unit teaches.

**Question 2 of 2: What would you need to see to read the figure fairly?**

What it is for: tells apart three things a claim can leave out that a figure has to be set beside before it means anything. The three names are defined by what is missing beside the figure. Each makes the figure look bigger, surer or fairer than it is, and each is put right by a different piece of arithmetic.

> A county report says: 'Deaths after heart surgery are 20% lower at St. Mark's than at County General: 120 deaths in 1,000 operations at St. Mark's, against 150 in 1,000 at County General.' The report adds that ⟦County General is the only hospital in the county that operates on the sickest patients, and that St. Mark's turns most of them away⟧.

Answer: **“The totals split back into their groups”**

Now the question is what you would need to see. Two totals are set side by side, and the case says this: “County General is the only hospital in the county that operates on the sickest patients, and that St. Mark's turns most of them away”. So the totals are made of very different mixes of patients, and ranking them as if they were alike is unfair to the hospital that takes the hard ones.

The claim does not give the split, so here is one that fits what it says. County General operated on 900 high-risk patients, with 144 deaths (16 in every 100), and on 100 lower-risk patients, with 6 deaths (6 in every 100): 150 deaths in all. St. Mark’s operated on 200 high-risk patients, with 40 deaths (20 in every 100), and on 800 lower-risk patients, with 80 deaths (10 in every 100): 120 deaths in all. County General has the lower death rate with both kinds of patient and the higher total, because nine in ten of its patients were high-risk.

What you would need is each total split into its groups, so the answer is **“The totals split back into their groups”**.

Still possible: **Simpson’s paradox**. Ruled out: **A percentage without the numbers** and **Base rate fallacy**.

**Name it:** **Simpson’s paradox**

**You are asked:** The report says "20% lower", so the opening can look like a percentage given without the numbers. Why is this **Simpson’s paradox** and not **A percentage without the numbers**? Every statement below is true of the case. Before you read the reason, choose the one that settles it.

- (a) The report says that deaths are 20% lower at St. Mark’s.
- (b) County General is the only hospital that operates on the sickest patients, and St. Mark’s turns most of them away.
- (c) The report counts deaths the same way at both hospitals.

**Shown as soon as you choose.** The one that settles it is (b): County General is the only hospital that operates on the sickest patients, and St. Mark’s turns most of them away.
- If you chose (a): True, and it is why the case can look like **A percentage without the numbers**. But the report gives the counts as well: 120 in 1,000 and 150 in 1,000. A percentage with its counts leaves out nothing about how many.
- If you chose (c): True, and it tells you that what is counted holds. It does not separate the two answers: a total with a hidden mix and a total with none can both be counted the same way.

**Why this is Simpson’s paradox and not A percentage without the numbers**

For **A percentage without the numbers** you must be able to point to this: a change or a risk given as a percentage of what it was, and no word on how many it was before and after. The report gives the percentage and it also gives the counts, so nothing is missing about how many. For **Simpson’s paradox** you must be able to point to this: two totals set side by side, each made of easier and harder cases (mild and severe illness, strong and weak students), the hard ones a much bigger share of one total than of the other, and the totals read as a fair ranking. The case shows two totals, and each hospital deals with a very different mix.

Are the counts behind the figures missing, or are they there, with each total hiding a different mix of easy and hard ones? Here the counts are there, and what is missing is what each total is made of, so the answer is **“The totals split back into their groups”**.

**Does it look like a case you know?**

Now the second look: does this case look like one you know? A percentage that says how much lower, "20% lower", may bring back the jogging headline first, and that was **“The numbers behind the percentage”**. So here the likeness and the answer seem to disagree.

When that happens, go back to the question and find the words in the case that answer it. They are “County General is the only hospital in the county that operates on the sickest patients, and that St. Mark's turns most of them away”. The jogging headline had no counts and nothing like these words. This case has the counts, and it shows the two hospitals dealing with different patients. So the case it really looks like is the two tutors, where one took the students who were already failing, and the answer stands.

### 16. What to carry away

*Unit Five · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 16 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap-compare`]

You have now run the question on your own. This card puts the unit in one place.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What would you need to see to read the figure fairly?
- The numbers behind the percentage → A percentage without the numbers
- How common the thing is to begin with → Base rate fallacy
- The totals split back into their groups → Simpson’s paradox

**For each name: what you must be able to point to, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **A percentage without the numbers**: a change or a risk given as a percentage of what it was, and no word on how many it was before and after.
- **Base rate fallacy**: a test or an alarm that is right most of the time, a yes from it, the chance that the yes is right read straight off its accuracy, and the thing being rare among the people tested.
- **Simpson’s paradox**: two totals set side by side, each made of easier and harder cases (mild and severe illness, strong and weak students), the hard ones a much bigger share of one total than of the other, and the totals read as a fair ranking.

**To carry away**

- A figure needs something beside it. Ask what has to stand beside it, and point to the words that show your answer. If you cannot point, you do not have an answer yet.
- A percentage is a share of something the claim has not told you. Ask for the counts before and after: the same percentage can be 2 more people in every 1,000 or 10 more in every 100.
- A test that is right 99 times in 100 does not make a yes right 99 times in 100. Count it out for 10,000 people: how many have the thing, how many the test catches, how many it flags by mistake. The rarer the thing, the more of the yeses are each a *false alarm*.
- Two totals set side by side are a fair ranking only when each is made of the same mix of easy and hard ones. Otherwise split each total by kind and compare kind by kind. The better one can have the lower total.
- A claim that comes with the counts, the split and a fair thing to set the figure beside is **A fair comparison**. When a headline percentage rests on a handful, the answer is **Too few to trust**.
