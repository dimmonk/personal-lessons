## Part 1 of 2: Three things a figure can leave out

### 1. Before you trust a percentage, a test result or a ranking, check what is missing

*Unit Five · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient-compare`]

Before you share an ad that says “cuts your risk by 50%”, a poster that says “99% accurate” or a ranking that says “Hospital A is better”, check what is missing beside the figure. It is always one of three things, and you can learn to spot each.

An ad says, 'Cuts your risk by 50%.' A poster says, 'This test is 99% accurate.' A ranking says, 'Hospital A saves 90 of every 100 patients, and Hospital B only 80.' Each figure is true as far as it goes, and each leaves out something you need before you can tell what it means.

A figure on its own is like a length with no unit. '50%' is half of something, and the ad does not say what. '99% accurate' says how often the test is right, but what you want to know is how likely it is that you have the thing when the test says yes. A ranking sets two totals side by side, and a total can hide what is inside it.

Two words are used all the way through. A count is an actual number of people or things, such as 3 out of 40. A percentage is a count turned into a share of 100, and it cannot give the count back: 3 out of 40 and 30 out of 400 are both 7.5%.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“Where does this claim first go wrong?”** Its answers:

- **“Who was counted”**: give this answer when the people or things the figure came from do not match the group the claim is about, or are too few to trust.
- **“What the number counts”**: give this answer when the figure could rise, fall or differ without the real thing it is taken to show doing the same.
- **“What it is compared with”**: give this answer when the figure is a percentage change, a test’s accuracy, or a total beside another total, and the claim leaves out what you need beside it to read it fairly. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“What caused what”**: give this answer when the claim says one thing made another happen, and the same result could have come about another way.
- **“Nothing goes wrong”**: give this answer when every part the claim uses checks out in order, and none of them goes wrong.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

- How many it was, before and after → A percentage without the numbers

### 2. A percentage without the numbers

*Unit Five · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-relrisk`]

Start with the one you see most often: a headline that gives a percentage and nothing else.

*The jogging headline*

> A health website runs the headline '⟦Joggers are 40% more likely to hurt an ankle⟧.' The article does not say how many joggers were hurt, how many people it looked at, or what the 40% is more than.

“40% more likely” means the old chance, plus 40% of the old chance. The headline hides the old chance, and the old chance is what tells you how big the news is.

Say 5 in every 1,000 people who don’t jog hurt an ankle in a year. 40% more is 7 in 1,000: 2 more people in every 1,000. Now say 25 in every 100 do. 40% more is 35 in 100: 10 more in every 100. The headline is true both times, and the news is very different.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the percentage: “40% more likely”.** A change given only as a share is the sign of this one.
2. **Ask what the 40% is 40% of: how likely an ankle injury was before.** The headline never says, and the size of the news depends on it.
3. **Look for the two counts: how many were hurt, out of how many people, before and after.** Without them you cannot tell 2 more in 1,000 from 10 more in 100.

This is **A percentage without the numbers**. The percentage is usually right. What is missing is the counts it was worked out from.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Ask “Out of how many, before and after?”** The same percentage can be a tiny change or a huge one.
2. **Look for the two counts in the report behind the headline, or in the small print.** They are often in the study even when the headline leaves them out.
3. **If you can’t find them, say so before you share it: “Up 40%, but from what?”** Then you are not passing on a figure that may mean almost nothing.

You may also hear this called “relative risk” or “a big percentage of a small number”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **A percentage without the numbers**.

### 4. A test that says yes when the answer is no

*Unit Five · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-falsealarm`]

The next idea rests on a word you may not have met. Start with something you probably have.

*The toast alarm*

> Priya's smoke alarm goes off every time she makes toast. Each time she opens a window, and each time there turns out to be no fire.

Priya’s smoke alarm said “fire” and there was no fire. The alarm isn’t broken. It is built never to sleep through a real fire, and the price is that it sometimes goes off for toast or steam.

**The word for this.** *False alarm*: a test or an alarm saying yes when the thing it looks for is not there.

Almost every test or alarm works this way: a medical test, a scanner at an entrance, a fraud alert on a card payment. Each sometimes says yes when the answer is no. Any figure about a test has to be read with that in mind.


### 5. Base rate fallacy

*Unit Five · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 5 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-baserate`]

The first name was about a change. This one is about a test: a poster says how often a test is right, then reads a yes from it.

*The skin test poster*

> A clinic's poster says: 'Our new test for a skin condition is 99% accurate. ⟦If your test says yes, you almost certainly have it⟧.' About 1 person in every 100 has the condition. The poster does not mention that.

“99% accurate” and “if your test says yes, you almost certainly have it” are two different numbers. To get from the first to the second you need a third that the poster leaves out: how common the condition is among the people tested.

Picture 10,000 people. 100 have the condition, and the test says yes to 99 of them. The other 9,900 don’t have it, and the test wrongly says yes to 1 in every 100 of them: 99 people, each a *false alarm*. That makes 198 yeses, and only 99 are right: half, not 99%.

Nothing is wrong with the test. What goes wrong is reading a yes without knowing how rare the condition is.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the test and how accurate it is: “99% accurate”.** That is how often the test is right, and nothing more.
2. **Find the yes being read as proof: “you almost certainly have it”.** The poster treats “right 99 times in 100” as “99% sure”.
3. **Ask how common the condition is among the people tested: 1 in 100.** The rarer it is, the bigger the share of yeses that are wrong.
4. **Count it out for 10,000 people: the right yeses against all the yeses.** Here 99 right yeses out of 198 means a yes is right half the time.

This is **Base rate fallacy**. The “base rate” is how common the thing is before anyone is tested, and the mistake is leaving it out.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Find out how common the thing is among the people tested.** The rarer it is, the less a yes means.
2. **Count it out for 10,000 people: the right yeses divided by all the yeses.** That gives the real chance that a yes is right.
3. **If the result matters to you, ask for a second test before you decide anything.** A second yes tells you far more than the first.

You may also hear this called “base-rate neglect” or “the false positive paradox” or “ignoring how common it is”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Base rate fallacy**.

### 7. Simpson’s paradox

*Unit Five · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-simpson`]

The first two names were about one figure. This one is about two totals set side by side, and what is inside each.

*The two tutors*

> A tutoring website compares two math tutors. It lists: 'Ms. Hale: 75 of her 100 students passed the exam. Mr. Ruiz: 54 of his 100 students passed.' It tells families to choose Ms. Hale. The site's own records show that ⟦Ms. Hale's students were mostly ones who were already doing well, and Mr. Ruiz's were mostly ones who were already failing⟧.

Ms. Hale looks far better, but look inside the totals. Ms. Hale taught 90 students who were already doing well (72 passed, 80 in every 100) and 10 who were already failing (3 passed, 30 in every 100). Mr. Ruiz taught 10 who were doing well (9 passed, 90 in every 100) and 90 who were failing (45 passed, 50 in every 100).

Group by group, Mr. Ruiz does better with both: 90 against 80, and 50 against 30. He has the lower total only because 90 of his 100 students were the kind who rarely pass. The totals show who each tutor had more than how well each taught.

So the totals can’t rank the tutors: they didn’t teach the same mix of students.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the two totals set side by side: 75 of 100 against 54 of 100.** A ranking built on totals is where this goes wrong.
2. **Ask whether each side had the same mix: Ms. Hale’s students were already doing well, Mr. Ruiz’s were already failing.** Students who are doing well pass more often under any tutor.
3. **Split each total by group and compare like with like: doing well against doing well, failing against failing.** A fair ranking sets easy against easy and hard against hard.

This is **Simpson’s paradox**. A “paradox” is something that seems to contradict itself, and here one tutor does better with every group and still has the lower total.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Ask who or what is inside each total.** A total hides how many easy and hard ones it holds.
2. **Look for the totals split by group, easy against easy and hard against hard.** That is the only fair way to rank them.
3. **If you can’t get the split, say so: “Better overall, but is it the same mix?”** That one question is enough to slow a bad ranking down.

You may also hear this called “totals that hide the groups”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Simpson’s paradox**.

## Part 2 of 2: Telling them apart, then the drill

### 9. A percentage without the numbers or A fair comparison: telling them apart

*Unit Five · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 9 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-relrisk-compok`]

The same percentage can appear in a claim that holds up and in one that doesn’t. Here are two claims about the same bus lines.

**Story A**

> The city transit blog says: 'Riders on Line 12 are 50% more likely to arrive late than riders on Line 9.' The post gives no counts.

**Story B**

> The city transit blog says: 'Riders on Line 12 are more likely to arrive late than riders on Line 9.' In March, 15 of 300 trips arrived late on Line 12 and 10 of 300 trips on Line 9. Both lines are weekday commuter lines of the same length, and every trip was timed the same way.

**What to compare.** Both claims are about Line 12 and Line 9, and in both Line 12 comes out likelier to be late. Compare one thing: whether you can find the counts the percentage came from.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Nothing goes wrong”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the claim gives “50% more likely” and no counts. It could be 4 late trips in every 100 against 6, or 40 against 60, and the claim doesn’t let you tell which. That is **A percentage without the numbers**.

In Story B the counts are given: 15 of 300 trips late on Line 12 and 10 of 300 on Line 9. 15 is 10 plus half of 10, so the same 50% now has the numbers behind it, and both lines were timed the same way. That is **A fair comparison**, and the answer is **“Nothing goes wrong”**.

The percentage is the same in both. What differs is whether the counts are beside it.

**How to tell them apart**

Can you find the two counts the percentage was worked out from, each out of its own total? If you can, a percentage beside them is fine. If you cannot, the percentage is all you have.


### 10. Base rate fallacy or A fair comparison: telling them apart

*Unit Five · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 10 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-baserate-compok`]

Results from the same test can be reported in a way that holds up. Here is one health office telling it two ways.

**Story A**

> A city health office says: 'Our home test for Rudd fever is 98% accurate, so if yours says yes, you very likely have it.' About 1 person in 200 has Rudd fever.

**Story B**

> The same health office reports on 10,000 people who took the home test for Rudd fever: 'People who test yes are far likelier to have Rudd fever than people who test no. Of the 248 who tested yes, 49 had it. Of the 9,752 who tested no, 1 had it.' Everyone took the same test in the same month.

**What to compare.** Both claims are about the same home test for Rudd fever. The test is 98% accurate, and about 1 person in 200 has Rudd fever. Compare one thing: whether the claim keeps in mind how rare the fever is when it reads a yes.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Nothing goes wrong”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the claim gives the accuracy, 98%, and says a yes is “very likely” right. Out of 10,000 people, 50 have the fever and the test says yes to 49 of them. Of the 9,950 who don’t, it says yes to 2 in every 100: 199. So 49 of the 248 yeses are right: about 1 in 5, not 98 in 100. That is **Base rate fallacy**.

In Story B the claim gives those same counts and reads them as they are: of the 248 who tested yes, 49 had it, and of the 9,752 who tested no, 1 had it. It never says a yes is nearly certain. That is **A fair comparison**, and the answer is **“Nothing goes wrong”**.

The test and the people are the same. What differs is whether the reading of a yes uses how rare the fever is.

**How to tell them apart**

Does the claim tell you how rare the thing is among the people tested, and does it read a yes with that in view?


### 11. Simpson’s paradox or A fair comparison: telling them apart

*Unit Five · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 11 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-simpson-compok`]

Two totals side by side are fine when both sides are alike. Here are two newsletters about the same two coaches.

**Story A**

> A swim club's newsletter says: 'Coach Dana's swimmers: 80 of 100 improved their time this season. Coach Eli's: 60 of 100.' It names Dana the better coach. Dana's swimmers are almost all beginners, who improve fast. Eli's are almost all national-level swimmers, who improve slowly.

**Story B**

> A swim club's newsletter says: 'Coach Dana's swimmers: 80 of 100 improved their time this season. Coach Eli's: 60 of 100.' Each coach has 50 beginners and 50 national-level swimmers, and every swimmer was timed the same way in the same pool.

**What to compare.** Both newsletters give the same two totals, 80 of 100 and 60 of 100, and both call Dana the better coach. Compare one thing: whether each coach has the same mix of beginners and national-level swimmers.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“What it is compared with”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story A.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A Dana’s swimmers are almost all beginners and Eli’s are almost all national-level. Beginners improve fast and national-level swimmers improve slowly, so Dana’s total is high partly because of who she coaches, and the totals can’t say whether she coaches better. That is **Simpson’s paradox**.

In Story B each coach has 50 beginners and 50 national-level swimmers, timed the same way. Both totals hold the same mix, so you can set them side by side as they are. That is **A fair comparison**, and the answer is **“Nothing goes wrong”**.

The totals are the same in both. A ranking by totals is fair only when both sides hold the same mix.

**How to tell them apart**

Does each total hold the same mix of easy and hard ones, or does one hold far more of the hard ones than the other?


### 12. A headline percentage built on a handful

*Unit Five · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 12 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `exception`, id `exc-handful`]

Some claims give a big percentage and the counts too. This one shows what changes when the counts are given and they are tiny.

*The shop owner's 300%*

> A shop owner posts: 'Shoplifting at my store is up 300% this month!' The store's own log shows one theft last month and four this month.

The owner’s post gives a percentage, up 300%, which is how **A percentage without the numbers** looks. But this is **Too few to trust**.

**You are asked:** This looks like **A percentage without the numbers**. Before you read why it is **Too few to trust**, tap the words in the story that settle it.

The pieces you can tap:
1. “A shop owner posts”
2. “Shoplifting at my store is up 300% this month!”
3. “one theft last month and four this month”

**Shown as soon as you tap.** The words are “one theft last month and four this month”.
- If you tapped “A shop owner posts”: That only tells you who is speaking.
- If you tapped “Shoplifting at my store is up 300% this month!”: That is the percentage, the part that catches the eye. The counts that decide this are in the next sentence.

**Why this is Too few to trust and not A percentage without the numbers**

Ask what the percentage is built on. The log tells you: one theft last month and four this month. The sum is right, 3 more on 1 is 300%, but with so few, one theft more or fewer moves the percentage a long way. If next month there are two thefts, the same owner could post “down 50%”, and nothing about shoplifting would have changed.

So the answer is **“Who was counted”**, and the name is **Too few to trust**. Check how many are behind a figure before you ask what it is set beside, because everything after rests on that.

**How to tell them apart**

Can you find the two counts behind the percentage? If you can, are they so small that one more or one fewer would change the percentage a long way?

When a story shows both **“What it is compared with”** and the wrong people or things counted, or too few to trust, the answer is **“Who was counted”**.


### 13. The one question to ask about a figure

*Unit Five · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 13 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-compare`]

Here is the question and its three answers in one place.

**The question:** **“What do you need to see beside this figure?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other two.

- **“How many it was, before and after”**
  - Give this answer when a change or a risk is given only as a percentage (up 300%, risk cut in half, 18% higher), with no word on how many it was before and after.
  - It leads to **A percentage without the numbers**.
- **“How common the thing is to begin with”**
  - Give this answer when a test’s or alarm’s accuracy is taken as the chance its yes is right, and the claim leaves out or ignores how rare the thing is among those tested.
  - It leads to **Base rate fallacy**.
- **“The totals split into easy and hard ones”**
  - Give this answer when two totals (two hospitals, two tutors, two stores) are set side by side as if they handled the same mix, but each handles a different mix of easy and hard ones.
  - It leads to **Simpson’s paradox**.

**Why it matters**

The three names differ by what is missing beside the figure. Each makes the figure look bigger, surer or fairer than it is, and each is fixed by a different bit of arithmetic.

Each name is a different thing missing from beside a figure, and each needs a different ask. For **A percentage without the numbers**, ask “Out of how many?” For **Base rate fallacy**, ask “How common is it?” For **Simpson’s paradox**, ask “What is inside each total?”

Asking the wrong one gets you nowhere. The counts behind a percentage don’t help you read a test, and the split of two totals doesn’t help you read a percentage.

**How to answer it**

1. **Read the whole claim, the last sentence included.** The words that show the mix or the rare thing are often last.
2. **Find the figure: a percentage change, a test’s accuracy, or two totals.** That tells you which of the three to look for.
3. **Ask the matching question: out of how many, how common is it, or what is inside each total.** Each name is fixed by a different ask.
4. **Find the exact words that show your answer.** If you can’t find them, you don’t have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Some claims look as if they need two of these, or as if nothing is missing at all. Each pair below has one question that tells it apart.

- A percentage without the numbers or Base rate fallacy: Is the figure a change or a risk, or is it how often a test is right? For a change or a risk, ask for the counts before and after. For a test, ask how rare the thing is among the people tested.
- Base rate fallacy or Simpson’s paradox: Is the figure how often a test is right, or two totals side by side? For a test, ask how rare the thing is among the people tested. For two totals, ask how many easy and hard ones are inside each.
- A percentage without the numbers or Simpson’s paradox: Are the counts behind the figures missing, or are they there, with each total hiding a different mix of easy and hard ones?

### 15. One whole claim, where the start points the wrong way

**Question 1 of 2: Where does this claim first go wrong?**

> A county report says: '⟦Deaths after heart surgery are 20% lower at St. Mark's than at County General⟧: 120 deaths in 1,000 operations at St. Mark's, against 150 in 1,000 at County General.' The report adds that County General is the only hospital in the county that operates on the sickest patients, and that St. Mark's turns most of them away.

Answer: **“What it is compared with”**

Take the parts in order. Who is counted: 1,000 operations at each hospital, all of them. That part holds. What is counted: deaths after heart surgery, counted the same way at both. That part holds too.

Next, what the figure is set beside. The claim gives a percentage, “Deaths after heart surgery are 20% lower at St. Mark's than at County General”, and sets two totals side by side as a ranking. **Question 2 of 2: What do you need to see beside this figure?**

> A county report says: 'Deaths after heart surgery are 20% lower at St. Mark's than at County General: 120 deaths in 1,000 operations at St. Mark's, against 150 in 1,000 at County General.' The report adds that ⟦County General is the only hospital in the county that operates on the sickest patients, and that St. Mark's turns most of them away⟧.

Answer: **“The totals split into easy and hard ones”**

Now, what would you need to see? Two totals are set side by side, and the story says this: “County General is the only hospital in the county that operates on the sickest patients, and that St. Mark's turns most of them away”. So the totals hold very different mixes of patients, and a ranking that treats them as alike is unfair to the hospital that takes the hard ones.

The claim doesn’t give the split, so here is one that fits what it says. County General operated on 900 high-risk patients, with 144 deaths (16 in every 100), and on 100 lower-risk patients, with 6 deaths (6 in every 100): 150 deaths in all. St. Mark’s operated on 200 high-risk patients, with 40 deaths (20 in every 100), and on 800 lower-risk patients, with 80 deaths (10 in every 100): 120 deaths in all.

County General has the lower death rate with both groups and the higher total, because nine in ten of its patients were high-risk. What you would need is each total split into its groups, so the answer is **“The totals split into easy and hard ones”**.

Still possible: **Simpson’s paradox**. Ruled out: **A percentage without the numbers** and **Base rate fallacy**.

**Name it:** **Simpson’s paradox**

**You are asked:** The report says “20% lower”, so it can look like **A percentage without the numbers**. What decides it? All of these are true. Choose the one that settles it.

- (a) The report says deaths are 20% lower at St. Mark’s.
- (b) County General takes the sickest patients, and St. Mark’s turns most of them away.
- (c) The report counts deaths the same way at both hospitals.

**Shown as soon as you choose.** The one that settles it is (b): County General takes the sickest patients, and St. Mark’s turns most of them away.
- If you chose (a): True, and it is why this looks like **A percentage without the numbers**. But the report gives the counts too, 120 in 1,000 and 150 in 1,000, so nothing is missing about how many.
- If you chose (c): True, but a total that hides a mix and a total that does not can both be counted the same way.

**Why this is Simpson’s paradox and not A percentage without the numbers**

A percentage with no counts would be **A percentage without the numbers**, but this report gives the counts too: 120 in 1,000 and 150 in 1,000. Two totals that hide different mixes are **Simpson’s paradox**, and this report says the two hospitals take very different patients.

Are the counts behind the figures missing, or are they there, with each total hiding a different mix of easy and hard ones? Here the counts are there, and what is missing is what is inside each total, so the answer is **“The totals split into easy and hard ones”**.

**Does it look like a story you know?**

### 16. What to carry away

*Unit Five · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 16 of 17*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap-compare`]

You have now run the question on your own. Here is the unit in one place.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What do you need to see beside this figure?
- How many it was, before and after → A percentage without the numbers
- How common the thing is to begin with → Base rate fallacy
- The totals split into easy and hard ones → Simpson’s paradox

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **A percentage without the numbers**: a change or a risk given only as a percentage (up 50%, risk cut in half), and no word on how many it was before and after.
- **Base rate fallacy**: a yes from a test or alarm that is right most of the time, that yes taken as just as likely right as the test is accurate, and the thing rare among those tested.
- **Simpson’s paradox**: two totals side by side, each a mix of easy and hard ones, like mild and severe illness, far more hard ones in one, and the totals taken as a fair ranking.

**To carry away**

- A figure needs something beside it. Ask what has to stand beside it, and find the words that show your answer. If you can’t find them, you don’t have an answer yet.
- A percentage is a share of something the claim hasn’t told you. Ask for the counts before and after: the same percentage can be 2 more people in every 1,000 or 10 more in every 100.
- A test that is right 99 times in 100 doesn’t make a yes right 99 times in 100. Count it out for 10,000 people: how many have the thing, how many the test catches, how many it flags by mistake. The rarer the thing, the more of the yeses are each a *false alarm*.
- Two totals side by side are a fair ranking only when each holds the same mix of easy and hard ones. Otherwise split each total by group and compare group by group. The better one can have the lower total.
- A claim that comes with the counts, the split and a fair thing to set the figure beside is **A fair comparison**. A headline percentage built on a handful is **Too few to trust**.
