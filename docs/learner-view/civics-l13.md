# US Civics & History: 1877 to 1900

Revision 1, live. Part: 1877 to 1900. Where the subject ends up: Answer the citizenship interview’s civics questions aloud from memory, and, reading a news story about a US law, order or ruling, say who acted, whether it was theirs to do, who can stop it, and whether to take the story as it stands or check who is behind it first.

## The why

The list’s questions on the 1800s stop with the Civil War and its amendments, and its next ones start in the 1900s. The years between still decide things today: a 2026 Supreme Court ruling on citizenship rested on one from 1898.
When a decision of today leans on the past, find what it rests on, put that in its time, and say what led to it.

## Step 1: Who could vote, and who could be a citizen

A plain table, "After the Civil War":

> When  |  What happened  (the column heads)
> After the Civil War  |  Some southern states made voters pay a poll tax or pass a reading test (a literacy test). These kept many Black citizens from voting.
> 1882  |  The Chinese Exclusion Act bans Chinese laborers for ten years. Behind it: other workers resented them as rivals for jobs, and politicians from the West pressed for it. (The law’s own words say only that they endanger “good order.”)
> 1898  |  The government held Wong Kim Ark, born here to Chinese parents, as no citizen under the Chinese Exclusion Act. In United States v. Wong Kim Ark the Supreme Court says the 14th Amendment makes him one.

A plain table, "What ended those rules, later":

> When  |  What happened  (the column heads)
> 1964  |  The 24th Amendment ends poll taxes in elections for president and Congress.
> 1965  |  The Voting Rights Act outlaws literacy tests for voters.
> 1966  |  A Supreme Court ruling ends any fee to vote in state elections too.

## Step 2: Train cars, a statue and a war

A plain table, "Three more stories of these years":

> When  |  What happened  (the column heads)
> 1886  |  The Statue of Liberty is dedicated: a gift from France, proposed to honor the Union’s win and the end of slavery.
> 1890 and 1896  |  A Louisiana law keeps Black and white train passengers in separate cars. In Plessy v. Ferguson (1896) the Supreme Court upholds it. Brown v. Board of Education and the civil rights acts of the 1950s and 1960s end such rules.
> 1898  |  After three years of Cuban fighting against Spain, the U.S. battleship Maine sinks in Havana harbor, and the Spanish-American War follows.

Also from these years: the light bulb and skyscrapers, two answers of the list.

## Step 3: A wrong idea

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “All men got the vote in 1870, so from then on every man voted.”
The right line: That was the law on paper. Poll taxes and literacy tests kept many Black citizens from voting until the 1960s.

## Step 4: When a decision leans on the past

A plain table, "Two questions to put to the story":

> Question  |  How  (the column heads)
> What does the decision rest on?  |  A story may name several dates and laws. Find the one the decision follows or relies on, not one that only says when something was built or changed.
> What led to it? Test 1  |  A cause comes before what it causes. Anything from a later year cannot be the cause.
> What led to it? Test 2  |  A cause is about the same thing. A ruling on citizenship answers something about citizenship, not about voting or train cars.

## Step 5: A worked example

A news story, from "The Capital Sun":

> # Court strikes down order on citizenship at birth
>
> On June 30, the Supreme Court struck down an order of the president about who is a citizen at birth. The order said some children born here would not be citizens if their parents were here for a short time or without permission. The court said the 14th Amendment makes those children citizens, as the court had said in 1898, in United States v. Wong Kim Ark. The court said that holds for nearly all children born in the United States.

The working, one step at a time:
1. Find what the decision rests on: One sentence says the court ruled “as the court had said in 1898”: the older ruling, United States v. Wong Kim Ark.
1. Put it in its time: 1898 is after the 14th Amendment of 1868, and after the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882.
1. Name what led to it, on the same subject: The subject is citizenship at birth. The government held Wong Kim Ark under the Chinese Exclusion Act and said he was not a citizen; the court said the 14th Amendment made him one.

Then the answer: The court said the 14th Amendment makes those children citizens, as the court had said in 1898, in United States v. Wong Kim Ark. / The Chinese Exclusion Act, under which a man born here was held as no citizen. The ruling rests on an 1898 one. In it, a man born here to Chinese parents living here was held by the government under the Chinese Exclusion Act, and the court said he was a citizen.
