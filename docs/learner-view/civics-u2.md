### 2. Four dates, and what happened in each

*Unit Two · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 61*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-date`]

The founding story in four dates.

*The timeline*

> A class is given four events on cards and has to put them in order. The Declaration of Independence is adopted. Delegates meet in Philadelphia to write a plan of government. The government under the plan begins. The Bill of Rights is added. One student says: ‘The first and the last are easy. But the two in the middle both sound like a plan of government being started, and I cannot tell which year goes with which.’

The student’s trouble is the middle two. Tie each year to one thing that happened in it.

In 1776 the colonies announced they were leaving Britain. That is the Declaration of Independence, adopted on July 4. It announced a break, but it set up no government.

The country’s first plan of government, the Articles of Confederation, left the government of the whole country almost powerless. So in 1787 delegates, people chosen to speak for their states, met in Philadelphia and wrote the Constitution to replace it. The government under it began in 1789. In 1791 the Bill of Rights was added.

1787 and 1789 are the pair people swap. Remember the order: the document is written first (1787), and the government starts working under it two years later (1789).

### 9. Which documents are law

*Unit Two · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 9 of 61*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-law`]

Can a court make anyone obey it?

*The neighborhood meeting*

> At a neighborhood meeting a man says: ‘The Declaration of Independence promises us the pursuit of happiness, so a court has to order the city to give me a better apartment.’ A woman answers: ‘A court cannot order that on the strength of the Declaration. It is not law.’ A third person asks: ‘Then what is law? Is the Constitution? And the Federalist Papers, the essays everybody quotes, are they law too?’ Nobody at the meeting is sure.

The woman is right: the Declaration is not law. Law means a court can order people to obey it. Some documents only explain or argue, and a court cannot order anything because of them.

The Constitution is law, and it is the highest law: it outranks every other law. In this unit “the Constitution” means the original text of 1787 plus every amendment added since. An amendment is a change added to the Constitution.

The Declaration of Independence, mainly written by Thomas Jefferson, is not law. It explains why the colonies were leaving Britain and sets up no government. No court can order anyone to give you “the pursuit of happiness” because of it.

The Federalist Papers are not law either. Alexander Hamilton, James Madison and John Jay wrote them as essays urging New York to approve the Constitution. A judge may quote one to see what the founders had in mind, but does not have to follow it.

### 15. What the Declaration of Independence says

*Unit Two · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 15 of 61*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-decl`]

The Declaration is not law, but it is quoted constantly.

*The voter*

> At a town meeting a woman is asked why she takes the trouble to vote. She says: ‘Nobody forced us to accept this government. It has power because we agree to be governed, so we get a say in who runs it.’ She adds: ‘And my rights are not something the government hands me. They cannot be taken away.’

The voter is repeating the two ideas from the Declaration that people still quote, even though it is not law.

The first is that people have unalienable rights, which means rights that cannot be taken away. It names three: life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness.

The second is that a government gets its power from the consent of the governed, which means from the people agreeing to be governed. That is why Americans vote: when power comes from the people’s agreement, the people get a say in who runs the government.

The rest of the Declaration is a long list of complaints against the king of Britain: the reasons it gives for leaving him.

### 24. The list of what Congress may do

*Unit Two · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 24 of 61*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-pow`]

Article I lists what Congress may do.

*The post office and the barber*

> Congress passes a law that sets up one postal service for the whole country. A reader asks where Congress gets the power to do that, and is shown Article I of the Constitution, which lists the power to establish post offices. Another reader asks about a different rule: how many hours of training a barber needs. Article I lists nothing about that, so it is not Congress’s to set. It belongs to the states.

Congress may set up a postal service, because Article I lists the power to establish post offices. Congress may not set how many hours a barber must train, because Article I says nothing about that. It belongs to the states.

The list is in Article I, Section 8. Congress may use only the powers on the list, plus one more: it may pass whatever laws are “necessary and proper” to carry out the listed powers. A power that is not on the list belongs to the states, or is something no government may do.

The list is long. The five below matter most: raising money, trade, the dollar and the mail, war and the armed forces, and the “necessary and proper” power. Courts argue all the time about how far each power reaches. This unit holds only what is on the list.


### 25. Five powers of Congress

*Unit Two · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 25 of 61*

[reviewers only: card kind `facts`, id `facts-pow`]

Congress may do only what the list gives it.

| The fact | The answer |
|---|---|
| What may Congress do to raise the money that the government spends? | Collect taxes, and borrow money |
| What may Congress do about trade? | Regulate trade with other countries and between the states |
| What may Congress do about the dollar and about the mail? | Coin money and run the post offices |
| What may Congress do about war and the armed forces? | Declare war, and raise and support armies and a navy |
| Besides the powers on the list, what else may Congress do? | Pass the laws “necessary and proper” to carry out its listed powers |

**How each fact fits the idea**

- **Collect taxes, and borrow money**: The first plan of government gave Congress no power to tax. It could only ask the states for money.
- **Regulate trade with other countries and between the states**: Import rules cover trade with other countries, and rules for goods shipped across state lines cover trade between the states. Another word for this trade is “commerce”.

## Part 2 of 2: The Bill of Rights and the later amendments, then the drill

### 31. The Bill of Rights is part of the Constitution

*Unit Two · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 31 of 61*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-bor`]

Added four years after the Constitution, and often mistaken for a separate document.

*The delegate who would not approve*

> Imagine a delegate from one of the states, after the Constitution has been written. He reads it and says: ‘It builds a government, and it says what that government may do. But nowhere does it promise, in writing, to protect a person’s rights. My state will not approve this Constitution until it does.’ Several states said the same.

The delegate’s complaint is why the Bill of Rights exists. The Constitution of 1787 set up a government and said what it could do, but it did not promise, in writing, to protect individual rights. Several states refused to approve it until it did. The answer, in 1791, was ten amendments, called the Bill of Rights.

So the Bill of Rights is not a separate document, and not a rival to the Constitution. It is the first ten amendments, so it is part of the Constitution, and it is law. People mix this up all the time. If one person says “the Constitution gives you the right to remain silent” and another says “no, that is the Bill of Rights”, both are right.

Most of it is written as limits on what the government may do to a person: “Congress shall make no law…”, “No person shall be…”.

### 35. Five amendments, and what each protects

*Unit Two · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 35 of 61*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-six`]

The ones that come up most: the number, and what it protects.

*The bag*

> A police officer stops a man on the street and wants to search his bag. The officer has no reason at all to think that the bag holds anything wrong. The man says: ‘That is an unreasonable search, and one of the amendments to the Constitution forbids it.’

The man with the bag is pointing at the Fourth Amendment without saying its number. The news uses the numbers: “the First Amendment”, “the Fifth”. So learn the number first, then what it protects. This unit covers the First, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth and Tenth, and not the other five.

A few words first. To petition is to ask the government to put right a wrong. A warrant is a judge’s written permission, which police generally need before a search. To testify is to say in court what you know. The Fifth says you cannot be forced to testify against yourself, which is called the right to remain silent. Due process means fair legal steps.

Three of the five protect a person who is in trouble with the law. A search is the Fourth, staying silent is the Fifth, and a lawyer or a jury is the Sixth. The First is about what you may freely say and do. The Tenth is about governments, not people: it says what happens to powers the Constitution does not give to the federal government.

### 43. How the Constitution is changed

*Unit Two · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 43 of 61*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-chg`]

How any change is made, and how many have been.

*Sam’s rule*

> Sam wants a new rule added to the Constitution. He asks his friend how many votes he would need. His friend says: ‘More than half is not enough. You would need far more than that, and from two different places, and one of the two is the states.’ Sam asks why it should be so hard. His friend says that it is meant to be.

Sam’s friend is right: it is meant to be hard. There are two steps. First, two-thirds of both chambers of Congress, the House of Representatives and the Senate, must vote to propose the change. Second, three-quarters of the states must approve it. The word for the states’ approval is “ratify”. A change made this way is an amendment, and it becomes part of the Constitution.

Because it is so hard, thousands of changes have been proposed and only twenty-seven adopted. Ten of those were added together in 1791: that is the Bill of Rights. Seventeen came after it.

### 49. The Fourteenth Amendment

*Unit Two · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 49 of 61*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-fth`]

The amendment that answers: does the Bill of Rights stop a state?

*The church permit*

> A state passes a law that no one may hold a religious service without a permit from the state, and then refuses a permit to one church. The church’s members say that the law breaks the First Amendment, which protects religion. A lawyer points out that the First Amendment begins ‘Congress shall make no law’. It names Congress, and this law was passed by a state. How can the First Amendment apply to it?

The lawyer asks a good question. The Bill of Rights was first written to limit only the federal government. The First Amendment begins “Congress shall make no law”, and it never mentions a state.

The answer is the Fourteenth Amendment, adopted in 1868, after the Civil War. It did three things.

First, anyone born in the United States is a citizen.

Second, no state may take a person’s life, liberty or property without due process, which means fair legal steps, or deny anyone equal protection of the laws, which means equal treatment in the same situation.

Third, the courts later read it to bring the limits in the Bill of Rights to the states, so that they protect you against your state and your city as well. That third one answers the lawyer.

### 61. What to carry away

*Unit Two · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 61 of 61*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap-const`]

Every fact in the unit, by group, and what to carry.

**The four dates**

- **In which year did the colonies announce that they were separating from Britain?** 1776
- **In which year did delegates meet in Philadelphia to write the Constitution?** 1787
- **In which year did the government under the Constitution begin?** 1789
- **In which year was the Bill of Rights added to the Constitution?** 1791

**Law, or not law**

- **Is the Declaration of Independence law?** Not law: it explains why the colonies were leaving Britain
- **Is the Constitution law?** Law: it is the supreme law of the land, above every other law
- **Are the Federalist Papers law?** Not law: they are essays urging people to approve the Constitution

**The two ideas**

- **Which rights does the Declaration say cannot be taken away?** Among them, life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness
- **Where does the Declaration say that a government gets its power?** From the consent of the governed: the people agreeing to be governed

**Three articles, three parts**

- **Which part of the government does Article I of the Constitution describe?** Congress
- **Which part of the government does Article II of the Constitution describe?** The President
- **Which part of the government does Article III of the Constitution describe?** The courts

**Five powers of Congress**

- **What may Congress do to raise the money that the government spends?** Collect taxes, and borrow money
- **What may Congress do about trade?** Regulate trade with other countries and between the states
- **What may Congress do about the dollar and about the mail?** Coin money and run the post offices
- **What may Congress do about war and the armed forces?** Declare war, and raise and support armies and a navy
- **Besides the powers on the list, what else may Congress do?** Pass the laws “necessary and proper” to carry out its listed powers

**Two facts about the Bill of Rights**

- **What is the Bill of Rights?** The first ten amendments, so it is part of the Constitution and is law
- **Why was the Bill of Rights added?** Several states refused to approve the Constitution without written protection for individual rights

**Five amendments and what each protects**

- **What does the First Amendment protect?** Freedom of speech, religion, the press, peaceful assembly, and petition
- **What does the Fourth Amendment protect?** No unreasonable searches or seizures; police generally need a warrant
- **What does the Fifth Amendment protect?** The right to remain silent, fair legal steps before punishment, and no second trial for the same crime
- **What does the Sixth Amendment protect?** A speedy, public jury trial, a lawyer, and the right to hear and question the witnesses against you
- **What does the Tenth Amendment say?** Powers not given to the federal government are kept by the states or the people

**Two fractions and a number**

- **What share of both chambers of Congress must vote to propose an amendment?** Two-thirds
- **What share of the states must approve a proposed amendment?** Three-quarters
- **How many amendments does the Constitution have so far?** Twenty-seven

**The three parts of the Fourteenth Amendment**

- **What does the Fourteenth Amendment make of anyone born in the United States?** A citizen of the United States
- **What does the Fourteenth Amendment require of a state that wants to take a person’s life, liberty or property?** Due process: fair legal steps
- **What does the Fourteenth Amendment forbid a state to deny anyone?** Equal protection of the laws: equal treatment in the same situation
- **What did the courts later read the Fourteenth Amendment to bring to the states?** The limits in the Bill of Rights, so they stop a state or a city too

**Three amendments, and what each did**

- **Which amendment ended slavery?** The Thirteenth Amendment
- **Which amendment says that the right to vote cannot be denied because of race?** The Fifteenth Amendment
- **Which amendment says that the right to vote cannot be denied because of sex?** The Nineteenth Amendment

**To carry away**

- The Constitution is law, the highest law in the country: the original text of 1787 plus every amendment added since, twenty-seven so far. The Bill of Rights is the first ten of those amendments, so it is part of the Constitution. The Declaration of Independence and the Federalist Papers are not law: one explains why the colonies left Britain, and the other argues for approving the Constitution.
- Hold each date with what happened in it: **1776** for the Declaration, **1787** for the Constitution being written, **1789** for the government under it beginning, and **1791** for the Bill of Rights.
- Congress has only the powers listed in Article I, plus the power to pass the laws needed to carry them out. A power that is not on the list belongs to the states, or is something no government may do.
- Changing the Constitution takes two steps: **Two-thirds** of both chambers of Congress must vote to propose the change, and **Three-quarters** of the states must approve it.
- The Bill of Rights first limited only the federal government. After the Civil War the courts read the Fourteenth Amendment to bring its limits to the states, so they stop a state or a city too.
