### 2. Four dates, and what happened in each

*Unit Two · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 61*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-date`]

The founding story in four dates.

*The timeline*

> A class is given four events on cards and has to put them in order. The Declaration of Independence is adopted. Delegates meet in Philadelphia to write a plan of government. The government under the plan begins. The Bill of Rights is added. One student says: ‘The first and the last are easy. But the two in the middle both sound like a plan of government being started, and I cannot tell which year goes with which.’

The student is right that the middle two sound alike. Hold each year together with what happened in it.

In 1776 the colonies announced that they were separating from Britain: the Declaration of Independence, adopted on July 4. It announced a break and set up no government. The country’s first plan of government, the Articles of Confederation, left the government of the whole country almost powerless, so in 1787 delegates, people chosen to speak for their states, met in Philadelphia and wrote the Constitution to replace it. The government under it began in 1789. In 1791 the Bill of Rights was added.

1787 and 1789 are both years of the Constitution, two years apart, and they are the pair that gets swapped: a document is written first, and a government begins to work under it afterwards.

### 9. Which of the three is law

*Unit Two · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 9 of 61*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-law`]

Can a court make anyone obey it?

*The neighborhood meeting*

> At a neighborhood meeting a man says: ‘The Declaration of Independence promises us the pursuit of happiness, so a court has to order the city to give me a better apartment.’ A woman answers: ‘A court cannot order that on the strength of the Declaration. It is not law.’ A third person asks: ‘Then what is law? Is the Constitution? And the Federalist Papers, the essays everybody quotes, are they law too?’ Nobody at the meeting is sure.

Some documents are law, which means that a court can order people to obey them. Others explain or argue, and a court cannot order anything on their strength. The woman at the meeting is right about the Declaration.

The Constitution is law. It is the supreme law of the land, which means that it outranks every other law. By “the Constitution” this unit means the original text of 1787 together with every amendment added to it since. An amendment is a change added to the Constitution.

The Declaration of Independence, mainly written by Thomas Jefferson, is not law. It says why the colonies were leaving Britain and sets up no government. A court cannot order anyone to give you “the pursuit of happiness” because of it.

The Federalist Papers are not law either. Alexander Hamilton, James Madison and John Jay wrote them as essays arguing that New York should approve the Constitution. A judge may quote one to understand what the founders had in mind, but it does not bind her.

### 15. What the Declaration of Independence says

*Unit Two · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 15 of 61*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-decl`]

The Declaration is not law, but it is quoted constantly.

*The voter*

> At a town meeting a woman is asked why she takes the trouble to vote. She says: ‘Nobody forced us to accept this government. It has power because we agree to be governed, so we get a say in who runs it.’ She adds: ‘And my rights are not something the government hands me. They cannot be taken away.’

The voter is repeating two ideas from the Declaration. They are why it is still quoted, though it is not law. The first is that people have unalienable rights, which means rights that cannot be taken away. It names three of them: life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness. The second is that a government gets its power from the consent of the governed, which means from the people agreeing to be governed. That is why Americans vote: where power comes from the people’s agreement, the people get a say in who runs it.

The rest of the Declaration is a long list of complaints against the king of Britain, the reasons it gives for separating from him.

### 24. The list of what Congress may do

*Unit Two · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 24 of 61*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-pow`]

Article I lists what Congress may do.

*The post office and the barber*

> Congress passes a law that sets up one postal service for the whole country. A reader asks where Congress gets the power to do that, and is shown Article I of the Constitution, which lists the power to establish post offices. Another reader asks about a different rule: how many hours of training a barber needs. Article I lists nothing about that, so it is not Congress’s to set. It belongs to the states.

Congress may set up a postal service, because Article I lists the power to establish post offices. Congress may not set how many hours a barber must train, because Article I lists nothing about that, so it belongs to the states.

Article I, Section 8, lists what Congress may do. Congress may use only the powers on the list, and one more: it may pass whatever laws are “necessary and proper” to carry the listed powers out. A power that is not on the list belongs to the states, or is something that no government may do.

The list is long. The facts below hold the five that matter most here: raising money, trade, the dollar and the mail, war and the armed forces, and the “necessary and proper” power. How far each power reaches is argued in court all the time. This holds only what is on the list.

## Part 2 of 2: The Bill of Rights, and the changes made later, then the drill

### 31. The Bill of Rights is part of the Constitution

*Unit Two · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 31 of 61*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-bor`]

What was added four years after the Constitution was written, and what people get wrong about it.

*The delegate who would not approve*

> Imagine a delegate from one of the states, after the Constitution has been written. He reads it and says: ‘It builds a government, and it says what that government may do. But nowhere does it promise, in writing, to protect a person’s rights. My state will not approve this Constitution until it does.’ Several states said the same.

The delegate’s complaint is the reason for the Bill of Rights. The Constitution of 1787 built a government and said what it might do, but it did not promise written protection for individual rights, and several states refused to approve it until it did. The answer, in 1791, was ten amendments, called the Bill of Rights.

So the Bill of Rights is not a separate document, and not a rival to the Constitution. It is the first ten amendments, so it is part of the Constitution, and it is law. That confuses even people who were born here: a person who says “the Constitution gives you the right to remain silent” and a person who answers “no, that is the Bill of Rights” are both right. Most of it is written as limits on what government may do to a person: “Congress shall make no law…”, “No person shall be…”.

### 35. Five amendments, and what each protects

*Unit Two · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 35 of 61*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-six`]

The ones that come up most, each as a number and what it protects.

*The bag*

> A police officer stops a man on the street and wants to search his bag. The officer has no reason at all to think that the bag holds anything wrong. The man says: ‘That is an unreasonable search, and one of the amendments to the Constitution forbids it.’

The man in the story is pointing at the Fourth Amendment without using its number. News stories name them by number all the time: “the First Amendment”, “the Fifth”. So hold the number first and what it protects second. This unit holds the First, the Fourth, the Fifth, the Sixth and the Tenth, and does not cover the other five.

Some words first. To petition is to ask the government to put right a wrong. A warrant is a judge’s written permission, which police generally need before a search. To testify is to say in court what you know, and the Fifth says that you cannot be forced to testify against yourself, which is called the right to remain silent. Due process means fair legal steps.

Three of the five protect a person who is in trouble with the law. A story about a search is about the Fourth, a story about silence is about the Fifth, and a story about a lawyer or a jury is about the Sixth. The First is about what a person may say and do freely. The Tenth is about government and not a person: it says what happens to powers that the Constitution does not give to the federal government.

### 43. How the Constitution is changed

*Unit Two · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 43 of 61*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-chg`]

How any change is made, and how many have been.

*Sam’s rule*

> Sam wants a new rule added to the Constitution. He asks his friend how many votes he would need. His friend says: ‘More than half is not enough. You would need far more than that, and from two different places, and one of the two is the states.’ Sam asks why it should be so hard. His friend says that it is meant to be.

Sam’s friend is right that it is meant to be hard. There are two steps. First, two-thirds of both chambers of Congress must vote to propose the change. The chambers are the House of Representatives and the Senate. Second, three-quarters of the states must approve it. The word for the states’ approval is “ratify”. A change added this way is an amendment, and it is part of the Constitution.

Because it is so hard, thousands have been proposed and only twenty-seven have been adopted. Ten of those were added together, in 1791: that is the Bill of Rights. Seventeen came after it.

### 49. The Fourteenth Amendment

*Unit Two · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 49 of 61*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-fth`]

The amendment that answers a question the Bill of Rights leaves open.

*The church permit*

> A state passes a law that no one may hold a religious service without a permit from the state, and then refuses a permit to one church. The church’s members say that the law breaks the First Amendment, which protects religion. A lawyer points out that the First Amendment begins ‘Congress shall make no law’. It names Congress, and this law was passed by a state. How can the First Amendment apply to it?

The lawyer’s question is a good one. The Bill of Rights was first written to limit only the federal government. Its First Amendment begins “Congress shall make no law”, and does not mention a state.

The answer is the Fourteenth Amendment, adopted in 1868, after the Civil War. It did three things. Anyone born in the United States is a citizen. No state may take a person’s life, liberty or property without due process, which means fair legal steps, or deny anyone equal protection of the laws, which means equal treatment in the same situation. And the courts later read it to bring the limits in the Bill of Rights to the states, so that they protect you against your state and your city as well. That last one answers the lawyer.

### 61. What to carry away

*Unit Two · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 61 of 61*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap-const`]

Every fact in the unit, in its group, and what to carry.

**The four dates**

- **In which year did the colonies announce that they were separating from Britain?** 1776
- **In which year did delegates meet in Philadelphia to write the Constitution?** 1787
- **In which year did the government under the Constitution begin?** 1789
- **In which year was the Bill of Rights added to the Constitution?** 1791

**Law, or not law**

- **Is the Declaration of Independence law?** Not law, because it says why the colonies were leaving Britain
- **Is the Constitution law?** Law, because it is the supreme law of the land, and it outranks every other law
- **Are the Federalist Papers law?** Not law, because they are essays that argued for approving the Constitution

**The two ideas**

- **Which rights does the Declaration say cannot be taken away?** Among them, life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness
- **Where does the Declaration say that a government gets its power?** From the consent of the governed, which means from the people agreeing to be governed

**Three articles, three parts**

- **Which part of the government does Article I of the Constitution describe?** Congress
- **Which part of the government does Article II of the Constitution describe?** The President
- **Which part of the government does Article III of the Constitution describe?** The courts

**Five powers of Congress**

- **What may Congress do to raise the money that the government spends?** Lay and collect taxes, and borrow money
- **What may Congress do about trade?** Regulate trade with other countries and between the states
- **What may Congress do about the dollar and about the mail?** Coin money and run the post offices
- **What may Congress do about war and the armed forces?** Declare war, and raise and support armies and a navy
- **Besides the listed powers, what else may Congress do?** Pass whatever laws are necessary and proper to carry the listed powers out

**Two facts about the Bill of Rights**

- **What is the Bill of Rights?** It is the first ten amendments to the Constitution, so it is part of the Constitution and is law
- **Why was the Bill of Rights added?** Several states would not approve the Constitution until it promised written protection for individual rights

**Five amendments and what each protects**

- **What does the First Amendment protect?** Freedom of speech, religion, the press, peaceful assembly, and petition
- **What does the Fourth Amendment protect?** No unreasonable searches or seizures; police generally need a warrant
- **What does the Fifth Amendment protect?** The right to remain silent, fair legal steps before punishment, and no second trial for the same crime
- **What does the Sixth Amendment protect?** A speedy, public jury trial, a lawyer, and the right to hear and question the witnesses against you
- **What does the Tenth Amendment say?** Powers not given to the federal government are kept by the states or the people

**Two fractions and a number**

- **What share of both chambers of Congress must vote to propose an amendment?** Two-thirds
- **What share of the states must approve a proposed amendment?** Three-quarters
- **How many amendments does the Constitution have so far?** Twenty-seven

**The three parts of the Fourteenth Amendment**

- **What does the Fourteenth Amendment make of anyone born in the United States?** A citizen of the United States
- **What does the Fourteenth Amendment require of a state that wants to take a person’s life, liberty or property?** Due process, which means fair legal steps
- **What does the Fourteenth Amendment forbid a state to deny anyone?** Equal protection of the laws, which means equal treatment in the same situation
- **What did the courts later read the Fourteenth Amendment to bring to the states?** The limits in the Bill of Rights, so that they stop a state or a city too

**Three amendments, and what each did**

- **Which amendment ended slavery?** The Thirteenth Amendment
- **Which amendment says that the right to vote cannot be denied because of race?** The Fifteenth Amendment
- **Which amendment says that the right to vote cannot be denied because of sex?** The Nineteenth Amendment

**To carry away**

- The Constitution is law, and it is the highest law the country has: the original text of 1787 together with every amendment added since, twenty-seven so far. The Bill of Rights is the first ten of those amendments, so it is part of the Constitution. The Declaration of Independence and the Federalist Papers are not law: one says why the colonies were leaving Britain, and the other argues for approving the Constitution.
- Hold each date with what happened in it: **1776** for the Declaration, **1787** for the Constitution being written, **1789** for the government under it beginning, and **1791** for the Bill of Rights.
- Congress has only the powers that the Constitution lists, in Article I, and the power to pass the laws that are necessary and proper to carry them out. A power that is not on the list belongs to the states, or is something that no government may do.
- To change the Constitution takes two steps: **Two-thirds** of both chambers of Congress must vote to propose the change, and **Three-quarters** of the states must approve it.
- The Bill of Rights was first written to limit only the federal government. After the Civil War the courts read the Fourteenth Amendment to bring its limits to the states, so that they stop a state or a city as well.

## After the unit: what comes back on later days

A name that is due returns as a case the learner has not seen, next to a case of the name they most often confuse it with. Each is run as a whole case: every question, then the name. These are the fresh cases held back for that purpose: three for each name, one for each scheduled return. The first return is about two days after the drill, the next about a week after that, the next about three and a half weeks later.
