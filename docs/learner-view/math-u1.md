### 1. Before you do the math, check what the problem is about

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 1 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient-kind`]

Before you start on a number problem from real life (a bill, a recipe, a loan), check what it is about. There are five kinds, each is worked out with different steps, and the wrong steps still give you a number that looks fine.

A friend is planning a birthday meal, and five questions come up in one afternoon, each with numbers in it. “Will 36 balloons go round 5 tables evenly, and how many are left over?” “The caterer charges a set fee plus a price for each guest, and the bill came to $310: how many guests?” “The cake shop raises its price by the same amount every year: what will the cake cost in five years?” “With 4 starters and 3 main courses, how many different menus can we offer?” “How long is the ribbon from the top of a 3 m pole to a peg 4 m from its foot?”

All five ask for a number, but each is worked out in a different way. Start with the wrong steps and you still get a number, and nothing in it tells you it is wrong. So the first thing to do is ask one question: **“What is this problem about?”**

What is this problem about?
- How whole numbers split and repeat → equal groups, leftovers, and repeating things that line up
- A missing number in a formula, rate or totals → a number you are not told, found from the numbers you are told
- An amount changing over time → one amount, followed as it changes
- Counting ways and chances → how many ways something can turn out, or how likely it is
- Right triangles, or one shape at two sizes → a length, area or volume from a right triangle or a bigger or smaller copy

The unit has three parts, and you can stop after any of them.

1. Splitting counts, a missing number, and change over time
2. Ways to count, how likely things are, and shapes
3. One whole problem, then the drill

Each starts from a real problem. After each one you answer a quick question, and the reason is shown right away. Nothing here is graded. A miss only decides what comes back.

### 2. How whole numbers split and repeat

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 2 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-whole`]

First: a problem about a count of things that has to split into equal groups.

*The chairs for the concert*

> The village hall has 72 chairs for a concert. The caretaker wants ⟦every row to hold the same number of chairs⟧, with more than one row and more than one chair in each row. ⟦In how many different ways can he set them out?⟧

The hall has 72 chairs, and the caretaker wants equal rows: 2 rows of 36, or 3 rows of 24, or 4 rows of 18, and so on. The numbers are whole counts, because you cannot have half a chair, and the question is how a count breaks into equal groups.

Three more questions about counts belong here too: how many are left over when things will not share evenly, when two things that repeat (one every 15 seconds, one every 20) happen together again, and where a count of days ends on the days of the week. It does not matter whether the things are chairs, tiles or days. What decides it is what the problem asks.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check the numbers are whole counts of things: 72 chairs.** You cannot have half a chair.
2. **Find the equal groups: every row holds the same number of chairs.** Splitting evenly, with none left over, is what this is about.
3. **Check the question is about how the count splits: “In how many different ways can he set them out?”** Nothing else is asked: no price, no length, nothing changing over time.
4. **Look for leftovers or repeats too: “how many are left over”, “when do they next happen together”, “what day will it be”.** These are the same kind of problem, because they are all about how whole counts fit together.

This is **“How whole numbers split and repeat”**. Swap the chairs for tiles or coins and nothing changes.

### 4. A calculation with a gap in it

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 4 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-formula`]

The next kind of problem leans on one word, so here it is first.

*The gym card*

> At the gym desk, the receptionist keeps a card that says: bill = 15 + 4 × classes.

The gym keeps a card at the desk, and it is how every bill is worked out: the bill is 15, plus 4 for each class. A member who books 3 classes pays 15 + 4 × 3 = 27. A member who books 10 pays 15 + 4 × 10 = 55.

The card is a calculation written out once, with a word where a number goes, so it works for any member. It also works backward: start from a bill of 43, and you can work out how many classes it was for.

**The word for this.** *Formula*: a calculation written out once, with a word or a letter where a number goes, so you can use it again with different numbers, such as bill = 8 + 0.25 × units.

A word or a letter where a number goes is how one calculation gets used again and again with different numbers.


### 5. A missing number in a formula, rate or totals

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 5 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-unknown`]

Second: a problem that hides one number, and gives you facts that fix it.

*The van hire*

> Maya hires a van. The hire shop charges ⟦a fixed $30 plus $0.40 for every kilometer driven⟧. ⟦Her bill is $54⟧. ⟦How many kilometers did she drive?⟧

Maya knows what the shop charges and what she paid. The one thing missing is how far she drove, and the facts are set up so that only one distance fits.

Here the facts are a *formula*: $30, plus $0.40 for each kilometer. A problem like this can give its facts in two other ways. One is a rate, so much for each thing, such as grams of rice for each person, with a new number of things to scale it to. The other is two totals about two numbers you are not told, such as how many things were bought in all and what they cost in all.

Every problem asks for a number, so having a number to find tells you nothing. What marks this kind is what you are given to find it with.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the number you are not told: how far Maya drove.** The problem asks for it and does not give it.
2. **Find the facts it has to fit: $30 plus $0.40 for every kilometer, and a bill of $54.** Only one distance makes the bill come out right.
3. **Check that no amount is growing or shrinking over time, and that there is no triangle.** Either of those would make it a different kind of problem.

This is **“A missing number in a formula, rate or totals”**. Hide the distance, and the other numbers still tell you exactly what it must be.

### 7. An amount changing over time

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 7 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-growth`]

Third: one amount that changes as time passes.

*The shrub*

> Oskar plants a shrub that is 40 cm tall. ⟦It grows 15 cm every year⟧. ⟦How tall will it be after 8 years?⟧

Oskar’s shrub starts at 40 cm and grows 15 cm every year. The problem follows that one amount through time and asks where it ends up: how tall it will be after 8 years. Some problems ask it the other way round: how long until it reaches a target.

The change comes in three ways. The same amount is added or taken off each time: the shrub gains 15 cm a year, or a bank balance loses $50 a month. The amount is multiplied by the same number each time: money that earns 4% a year, or a rumor that doubles every day. Or it changes once and then stays: a fee that went up in March and has not moved since. Each has its own steps, and you only need to see that one amount is followed through time.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the one amount that is followed: the height of the shrub.** The whole problem is about it.
2. **Find how it changes and how often: 15 cm, every year.** The time is nearly always in the words: each hour, every year, after 8 years.
3. **Check the question is about later: “How tall will it be after 8 years?”** It asks where the amount ends up, or how long until it reaches a target.

This is **“An amount changing over time”**. If you cannot find the hours, days, months or years, it is not this.

The van hire had a fixed fee, a price that repeats, a bill and a number you are not told, and it was **“A missing number in a formula, rate or totals”**. This problem has the same four parts. But the answer here is **“An amount changing over time”**.

A price for each thing is a missing number. A price for each hour, day, month or year is an amount that changes as time passes. The sums can come out the same either way: what changes is which steps you use.

### 11. Counting ways and chances

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 11 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-chance`]

Fourth: counting the different ways something can turn out.

*The packing*

> Zara is packing for a trip. She will wear ⟦one top, one pair of pants and one pair of shoes⟧. She has 5 tops, 4 pairs of pants and 3 pairs of shoes. ⟦How many different outfits can she make?⟧

Zara picks one top, one pair of pants and one pair of shoes, each from its own list. The problem is not about what an outfit costs or weighs. It asks how many different outfits there are.

This kind has two halves. One is counting ways: how many different results you get when you choose, pick or order things. The other is chance: how likely it is that something happens, or that a test result can be trusted. Zara’s problem is the counting half.

Many problems ask “how many”. What marks this one is that you are counting the different results of a choice.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the separate choices: a top, pants and shoes.** Each is picked from its own list.
2. **Check the question counts results: “How many different outfits can she make?”** It asks how many different results the choices give, not what any one of them is.
3. **If it asks how likely something is instead, look for “at least one”, or for a test result that may be wrong.** Those are the two chance problems this course covers.
4. **Check nothing is split into equal groups, measured or followed over time.** Here the numbers only say how many things there are to choose from.

This is **“Counting ways and chances”**. The numbers 5, 4 and 3 only say how long each list is.

**How to tell them apart**

Is the question about splitting a count evenly, what is left over, or when repeats meet? Or is it about the different results of a choice, how many there are or how likely one is?


### 14. A triangle with a square corner

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 14 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-righttriangle`]

The last kind of problem leans on one word, so here it is first.

*The sheet cut across*

> Take a sheet of paper and cut it along a straight line from one corner to the opposite corner. You get two pieces.

Look at a sheet of paper. Each of its four corners is a square corner, the kind you would use to check that a picture frame is straight. Cut the sheet along a straight line from one corner to the opposite corner, and each half is a triangle that keeps one of the sheet’s square corners.

You see the same shape everywhere: a wall, the floor and a ladder leaning between them. Or two roads that cross at a square junction, with a path cut straight across from one road to the other.

**The word for this.** *Right triangle*: a triangle with one square corner, like the corner of a page or where a wall meets the floor. That square corner is called a right angle.

The square corner is what matters. Once two of the sides are fixed, the third can only have one length.


### 15. Right triangles, or one shape at two sizes

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 15 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-shape`]

Fifth: a problem about a triangle with a square corner, or about a copy of a shape at another size.

*The hike*

> A hiker walks ⟦9 km due north and then 12 km due east⟧. ⟦How far in a straight line is she from where she started?⟧

The hiker walks 9 km north, then 12 km east. North and east meet at a square corner, so her two legs and the straight line back to the start make a *right triangle*. Two sides are given, and the problem asks for the third: how far she is from where she started.

This kind has a second half: two things of exactly the same shape at different sizes, such as a model and the real thing, a floor plan and the room, or two round pizzas. Then the problem asks for a length on one of them, or how much more area or volume the bigger one has.

A shape in the problem is not enough. A garden 8 m long with an area of 40 square meters has no triangle and no copy, so asking for its width is a different kind of problem.

**How to spot it**

1. **Look for a square corner: north and east meet at one.** Two straight legs at a square corner make a triangle with the line back to the start.
2. **Find the two sides you are given: 9 km and 12 km.** Two sides settle the third, so the problem only needs to give two.
3. **Check what is asked: “How far in a straight line is she from where she started?”** In this kind you are always asked for a length, an area or a volume.
4. **With no triangle, look for two things of exactly the same shape at different sizes.** A model and the real thing, or a plan and the room, count as one shape at two sizes.

This is **“Right triangles, or one shape at two sizes”**. A photo and its enlargement are one shape at two sizes: every length is made the same number of times longer.

### 18. The question to ask before any math

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 18 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-kind`]

Here is the question and its five answers in one place.

**The question:** **“What is this problem about?”**

**Its answers**

- **“How whole numbers split and repeat”**
  - Give this answer when you are given whole numbers and asked about equal groups, leftovers, which numbers multiply to make one, when repeats line up, or if a number can be written exactly.
- **“A missing number in a formula, rate or totals”**
  - Give this answer when one or two numbers are left out, and you are given a formula, a rate such as so much for each thing, or totals that they have to fit.
- **“An amount changing over time”**
  - Give this answer when one amount changes by the same number added or multiplied each month or year, or changes once, and you are asked what it will be or when it reaches a target.
- **“Counting ways and chances”**
  - Give this answer when you are asked how many ways something can be chosen or ordered, how likely at least one of several things is, or how likely a test result is to be right.
- **“Right triangles, or one shape at two sizes”**
  - Give this answer when there is a right triangle, or two things of exactly the same shape at different sizes, and you are asked for a length, an area or a volume, or how they compare.

**Why it matters**

Each of the five is worked out with different steps, and the questions after this one differ too. The wrong steps still give you a number, and nothing in that number tells you it is wrong.

Treat a hike as a missing number and you go looking for a calculation that is not there. Treat an amount followed through time as a missing number and you can find a number that fits a calculation and is wrong for the problem.

**How to answer it**

1. **Read to the end before you answer.** The question is usually the last sentence, and the sentences before it are the background.
2. **Mark the words that say what to work out.** The topic and the numbers do not decide it: money, building and cooking turn up in all five.
3. **If the question ends on a day of the week or a time on a clock: “How whole numbers split and repeat”.** A count that goes round a loop is about whole numbers, even though it runs over time.
4. **Next, if one amount is followed as hours, days, months or years pass: “An amount changing over time”.** This holds even when a missing number and a calculation are there too.
5. **Next, if there is a *right triangle*, or two copies of one shape: “Right triangles, or one shape at two sizes”.** This holds even when a rate is there too.
6. **Next, a missing number that has to fit a calculation, a rate or totals: “A missing number in a formula, rate or totals”.** This is what is left when there is no time, no triangle, no copy and no loop.
7. **A question that counts the different results of a choice, or asks how likely something is: “Counting ways and chances”.** It gives way to no other answer, and no other answer gives way to it.
8. **Find the exact words that show your answer.** If you can’t find them, you don’t have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Some problems show two at once. You have met three: a price for each hour looks like a missing number and is an amount over time; a model with a scale looks like a rate and is a shape; a box of tablets taken one a day is an amount over time and asks for a day of the week. Use the order above. The test for each pair is below.

- How whole numbers split and repeat or Counting ways and chances: Is the question about splitting a count evenly, what is left over, or when repeats meet? Or is it about the different results of a choice, how many there are or how likely one is?
- A missing number in a formula, rate or totals or An amount changing over time: Does the problem follow one amount as hours, days, months or years pass? Or does it hide a number that has to fit a calculation, a rate for each thing or two totals?
- A missing number in a formula, rate or totals or Right triangles, or one shape at two sizes: Is there a *right triangle*, or are there two things of exactly the same shape at different sizes? Or are there only facts that a missing number has to fit?
- An amount changing over time or How whole numbers split and repeat: Is the question about how an amount changes as time passes? Or is it about how counts fit together, such as a day of the week, what is left over, or when two repeats meet?
- A missing number in a formula, rate or totals or Counting ways and chances: Do the facts fix exactly one answer that has to fit? Or does the problem ask how many different results a choice has, or how likely one is?

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What is this problem about?
- How whole numbers split and repeat
- A missing number in a formula, rate or totals
- An amount changing over time
- Counting ways and chances
- Right triangles, or one shape at two sizes

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **“How whole numbers split and repeat”**: whole numbers, and a question about equal groups, leftovers, which numbers multiply to make one, when repeats line up, or whether a number can be written exactly.
- **“A missing number in a formula, rate or totals”**: a number you are not told, and a formula, a rate or totals, made of numbers you are told, that it has to fit.
- **“An amount changing over time”**: one amount changing each day, month or year by the same number added or multiplied, or changing once, and a question about what it will be or when it reaches a target.
- **“Counting ways and chances”**: something that can turn out in different ways, and a question about how many ways, how likely at least one of several things is, or whether a test result is right.
- **“Right triangles, or one shape at two sizes”**: a right triangle, or two things of exactly the same shape at different sizes, and a question about a length, an area or a volume.

**To carry away**

- Before any sum, ask what the problem is about, and find the words that show it. If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.
- The topic and the numbers do not decide it. Money, building and cooking turn up in all five, and so do “how many”, “how long” and “how much”.
- A price for each hour is **“An amount changing over time”**. A price for each kilometer or each person is **“A missing number in a formula, rate or totals”**.
- A model, a map or a shadow is **“Right triangles, or one shape at two sizes”**, even though it comes with a rate.
- A count of days or hours that has to end on a day of the week or a time on a clock is **“How whole numbers split and repeat”**, even though it runs over time.
