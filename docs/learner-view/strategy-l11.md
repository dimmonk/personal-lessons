# Strategy: What would have to be true

Revision 1, draft (the screen carries the draft line). Part: What would have to be true. Where the subject ends up: After this you can look at a business decision (expand, add a product, chase a big customer, answer a rival, invest) and tell whether it is a strategy, say where the business makes its money and why, and pick the move that keeps it different in a way rivals cannot copy, when you face that decision in your own business.

## The why

Most owners decide a big move by gathering every reason for it, then spend months on numbers for a move that one phone call could have ruled out.
Strategists turn it around: for each move, what would have to be true for it to work, and which of those do we have least reason to believe? Here you learn to find that condition and test it first.

## Step 1: Test the doubtful condition first

Say the decision. List the possible moves. For each move, say what would have to be true for it to work: about customers, about costs, about the people who would do it, about rivals.
Then look at what backs each condition. Something someone has said, signed, paid, measured or done already is evidence. A guess, a hope, or nobody having asked is not.
Test first the condition with the least behind it. Test it cheaply: a phone call, a week’s trial, a count. If it fails, the move fails, and nothing more needs working out.
Choose the move whose conditions held up. A full plan with years of numbers comes after the tests, for the move that survived.
Why it matters: a plan full of numbers feels certain, but it rests on its weakest condition. Testing that one first is the cheapest way to find out.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “Before deciding anything, I will write the full business plan with five years of numbers.”
The right line: Five years of numbers rest on conditions nobody has tested. Find the condition you have least reason to believe, test it, and write the full plan only for the move that survives.

## Step 2: Two conditions for one move

| A | B |
|---|---|
| “The bakers can make three more cakes a week: they made that many last June with the same staff.” | “The planners would send couples to a bakery that pays them no fee: none has said, and the bakery they use now pays 10%.” |

Compare: Both have to be true. The first is backed by what the bakers did. Nothing backs the second, so test it first.

## Step 3: A worked example

Your own note, written after an argument: "Dana’s notes on a move", Written in January.

> The move: sell wedding cakes through the three wedding planners in town.
> The planners plan enough weddings: about 150 a year between them, they told us.
> Our bakers can make three more cakes a week: we made that many last June with the same staff.
> The planners would send couples to a bakery that pays them no fee: none has said, and the bakery they use now pays them 10%.
> Couples would pay our price: our wedding cakes sell at that price now.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**Which condition should be tested first?**
(the options come in an order that depends on the seed of the question)
- The planners plan enough weddings
- The bakers can make three more cakes a week
- The planners would send couples with no fee
- Couples would pay our price

The working, one step at a time:
1. Read what would have to be true, and what backs each.: The number of weddings: the planners said so. The bakers: they did it last June. The price: cakes sell at it now. The planners sending couples with no fee: nobody has said, and the rival pays 10%.
1. Test first the one with the least behind it.: The fee is the one nobody has said yes to, and the rival pays one. If it fails, the move fails, whatever else holds.

Then the answer: The planners would send couples with no fee / Phone each planner this week and find out whether they would send couples to us with no fee, and if not, what fee they would want. Three conditions already have something behind them: what the planners said, what the bakers did, what couples pay now. Nothing backs the planners sending couples with no fee, and the bakery they use pays 10%, so that is the one to test first.
