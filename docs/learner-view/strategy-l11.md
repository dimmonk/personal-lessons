# Strategy: What would have to be true

Revision 1, draft (the screen carries the draft line). Part: What would have to be true. Where the subject ends up: After this you can look at a business decision (expand, add a product, chase a big customer, answer a rival, invest) and tell whether it is a strategy, say where the business makes its money and why, and pick the move that keeps it different in a way rivals cannot copy, when you face that decision in your own business.

## The why

Most owners decide a big move by gathering every reason for it, then spend months on numbers for a move that one phone call could have ruled out.
Strategists turn it around: for each move, what would have to be true for it to work, and which of those do we have least reason to believe? Here you learn to find that condition and test it first.

## Step 1: Test the condition least likely to hold

Say the decision. List the possible moves. For each move, say what would have to be true for it to work: about customers, costs, the people who would do it, rivals. Each one must hold, or the move fails; something that would only help is not one of them.
Then weigh what backs each condition. Something done, paid, signed or counted is evidence for it. People saying they like the idea, a friend’s praise, or the owner being sure is only talk: it costs nothing. A fact that points the other way is evidence against it.
Test first the condition least likely to hold: one with a fact against it before one resting on talk or on nothing, and either of those before one with evidence behind it. Test it cheaply: a phone call, a week’s trial, a count.
If the test fails, drop the move, or change it so it no longer needs that condition, and turn to the next move. If it holds, test the next least likely condition of the same move. Choose the move whose conditions held; the full plan with years of numbers comes after, for that move.
Why it matters: a plan full of numbers feels certain, but it rests on its weakest condition. Testing that one first is the cheapest way to find out.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “Before deciding anything, I will write the full business plan with five years of numbers.”
The right line: Five years of numbers rest on conditions nobody has tested. Find the one least likely to hold, test it, and write the full plan only for the move that survives.

## Step 2: Two conditions for one move

| A | B |
|---|---|
| “The planners would send couples to us: they said over coffee that they would be glad to.” | “The planners would do it with no fee: the bakery they use now pays them 10% of every cake.” |

Compare: Both are in doubt. The first rests on talk, which costs nothing. The second has a fact pointing against it, so it is the less likely to hold: test it first.

## Step 3: A worked example

A written note: "Dana’s memo on a move", Written in January.

> The move: sell wedding cakes through the three wedding planners in town.
> The planners plan enough weddings: their calendars show about 150 a year between them.
> The planners would send couples to us: they said over coffee that they would be glad to.
> Our bakers can make three more cakes a week: they made that many every week last June.
> The planners would do it with no fee: the bakery they use now pays them 10% of every cake.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**Which condition should be tested first?**
(the options come in an order that depends on the seed of the question)
- The planners plan enough weddings
- The planners would send couples
- The bakers can make three more a week
- The planners would take no fee

The working, one step at a time:
1. Weigh what backs each condition.: The weddings: counted on the calendars. The bakers: done last June. The planners sending couples: only said over coffee, which costs nothing. No fee: the bakery they use now pays them 10%, a fact pointing the other way.
1. Test first the one least likely to hold.: Two are in doubt, but the fee has a fact against it, so it is the least likely. If it fails, the move fails, whatever else holds.

Then the answer: The planners would take no fee / Phone each planner this week to find out whether they would send couples with no fee, and if not, what fee they would want. Two conditions are counted or done already. The planners sending couples rests on talk, but no fee has a fact against it: their bakery pays 10%. That is the least likely to hold, so it is tested first.
