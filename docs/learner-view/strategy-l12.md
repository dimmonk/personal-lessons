# Strategy: How did others like it do?

Revision 1, draft (the screen carries the draft line). Part: How did others like it do?. Where the subject ends up: After this you can look at a business decision (expand, add a product, chase a big customer, answer a rival, invest) and tell whether it is a strategy, say where the business makes its money and why, and pick the move that keeps it different in a way rivals cannot copy, when you face that decision in your own business.

## The why

A plan written by its owner nearly always looks likely to work. That is the trouble: it is built from the owner’s hopes, and it forgets that rivals move too.
Strategists check a plan against how others like it did, and against how the rival will answer. Here you learn to start from the record, read a rival, and test an example on its reason.

## Step 1: Start from how others did

First, find many ventures like this one, alike in what makes them work or fail, and see what share of them worked. Start your forecast from that share. Move from it only for a real difference, such as customers already signed up, never because your plan sounds good.
Second, work out how the rival will answer. It answers when your move takes customers it serves and it can follow without changing how it runs. It lets the move go when those customers are not its own, or following would cost it its own way of running.
Third, when someone says “it worked for them”, find the reason it worked there, and check that the reason is true here. Looking alike is not enough.
Why it matters: owners who judge a plan by its own story expect too much, and a rival’s answer can take the gain away in weeks.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “Our plan is solid and we know this business, so it will work.”
The right line: Every owner in the record had a plan and knew the business, and the record shows how many made it. Start from that, and plan for the rival’s answer.

## Step 2: A worked example

Mark’s plan for a second gym says it will surely still be open in three years. Here is how others like it did.

A plain table, "New gyms in the county, and how many were still open three years later":

> Year opened  |  Opened  |  Still open after three years  (the column heads)
> 2019  |  14  |  4
> 2020  |  16  |  3
> 2021  |  12  |  5

The working, one step at a time:
1. Read the table roughly: about how many of each year’s openings were still open?: About 4 of 14, 3 of 16, 5 of 12: each year well under half, roughly a quarter to a third.
1. Add those still open, add all that opened, and divide.: 4 + 3 + 5 = 12 still open; 14 + 16 + 12 = 42 opened; 12 ÷ 42 = 28.6%.
1. Start the forecast from that share, not from the plan.: Begin near 29%. Move from it only for a real difference between Mark’s gym and the 42 others, such as members already signed up.

Then the answer: 28.6% / 28.6% / From 28.6%, the share of others like it still open, then adjust only for real differences. Of 42 gyms that opened, 12 were still open three years later: 28.6%. Mark’s forecast starts there and moves only for a real difference, however sure the plan sounds.
