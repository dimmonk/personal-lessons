## Part 1 of 2: One big holding, and a business you run

### 1. When most of your money is in one thing

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `w3-orient`]

Before you act on a warning that your money is all in one place, check what the one thing is and what you can actually do about it. Sometimes it is already safe, and the right move is to leave it alone.

You have heard stories like these. A woman has most of her savings in the shares of the company she has worked for over thirty years: “It has never let me down.” A man put everything into his roofing firm, then borrowed against it to buy a truck. A landlord has six rentals in his own name, and a tenant has just fallen on the stairs of one.

Each time the first question gives the same answer: **“One thing that could wipe it out”**. What to do next depends on what the one thing is and what the person is able to do about it. Sometimes the answer is to do nothing.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“What could lose this money?”** Its answers:

- **“Money going out every year”**: give this answer when money goes out of the pot every year: fees charged by funds or an adviser, tax on the investments, or a sum taken out to spend.
- **“One thing that could wipe it out”**: give this answer when most of the pot depends on one thing: one company or property that is most of it, a claim that could reach everything they own, or a lender who could force a sale. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“Prices falling at the wrong time”**: give this answer when a fall in prices would hurt because of what the money has to pay for or how it is split: living costs, a bill due on a set date, or a mix drifted from its plan.
- **“Handing it over”**: give this answer when the money passes to others when the owner dies, can no longer manage it or gives it away, and the question is who gets it, the tax on it, the papers, or the people.
- **“Nothing could lose it”**: give this answer when money is being kept, and none of the other four show up: no needless fee or tax, nothing most of it depends on, no bill or drifted mix, and no handover coming.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. Sell it off bit by bit

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `w3-meet-diversify`]

Start with the plainest one: a big holding, and nothing in the way of selling it.

*Meena and the bus shares*

> Meena, 48, is a nurse. Her late father left her shares in the regional bus company, now worth $420,000. Apart from those she has $110,000 in savings and a 401(k). She has never worked for the bus company, ⟦has no say in how it is run⟧, and ⟦the shares can be sold on any day⟧.

Meena has $420,000 of her $530,000 in one company. That is 79%. If its price halves, she loses $210,000, which is 40% of everything she has, whatever the rest of the market does.

Nothing stops her selling, and she does not work there, so selling costs her no job. So she can sell in steps and put the money into funds that hold many companies. Four sales of $105,000, three months apart and planned on paper first, leave nothing in the one company after a year. She does not sell everything today, because nobody knows which day is a good one.

**How to spot it**

1. **Work out the share: $420,000 of $530,000 is 79%.** Most of her money is in one company.
2. **Check that nothing stops the sale: “the shares can be sold on any day”.** If something does stop it, the fix is different.
3. **Check that she does not run the company: she “has no say in how it is run”.** If she ran it, selling would cost her her job.

This is **“One holding they can sell and do not run”**. What to do is **Sell it off bit by bit**: sell part, then more, on planned dates, and put the money into funds. It does not say the company is a bad one, only that no one company should be most of what you have.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Divide the holding by everything you own and write down the percentage: $420,000 ÷ $530,000 is 79%.** That is the number to bring down.
2. **Plan the sales on paper: how many, and how far apart.** A plan made in advance saves you from guessing which day is best.
3. **Put each sale into funds that hold many companies, after checking the fees and tax.** Another single company would bring the same risk back.
4. **Follow the dates, whatever the price did.** The plan only works if you keep to it.

You may also hear this called “staged diversification”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Sell it off bit by bit**.

### 4. Cap the loss without selling

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `w3-meet-hedge`]

The same situation with one fact changed: a rule stops the sale.

*Tomasz and the locked shares*

> Tomasz, 38, joined a software company early on. Last month the company sold its shares to the public for the first time, and the rules say that ⟦staff may not sell any of their own shares for two years⟧. His 20,000 shares are worth $400,000 at today’s price of $20, and his other money is $50,000 in savings.

Tomasz cannot sell a quarter every three months, because for two years he cannot sell at all. A company that has just sold shares to the public often bars its staff from selling for a while, and a pay plan often holds shares back until a set date.

So the question is how to limit his loss while he waits. A bank or broker will sell him, for a fee, the right to sell at a set price at any time in the next two years. Say today’s price is $20 and he pays $1.60 per share for the right to sell at $16. If the price falls to $8 he still sells at $16, so he loses $5.60 per share instead of $12. If the price rises, he loses only the fee.

This only limits the loss. It does not spread his money around. When the rule ends, he can sell in steps, as Meena can. Find out first what is allowed: the agreements that bar staff from selling often bar these contracts too, and many employers forbid them at any time.

**How to spot it**

1. **Work out the share: $400,000 of $450,000 is 89%.** Most of his money is in one company.
2. **Find the rule that stops the sale: staff “may not sell their own shares for two years”.** This is the one fact that is different from Meena’s story.
3. **Find when the rule ends: two years from the day the company sold its shares to the public.** Until then he can only limit the loss, not sell.

This is **“One holding they are not allowed to sell yet”**. What to do is **Cap the loss without selling**: put a floor under the price while the shares are locked. It says nothing against the company.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Find the exact rule: which shares, until what date, and what happens to them if you leave.** Everything else depends on those three facts.
2. **Ask whether your employer allows a contract that sets a floor under the price, and get its cost in writing.** Many employers forbid them, and the fee is real money.
3. **Plan your first sale for the day the rule ends.** From then on you can sell in steps.

You may also hear this called “hedging” or “a collar”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Cap the loss without selling**.

### 6. Sell it off bit by bit or Cap the loss without selling: telling them apart

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 6 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `w3-look-diversify-hedge`]

Both are one company’s shares that are most of what the person has. The only difference is whether she is allowed to sell.

**Story A**

> Ruth, 50, owns $500,000 of shares in the sports-shoe company where she used to work, and she has $60,000 of other savings. She left the company two years ago and has no part in running it. She could sell the shares on any day.

**Story B**

> Ruth, 50, owns $500,000 of shares in the sports-shoe company where she works, and she has $60,000 of other savings. The rules of the staff share plan say she may not sell any of them for another eighteen months.

**What to compare.** Both stories are about Ruth, who has $500,000 of shares in the same sports-shoe company and $60,000 of other savings. Compare one thing: whether anything stops her selling the shares.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“One holding they are not allowed to sell yet”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A Ruth left the company two years ago and could sell on any day. That is **“One holding they can sell and do not run”**, so the plan is **Sell it off bit by bit**: sell in steps.

In Story B the shares and the sum are the same, but the rules of the staff share plan stop her selling for another eighteen months. That is **“One holding they are not allowed to sell yet”**, so the plan is **Cap the loss without selling**: she can only limit the loss while she waits.

Only the rule is different, and the rule decides. How much is in one company does not tell you which this is.

**How to tell them apart**

Is anything stopping the person selling, and until when?


### 7. What makes it safe to keep most of your money in a business you run

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `w3-term-threesupports`]

The next answers are about a business the person runs. Here is one where nothing is missing.

*Lucía’s printing firm*

> Lucía, 50, runs the printing firm her father started. It is worth $900,000. Her other money is $360,000: $270,000 in funds that hold thousands of companies, and $90,000 in savings. She and her family spend $30,000 a year. The loan on her house is secured on the house, and no bank holds her shares in the firm as security.

Lucía’s firm is $900,000 of her $1,260,000, which is 71%. If it had a very bad year, a lot of what she owns would go with it. Three things are why she is still safe.

First, everything else she has is spread out. Her other $270,000 is in funds that hold thousands of companies, so a bad year for printing is not a bad year for the rest of her money.

Second, several years of spending are held outside the firm. $90,000 in savings against $30,000 a year is three years, the figure this unit uses. If the firm earned nothing for a year or two, she would not have to drain it or sell it at a bad moment.

Third, no loan is secured on her shares in the firm. A lender that holds your shares as security can take them, or force a sale, if payments slip. Her house loan is secured on the house, so nothing the bank does touches the firm.

With all three she can keep most of what she has in a business she knows and runs. If one is missing, that is a gap.

**The word for this.** *The three safety nets*: what makes it safe to keep most of the pot in a business you run: everything else spread across many investments, several years of spending held outside the business, and no loan against its shares.

From here on, *the three safety nets* means these three things. A business is safe only with all three together.


### 8. Put the three safety nets in place

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 8 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `w3-meet-supports`]

The other side of Lucía’s story: when one of the three is missing, a business that is most of what you have stops being a sound choice and becomes a risk.

*Femi and the roofing firm*

> Femi, 46, runs the roofing firm he started twenty years ago. It is worth $480,000, which is most of the $560,000 he owns. ⟦The other $80,000 is a van worth $66,000 and $14,000 in a savings account⟧. ⟦His family spends $36,000 a year⟧. Two years ago he borrowed $90,000 to buy a truck, and ⟦the bank holds his shares in the firm as security⟧.

Femi’s other money is a van and a savings account, so nothing is spread out: a bad year for roofing hurts the van too. His family spends $36,000 a year, so $14,000 is under five months, and a year of no earnings would force him to drain the firm or sell it. And a bank holds his shares as security for the $90,000 truck loan, so if payments slip, the bank could take the shares and decide the firm’s future when he is least able to argue.

One missing is enough for this answer, and Femi has all three missing. Fix them in the order of urgency: the loan first, because a lender can use it against him; then the savings, because they buy time; then spreading the rest, because that is the slowest.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check he runs the business and it is most of what he owns: the roofing firm is $480,000 of $560,000.** That is when *the three safety nets* matter.
2. **Check whether the rest is spread out: a van and $14,000 in savings is not.** A bad year for the firm would hurt the van too.
3. **Divide savings by yearly spending: $14,000 ÷ $36,000 is under five months.** You want several years, not months.
4. **Check for a loan against the firm’s shares: “the bank holds his shares in the firm as security”.** The bank could take the shares if payments slip.
5. **If any one of the three is missing, this is the answer.** All three have to be there to make it safe.

This is **“A business they run, missing a safety net”**. What to do is **Put the three safety nets in place**: build whichever of *the three safety nets* is missing. It says nothing against running a business or keeping most of what you own in it.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Check each of the three with numbers: savings ÷ yearly spending, how much else is in funds, and any loan that has the firm’s shares as security.** That shows which ones are missing.
2. **Deal with a loan against the shares first.** It is the one a lender can use against you.
3. **Then build the savings toward about three years of spending.** That buys time in a bad year.
4. **Then put money that comes out of the business into funds that hold many companies.** This is the slowest to build.
5. **Check all three again once a year.** One change can open a gap.

### 10. Safe as it stands

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 10 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `w3-meet-safe`]

Now the opposite: the same business, the same share of his money, and nothing missing.

*Hugo and the lumberyard*

> Hugo, 57, runs the family lumberyard, worth $800,000, which is most of the $1,150,000 he owns. He has ⟦$250,000 in funds that hold thousands of companies⟧ and $100,000 in savings. His household spends $33,000 a year, so ⟦the savings cover three years⟧. ⟦No bank holds his shares in the yard as security⟧.

Hugo’s story has Femi’s shape, but he has all three of *the three safety nets*, in numbers. So nothing needs doing, and that is a real answer: “Yes, most of what I have is in one business, and it is looked after.”

Say it firmly, because every fix has a price. If an adviser told Hugo to sell half the yard, he would pay tax and fees and give up part of a business he runs and likes, to guard against a bad year that his savings, his funds and his clean ownership already guard against.

This holds only while the numbers hold. If he borrows against the shares or spends the savings, one safety net goes and the answer changes. The same answer fits other stories where the one thing is already looked after: insurance well above any claim, each property in its own company, or a small fixed loan the bank cannot demand back while it is paid.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check he runs the yard and it is most of what he owns: $800,000 of $1,150,000.** It starts out the same as Femi’s story.
2. **Check the rest is spread out: $250,000 in funds that hold thousands of companies.** A bad year for lumber does not hit all his money.
3. **Divide savings by yearly spending: $100,000 ÷ $33,000 is just over three years.** Three years is the figure this unit uses.
4. **Check no bank holds his shares in the yard as security: “No bank holds his shares”.** Nobody else can take them.
5. **If all three are there, leave it alone.** A fix would cost money to solve a problem he does not have.

This is **“Nothing: it is already safe”**, and the name is **Safe as it stands**. It is the one name in this unit that says nothing needs doing. It does not say nothing could go wrong, only that a bad year would not force him to do anything.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Write down the numbers that make it safe: the years of spending set aside, the insurance next to the biggest claim, whose name holds each property, the loan’s rate and terms.** Then you can check them later.
2. **Leave the one big thing alone.** Every fix has a price.
3. **Check the numbers once a year, and whenever you borrow, spend the savings or change a policy.** One change can turn a safe thing into a gap.
4. **If someone offers a fix, ask what problem it solves that your numbers do not.** If they cannot say, you do not need it.

## Part 2 of 2: Claims, properties in one name and loans, then the drill

### 13. Insure against a huge claim

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 13 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `w3-meet-insure`]

Next, harm that someone could ask you to pay for. Start with a person who has insurance, and a demand that could be bigger.

*Hari and the pool*

> Hari, 53, owns a house worth $700,000 and has $250,000 in savings and investments. His house has a swimming pool, and the neighbors’ children often swim in it. ⟦His home insurance pays up to $500,000 if someone is hurt on his property⟧. A lawyer he knows says that ⟦a serious injury to a child can lead to a demand for $2,000,000 or more⟧.

What matters is the gap between what *a claim* could be and what the insurance pays: $2,000,000 less $500,000 is $1,500,000, more than the $950,000 Hari owns. A court can order a gap like that paid from his savings and investments and from much of his house, so one accident at the pool could take most of what he has. Whether he would be at fault is for a court to decide. The story shows how big the demand could be.

The fix is more insurance on top of what he has. Extra coverage of $2,000,000 would meet a demand of $2,500,000 from the insurer, not from his house. It usually costs little next to what it covers, so ask for a quote. It will not pay for harm done on purpose, and every policy has exclusions, so read what it leaves out. If his insurance paid more than any claim a lawyer could name, this would be **Safe as it stands**.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find what could bring *a claim*: the swimming pool that the neighbors’ children use.** A car, a home, a pool or a rental can all do it.
2. **Find what the insurance pays: up to $500,000.** That is the most the insurer will cover.
3. **Find how big *a claim* could get: a lawyer says “$2,000,000 or more”.** Use a number from a lawyer or an insurer, not a guess.
4. **Subtract: $2,000,000 less $500,000 leaves $1,500,000 uncovered.** If that gap is far bigger than what you can afford to lose, this is the answer.

This is **“A claim bigger than their insurance”**. What to do is **Insure against a huge claim**: add insurance on top of what you have. It does not say *a claim* will come, only that if one did, there would be a gap.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Find the limit on each policy that pays if someone sues you: home, car, rental, business.** Those limits are all the insurer will pay.
2. **Put each limit next to the biggest claim a lawyer or insurer thinks is realistic.** The gap between them is what to close.
3. **If the gap is large, get a quote for extra coverage: the amount, the price a year, and what it leaves out.** You decide with the price in front of you.
4. **Check again when something changes: a new driver, a pool, another property.** Each one can open a new gap.

You may also hear this called “umbrella insurance” or “risk transfer”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Insure against a huge claim**.

### 15. A business that the law treats as a person

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 15 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `w3-term-company`]

The next answer is about several properties that could each bring *a claim*, and its fix uses one more word. Here is what the word does.

*Ana and Bo*

> Ana and Bo each own a small house worth $250,000 that they rent out, and each also lives in a home worth $500,000. Ana owns her rental in her own name. Bo’s rental belongs to an LLC he set up, which has $20,000 in its bank account; Bo owns the LLC, and his home is in his own name. In each rental a tenant is badly hurt in a fall on the stairs. Each tenant wins $400,000 from the owner, and each owner’s insurance pays $100,000 of it, which leaves $300,000 to find.

Ana and Bo have the same rental, the same home and the same loss: $300,000 to find after the insurance. The only difference is whose name the rental is in.

Ana holds hers in her own name, so the order is against Ana and can reach everything she owns that the law does not protect: the rental’s $250,000 and, in most states, her home for the other $50,000.

Bo holds his in a company he set up, of the kind most small landlords use. The law treats a company as a person of its own, so the claim is against the company. It owns the rental and $20,000 in the bank, so it can pay $270,000 and no more. The other $30,000 is not Bo’s to pay, and his home is out of reach.

The company did not make the claim go away. It stopped the claim at the edge of what the company owns. A company costs money to set up and keep, it does not shield an owner from their own wrongdoing, and a lender will often ask the owner to promise personally to repay the company’s loan.

**The word for this.** *An LLC*: a company, common for small businesses and rental property, that the law treats as a person of its own: it owns things and owes its debts in its own name, so its owners do not normally pay those debts from their own money.

A company like Bo’s is *an LLC*. From here on, when a story says a property or a business is in a company of its own, it means a company like Bo’s: *a claim* reaches what the company owns and, normally, no further.


### 16. One LLC per property or business

### 19. Borrow less, on safer terms

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 19 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `w3-meet-deleverage`]

The last problem is a loan. A loan can be a good thing. What matters is what the lender is allowed to do.

*Ian and the margin loan*

> Ian, 54, owns shares worth $600,000. He owes his brokerage $350,000 on a margin loan he used to buy them, and the shares are the brokerage’s security for it. The contract says that ⟦if the loan ever becomes more than 60% of what the shares are worth, Ian must pay in more money within two days, or the brokerage will sell some of his shares⟧.

Ian owes $350,000 against $600,000 of shares, which is 58%, and the limit in his contract is 60%. A fall of just 10%, to $540,000, puts him over it: 60% of $540,000 is $324,000, so the brokerage may demand $26,000 or sell.

The brokerage is protecting its own loan, on its own timetable. If Ian cannot find $26,000 in two days, some of his shares are sold at the low price, for good, and when prices come back they are not there. A loss on paper becomes a real loss, at the moment the lender chooses. That needs no crash, and the company need not be a poor one: only a loan that is large against what it is secured on, and a lender who is allowed to act.

The fix is to change the loan, not the shares. Borrow less: $150,000 against $600,000 is 25%, and the shares would have to fall 58% before the limit was reached. Prefer a rate fixed for years and terms the lender cannot cancel while you make the payments. A loan that is small, fixed and cannot be demanded back while it is paid is **Safe as it stands**.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the loan and what it is secured on: $350,000, secured on his shares.** The lender can sell what the loan is secured on.
2. **Divide the loan by the value: $350,000 ÷ $600,000 is 58%.** The bigger the share, the less room the shares have to fall.
3. **Find what the lender may do: demand more money or sell some shares if the loan passes 60% of their value.** That power is what can force a sale.

This is **“A loan the lender could use to force a sale”**. What to do is **Borrow less, on safer terms**. It does not say borrowing is wrong or the lender is acting badly, only that this loan gives the lender a power that could force a sale.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Write down every loan: how much, what it is secured on, whether the rate is fixed, and whether the lender can demand the money back or ask for more security.** Those facts show what the lender can do.
2. **Divide the loan by the limit the contract names: $350,000 ÷ 0.6 is about $583,000.** If the shares fall below that, the lender can act.
3. **Reduce the loan or change its terms until the fall needed is very large.** The bigger the cushion, the less the lender can do.
4. **Do not replace it with another loan on the same terms.** That brings the same power back.

### 22. The question to ask each time

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 22 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `w3-q-shock`]

Here is the question and its seven answers in one place.

**The question:** **“What one thing could take most of it?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other six.

- **“One holding they can sell and do not run”**
  - Give this answer when one company’s shares or one property is most of what the person owns, nothing stops them selling it, and they take no part in running it.
  - It leads to **Sell it off bit by bit**.
- **“One holding they are not allowed to sell yet”**
  - Give this answer when one company’s shares are most of what the person owns, and a rule stops them selling for a set time, such as shares paid as part of their wages.
  - It leads to **Cap the loss without selling**.
- **“A business they run, missing a safety net”**
  - Give this answer when a business the person runs is most of what they own, and at least one of the three safety nets is missing, such as years of spending held outside the business.
  - It leads to **Put the three safety nets in place**.
- **“A claim bigger than their insurance”**
  - Give this answer when something in the person’s life could bring a claim against them, like a car or a rented-out property, and that claim could be far bigger than their insurance.
  - It leads to **Insure against a huge claim**.
- **“Several properties or businesses, all in their own name”**
  - Give this answer when the person owns several properties or businesses that could each bring a claim, all held in their own name, so one claim could reach the rest and their home.
  - It leads to **One LLC per property or business**.
- **“A loan the lender could use to force a sale”**
  - Give this answer when a loan’s lender can demand it back at any time, or its rate can jump, or it is large next to what it is borrowed against, so a fall in prices could force a sale.
  - It leads to **Borrow less, on safer terms**.
- **“Nothing: it is already safe”**
  - Give this answer when the one big thing is already made safe, like a business with all three safety nets in place or insurance well above any claim, and none of the other six show up.
  - It leads to **Safe as it stands**.

**Why it matters**

The fix depends on what the one thing is and what the person can do about it: whether they can sell it, whether they run it, and whether a claim or a lender could reach everything. One fix leaves the others where they were.

A wrong answer buys a fix for a problem you do not have: selling does nothing for a loan, and more insurance does nothing for properties in one name. “Already safe” is as much an answer as the rest.

**How to answer it**

1. **Read the story to the end, then find the words that show what the one thing is and what the person can do about it.** If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.
2. **Ask first: does the person run a business that is most of what they have? Then check *the three safety nets*.** One missing is **Put the three safety nets in place**, and all three there is **Safe as it stands**.
3. **If they do not run it, ask whether they can sell it.** If they can, it is **Sell it off bit by bit**; if a rule stops them, it is **Cap the loss without selling**.
4. **For *a claim*, several properties or a loan, ask whether there is a gap.** A gap is **Insure against a huge claim**, **One LLC per property or business** or **Borrow less, on safer terms**, and no gap is **Safe as it stands**.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Two pairs can both seem to fit one story, and there is a rule for each. One claim bigger than the insurance wins over properties in one name. A business the person runs, with one of *the three safety nets* missing, wins over a loan the lender could use, because a loan against a business’s shares is one of the three. - Sell it off bit by bit or Cap the loss without selling: Is anything stopping the person selling, and until when?

- Insure against a huge claim or Safe as it stands: Put the biggest claim that could come next to what the insurance pays. Which is bigger, and by how much?

- Borrow less, on safer terms or Safe as it stands: How large is the loan against what it is secured on, and what is the lender allowed to do?

### 25. What to carry away

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 25 of 26*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `w3-recap`]

You have now run the question on your own. Here is the unit in one place.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What one thing could take most of it?
- One holding they can sell and do not run → Sell it off bit by bit
- One holding they are not allowed to sell yet → Cap the loss without selling
- A business they run, missing a safety net → Put the three safety nets in place
- A claim bigger than their insurance → Insure against a huge claim
- Several properties or businesses, all in their own name → One LLC per property or business
- A loan the lender could use to force a sale → Borrow less, on safer terms
- Nothing: it is already safe → Safe as it stands

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Sell it off bit by bit**: one company’s shares or one property that is most of what the person owns, nothing stopping them selling it, and no part for them in running it.
- **Cap the loss without selling**: one company’s shares that are most of what the person owns, and a rule that stops them selling for a set time, such as shares paid as part of their wages.
- **Put the three safety nets in place**: a business the person runs day to day that is most of what they own, and at least one of the three safety nets missing.
- **Safe as it stands**: the one thing most of the pot depends on already made safe, like a business with all three safety nets in place, or insurance well above any claim that could come.
- **Insure against a huge claim**: something in the person’s life that could bring a claim against them, like a car or a rented-out property, and a claim that could be far bigger than their insurance.
- **One LLC per property or business**: several properties or businesses that could each bring a claim, all held in the person’s own name, so that one claim could reach the rest and their home.
- **Borrow less, on safer terms**: a loan a lender could use to force a sale: one they can demand back at any time, one whose rate can jump, or one that is large next to what it is borrowed against.

**To carry away**

- Before you reach for a fix, say what the one thing is and what the person can do about it, and find the words that show it. If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.
- A big holding is not enough on its own. Whether the person can sell it, and whether they run it, decide between **Sell it off bit by bit**, **Cap the loss without selling** and **Put the three safety nets in place**.
- A business you run is made safe by all of *the three safety nets*. One missing is a gap, and a loan against the shares is the one a lender can use.
- For *a claim*, compare two numbers: how big it could be, and what the insurance pays. **Insure against a huge claim** is for the gap between them. **One LLC per property or business** limits how far *a claim* reaches, and it costs money, so it is worth it only where the saving is larger.
- A loan is about what the lender may do: ask for the money back or for more security, change the rate, or lend against nearly all of the value. The fix is **Borrow less, on safer terms**.
- When two answers show up in one story, one wins: *a claim* bigger than the insurance wins over properties in one name, and a business you run wins over a loan against its shares.
- Saying “it is already safe” is as much an answer as the rest, and it saves you paying for a fix you do not need.
