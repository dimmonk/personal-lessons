## Part 1 of 3: The three papers

### 1. Before you pay for “estate planning”, check what could actually go wrong

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 1 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient-handover`]

Before you pay for a family trust or “estate planning”, find out what, if anything, is actually at risk when your money passes to other people. It is one of four things, or none, and each needs a different fix.

You have heard these. “I’ve got a will somewhere.” “The IRS takes forty percent of everything over the limit.” “Those two haven’t spoken since the funeral.” “It’s all in order.” “Everyone says you need a family trust.”

Each is about the same moment: the day money passes to other people, or the day someone else has to act for its owner. One is about paper, one about tax, one about people, one says nothing is wrong, and one is somebody selling something. A fix for one does nothing for the others, and a fix bought for a problem you do not have costs money every year and solves nothing.

Four things can go wrong, and the fifth answer is that none of them does. That one is as common as the other four.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“What could lose this money?”** Its answers:

- **“Money going out every year”**: give this answer when money goes out of the pot every year: fees charged by funds or an adviser, tax on the investments, or a sum taken out to spend.
- **“One thing that could wipe it out”**: give this answer when most of the pot depends on one thing: one company or property that is most of it, a claim that could reach everything they own, or a lender who could force a sale.
- **“Prices falling at the wrong time”**: give this answer when a fall in prices would hurt because of what the money has to pay for or how it is split: living costs, a bill due on a set date, or a mix drifted from its plan.
- **“Handing it over”**: give this answer when the money passes to others when the owner dies, can no longer manage it or gives it away, and the question is who gets it, the tax on it, the papers, or the people. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“Nothing could lose it”**: give this answer when money is being kept, and none of the other four show up: no needless fee or tax, nothing most of it depends on, no bill or drifted mix, and no handover coming.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. A form that pays out without the will

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 2 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-benform`]

The first problem is about three papers. You know the will. The other two you may not have met.

*Nuala and the 401(k) form*

> Nuala, 45, pays into a 401(k) at work, run by an investment company. When she joined, she filled out a form for the company with one thing on it: who should receive the money if she dies. She wrote her husband's name. Ten years later she wrote a will, which leaves everything she owns to her two children. The company has never seen the will.

Nuala’s will says her two children. Her 401(k) form says her husband. When she dies, the company that runs the 401(k) will not go looking for her will: it pays the person its own form names, her husband.

In the US that form decides who gets the account, and a will that says something else does not change it. So someone can rewrite a will with great care and still leave a 401(k), an IRA, an insurance policy or a bank account paying a person they would no longer choose.

**The word for this.** *A beneficiary form*: a form held by the firm that runs a 401(k) or an IRA, by a life insurer or by a bank, that names who gets that account when its owner dies (a beneficiary designation). It is separate from the will, so changing the will does not change it.


### 3. The paper for the day you cannot sign

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 3 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-poa`]

The third paper is not about who gets the money. It is about who may act while the owner is still alive.

*Rashid and the stroke*

> Rashid, 63, runs his own money: he pays the bills, moves savings between accounts and signs the forms. One morning he has a stroke. He survives, but for months he cannot speak or sign his name. His wife wants to pay the mortgage from his savings account. The bank will not let her: the account is in his name alone, and he never signed anything that says she may act for him.

Nobody has died, and Rashid will get better, but for months nobody can act for him. The bank is not being difficult: it cannot know what he would want, because he never put it in a paper the bank can accept.

That paper is a signed document, made while he was well, naming someone to handle his money and affairs if he cannot. Without it, the family usually has to ask a court to appoint a guardian before anyone may act, and that takes time and money while the bills pile up.

So a handover is not only a death. It is any time someone else has to act for the owner.

**The word for this.** *A power of attorney*: a signed document naming someone who may handle your money and affairs if you cannot, for example after a stroke.


### 4. Update the basic paperwork

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 4 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-basicdocs`]

Start with Edith, and the will, the first of the three papers.

*Edith and the old will*

> Edith is 71. In 2008 she wrote ⟦a will that leaves everything she owns to her husband, Leo⟧, and names nobody else. ⟦Leo died four years ago⟧. She has not looked at the will since.

A will says who gets what you own when you die. It is read on the day by people who were not there when it was written, and it says exactly what it says. Edith’s will leaves everything to Leo and names nobody else, and Leo has died. If she died tomorrow, the law of her state would decide who gets her money, by rules written for everybody and not for her.

Papers go out of date when a marriage, a divorce, a birth or a death changes a life. A paper that was never written is the same problem. Both are cheap to fix now, and expensive for the people left to sort it out after the one person who knew what was meant can no longer say.

**How to spot it**

1. **Look for each of the three papers: a will, *a beneficiary form* and *a power of attorney*. Edith’s story shows one, a will from 2008.** Any one of the three can be the one that is wrong.
2. **Check who each paper names: Edith’s will names Leo.** The name on the paper gets the money, whatever the family expects.
3. **Check that person is still the right one: Leo died four years ago.** A death, a divorce, a marriage or a birth since the paper was written can make the name wrong.
4. **Check that none of the three is missing: Edith’s will names nobody to take over from Leo.** A paper that was never written leaves it to the state’s rules.

This is **Update the basic paperwork**. “Update” is the fix: bring the papers up to date, and write any that are missing.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **List your three papers and read the name on each against your life today: a former partner, someone who has died, a child born since.** Those are the names that go wrong.
2. **Where a name is wrong or a paper is missing, ask the company for its form or book a lawyer this week.** It is the cheapest fix, and everything bigger rests on it.

You may also hear this called “putting your affairs in order”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Update the basic paperwork**.

### 6. Nothing more needed

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 6 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-simple`]

The papers can also be in good order. That has a name too, and you will use it as often as the first.

*Anselm and Marit*

> Anselm and Marit are both 68 and retired. ⟦Their house and savings come to $410,000⟧, and their two children get on well. ⟦Last spring they rewrote both wills, renewed the beneficiary forms on both 401(k)s and signed new powers of attorney naming each other⟧. Everything they own is far below the tax-free limit for estate tax. At a seminar a man told them that every couple their age needs a family trust to save estate tax.

Ask what could go wrong, then look in the story for the words that show it. The papers are fine: all three were renewed. The tax is $0: the federal estate tax takes 40% only of what a person leaves above a tax-free limit, and that limit is many times larger than $410,000. The people are fine: the children get on well.

A family trust would cost money to set up and every year to run, to fix a problem nothing here shows. So the honest answer is that nothing more needs doing. That is a real answer, and as common as the others. It does not say that nothing could ever go wrong, only that this story shows nothing.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check every paper is current: Anselm and Marit renewed both wills, both 401(k) forms and their powers of attorney last spring.** One out-of-date paper makes it a different answer.
2. **Add up what they own and compare it with the tax-free limit: $410,000 is far below it.** Below the limit, estate tax is $0.
3. **Look at the people: their two children get on well.** Nothing is shown that could go wrong with them.
4. **Set the seminar speaker aside: he says they need a family trust, but he shows no problem for it to fix.** A sales pitch is not something that could go wrong.

This is **Nothing more needed**. The papers are current and nothing else is in question, so there is nothing more to set up.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Say no to the offer for now, and ask the seller: “What could go wrong in my situation that this fixes, in numbers?”** If the seller cannot say, you have your answer.
2. **Write down the date you checked your papers, and check again after a marriage, a divorce, a birth, a death or a big change in what you own.** Those are the moments a paper goes out of date.

### 8. Update the basic paperwork or Nothing more needed: telling them apart

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 8 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-basicdocs-simple`]

These two are easy to mix up: the same three papers, and the same kind of person.

**Story A**

> Rosalind is 59. She divorced six years ago, and since then she has lived in a condo worth $180,000, with $200,000 in savings. Her estate is far below the tax-free limit for estate tax. The beneficiary form on her 401(k) still names her former husband as the person who is to receive it if she dies.

**Story B**

> Rosalind is 59. She divorced six years ago, and since then she has lived in a condo worth $180,000, with $200,000 in savings. Her estate is far below the tax-free limit for estate tax. The month after the divorce she changed the beneficiary form on her 401(k) to name her daughter, rewrote her will and signed a power of attorney naming her daughter.

**What to compare.** Both stories are about Rosalind, who divorced six years ago and has the same condo and the same savings. Compare one thing: what each story says about her papers.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Nothing: the papers are up to date and nothing else is at stake”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the form on Rosalind’s 401(k) still names her former husband, so the plan would pay him if she died. That is **Update the basic paperwork**.

In Story B she changed the form the month after the divorce, and rewrote her will and signed *a power of attorney* at the same time. All three papers match her life, and the estate is far below the limit. That is **Nothing more needed**.

The divorce, the condo and the savings are the same in both. One sentence about one paper decides it, so read for that sentence before you decide.

**How to tell them apart**

Check the three papers one at a time: the will, any beneficiary form held by a 401(k) or IRA provider, an insurer or a bank, and the power of attorney. Is one missing or wrong for the person’s life now, or are all three right for it?

## Part 2 of 3: Tax on what you leave

### 9. Everything a person leaves

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 9 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-estate`]

The papers decide who gets the money. The next two problems are about tax taken before anyone gets anything, and to see them you need one word.

*Everything Ruth leaves*

> Ruth, 80, is a widow. She owns a house worth $4,000,000, $21,000,000 in investments and shares in her family's firm worth $5,000,000, and she owes nothing. The federal estate tax takes 40% of whatever a person leaves above a tax-free limit. She will leave everything to her two children.

Add up everything Ruth owns on the day she dies and you get one number: $30,000,000. That is what she leaves, her estate.

The federal estate tax does not tax all of it. It takes 40% of whatever is above a tax-free limit, which is so high that only the largest estates pay anything. Ruth’s $30,000,000 is above it, so every $1,000,000 above the limit costs $400,000. Someone who leaves $500,000 pays nothing.

**The word for this.** *An estate*: everything a person owns when they die. The federal estate tax takes a share of the part above a tax-free limit before the heirs get it, so only large estates pay it; some states tax smaller estates too.

The limit changes every year, so the stories in this unit say whether an estate is above it or below it, and use 40% for the part above.


### 10. Give some away each year

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 10 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-gifting`]

The first of the two tax problems, in the plainest story: a big sum above the limit, and nothing about it that is going to change.

*Harold’s savings*

> Harold is 78 and a widower. His house is worth $3,000,000 and his investments come to $37,000,000, ⟦so his estate is $40,000,000, well above the tax-free limit⟧. His investments pay him $1,000,000 a year and ⟦he spends about $600,000, so the savings grow every year and he has never touched them⟧. His will, forms and power of attorney were all renewed last year. The federal estate tax takes 40% of whatever a person leaves above a tax-free limit. He has three grandchildren, and ⟦nothing he owns is expected to change much in value⟧.

Everything Harold owns adds up to $40,000,000. That is *an estate* far above the limit, so every $1,000,000 above it costs $400,000. Nothing is taken while he lives. The tax comes once, when he dies, and his three grandchildren get the rest.

The tax is charged on what he leaves, so money he has already given away is not in the sum. Each year a person may give a set amount to each of as many people as they like, and a gift within it is not taxed and does not count against the limit. Harold gives each grandchild $10,000 a year, $30,000 in all. After ten years $300,000 has left his estate, and 40% of that, $120,000, is tax saved. Tuition or medical bills paid straight to the school or hospital do not count as gifts, so he can pay those too.

It is small in a year and large in a decade, so start early. It needs no trust and no company, and it costs nothing to run. A gift cannot be taken back, so give only what you truly do not need. The yearly amount and the limit change from time to time, and the idea stays the same.

**How to spot it**

1. **Add up what the owner will leave and compare it with the tax-free limit: Harold’s $40,000,000 is far above it.** Below the limit there is no tax to cut.
2. **Check there is money to spare: his income is $400,000 a year more than he spends.** Gifts only work with money the owner will never need.
3. **Check nothing he owns is about to shoot up in value: nothing is.** If something is, that rise is the bigger problem.
4. **Check the papers are current: his will, forms and power of attorney were renewed last year.** If a paper is out of date, fix that first.

This is **Give some away each year**: giving spare money away a little at a time, every year while the owner is alive, so that less is left to be taxed.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Add up everything the owner will leave and compare it with the federal tax-free limit (and your state’s, if it taxes estates).** Below the limit, nothing needs doing.
2. **Work out what the owner needs to live on for the rest of their life, with a margin.** Only what is left over is spare.
3. **Look up this year’s yearly gift amount, give the spare money within it every year, and write down who got what.** A record shows what has been given.

You may also hear this called “lifetime gifting”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Give some away each year**.

### 12. Money held by someone else for someone else

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 12 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-trustword`]

The second tax problem is often fixed with one tool. Here it is in its simplest form, before any tax comes into it.

*Beatrice and the condo money*

> Beatrice, 66, sells a condo for $240,000 and wants her three grandchildren to have the money, but not before each of them is 25. She signs a paper with a firm of lawyers. The paper says that the firm will hold the money, pay each grandchild a third when they turn 25, and may pay tuition in the meantime. The firm may not spend it on anything else.

Beatrice cannot make her grandchildren wait until 25 just by asking. The paper does it: the firm is the trustee, the paper’s rules are the terms, each grandchild gets $80,000 at 25, and until then the firm may spend the money on tuition and nothing else.

That is *a trust*: money held for other people under written terms. Nothing here is about tax. It is only a way of deciding who holds money and under what rules.

**The word for this.** *A trust*: a setup in which a person or firm, the trustee, holds money or property for other people under written terms.

A revocable living trust can be changed or ended by the owner at any time, and it saves no estate tax, because the owner still controls it. An irrevocable trust cannot be undone, and what is in it is no longer the owner’s.


### 13. Move it out of the estate before it grows

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 13 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-trust`]

The second tax problem, in a story where one thing the owner holds is about to become very valuable.

*Selim and the field*

> Selim is 59 and owns a farm. A field on the edge of town is worth $1,000,000. The county's new plan, published last month, ⟦zones the field for 120 houses⟧, and a developer has told him that ⟦once it is rezoned it would be worth about $30,000,000, probably within three years⟧. His house, the rest of the farm and his investments come to $25,000,000. His will, forms and power of attorney were renewed last year. The federal estate tax takes 40% of whatever a person leaves above a tax-free limit, and he is already above it.

Today Selim’s estate is $26,000,000, already above the limit. If the rezoning comes, the field is worth $30,000,000 and the estate $55,000,000. The rise of $29,000,000 brings $11,600,000 of new tax.

The tax is charged on what he owns on the day he dies, so if the field is not his that day, it is not in the sum. He moves the field out of his estate now, while it is worth $1,000,000. The move is a gift, and it uses up $1,000,000 of his tax-free limit. Then the rise happens to something that is not his. Timing is the whole idea: after the rezoning, moving it out would be a gift of $30,000,000. Small yearly gifts, as in **Give some away each year**, could never move a field.

It is not free. Only an irrevocable trust, which the owner cannot undo, takes something out of the estate, so control passes to a trustee for good. It costs money to set up and every year to run, and it needs a specialist lawyer. It is worth it only when the tax saved is far more.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find something the owner holds that is expected to be worth many times more: Selim’s field, zoned for 120 houses.** A business or land is the usual example.
2. **Find the evidence it will happen: the county’s new plan, and a developer who says $30,000,000 within three years.** A hope is not enough; a plan or an offer is.
3. **Add up the estate before and after the rise: $26,000,000 now, $55,000,000 after.** The difference shows how much new tax the rise brings.
4. **Check the papers are current: Selim’s were renewed last year.** If a paper is out of date, fix that first.

This is **Move it out of the estate before it grows**: the thing is moved out of the estate while it is still worth little, so the rise happens outside it. The usual way is *a trust*, or a company the family sets up to own it.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **List what you hold that could soon be worth many times more, and write down the evidence: a plan, an agreement, an offer.** Without evidence there is nothing to act on.
2. **Before you move anything, ask a specialist lawyer what the move costs in tax and fees, what it costs each year, and what control you give up.** The saving has to be far bigger than all three.
3. **If the saving is far bigger, move it before the rise.** After the rise, the same move is a much bigger gift.

You may also hear this called “putting it in a trust” or “a family holding company”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Move it out of the estate before it grows**.

### 14. A question about a new story

### 16. Family rules for the money

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 16 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-governance`]

The tax problems were about a sum set against a limit. This one is about a person: the papers are fine, and tax is not the problem.

*Wilf and Joel*

> Wilf is 68 and a widower. He will leave $450,000 to his son Joel, 29, in one payment on the day he dies. ⟦Joel has had three jobs in two years and has borrowed money from his father four times, none of it repaid⟧. Wilf's will, forms and power of attorney are all current. His estate is far below the tax-free limit for estate tax.

The papers are right, the money is safe from tax, and nobody accuses Joel of anything. But the story shows a pattern, and then hands him $450,000 in one payment with nobody able to say no. That is a risk in the person who will get the money. It is not a prediction that Joel will lose it. It is a reason to act, and the way the money is handed over can do something about it.

If $450,000 arrives in one day, it all arrives together. If it is paid in three parts, $150,000 at 30, 35 and 40, then even if the first is gone within a year, $300,000 is still to come. Other ways to put rules around money are an outside person (a trustee) who decides, with a veto where that matters, and written agreements, such as a prenuptial agreement about an inheritance.

The risk can also be control: two heirs who will not speak, and a will that needs both signatures before anything is done, can leave a business standing still. Rules are far easier to agree while everyone is alive and talking. A rule made with Joel and explained to him protects him as much as the money.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the person who will get or run the money: Joel, 29.** This one is about a person, not a sum.
2. **Find what they have done with money or work: three jobs in two years, and four loans from his father, none repaid.** A pattern is what makes it a risk.
3. **Find how the money will arrive: $450,000, all in one payment, with nobody able to say no.** A pattern plus a lump sum is what puts the money at risk.
4. **Check the papers and the tax: Wilf’s papers are current and his estate is far below the limit.** If a paper is wrong, fix that first.

This is **Family rules for the money**: written rules, agreed ahead of time, about who decides and when each person is paid, so that a risk in the people is met in the terms of the handover.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Name the person, or the pair, and write down the words that show the risk.** If you cannot find the words, there may be no risk.
2. **Talk to them early and say why.** A rule agreed with someone protects them as well as the money.
3. **Write the rules into the papers: payments in stages, an outside person to decide where two must agree, agreements about marriage where that is the risk.** A rule that is not written down will not be followed after you are gone.

You may also hear this called “family governance”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Family rules for the money**.

### 19. The question to ask every time

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 19 of 23*

**The question:** **“What could go wrong when it is handed over?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other four.

- **“The will or other papers are out of date or missing”**

  - It leads to **Update the basic paperwork**.
- **“Estate tax, with money to spare”**
  - Give this answer when the estate is above the tax-free limit for estate tax, the owner has more than they will need, and nothing in it is expected to shoot up in value.
  - It leads to **Give some away each year**.
- **“Estate tax on something about to shoot up in value”**
  - Give this answer when something the owner holds is expected to shoot up in value, and the estate is or will then be above the tax-free limit, so the rise would be taxed at death.

- **“Nothing: the papers are up to date and nothing else is at stake”**
  - Give this answer when the will, beneficiary forms and power of attorney are up to date, the estate is below the tax-free limit or nothing is at stake, and the people raise no worry.

**Why it matters**

Each answer has its own fix, and one fix does nothing for another: new papers do not lower a tax bill, and a gift does not stop heirs falling out. Anything set up for a problem nobody has costs money every year and solves nothing.

2. **First check the three papers: is any one missing, or wrong for the person’s life now?** If so, the answer is **“The will or other papers are out of date or missing”**, however big the estate is.
3. **Next look for something the owner holds that is about to shoot up in value.** If there is one, the answer is **“Estate tax on something about to shoot up in value”**.
4. **Next add up the estate, compare it with the limit, and look for money to spare.** Above the limit with money to spare, and nothing about to shoot up, is **“Estate tax, with money to spare”**.
5. **Next look at the people: an heir in trouble with money, a marriage about to start or end, heirs who will not agree.** That is **“The people who will get it or run it”**.
6. **If none of the four is there, look for the words that show every paper is current.** That is **“Nothing: the papers are up to date and nothing else is at stake”**.
7. **Find the exact words that show your answer.** If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.

### 22. What to carry away

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What could go wrong when it is handed over?
- The will or other papers are out of date or missing → Update the basic paperwork
- Estate tax, with money to spare → Give some away each year
- Estate tax on something about to shoot up in value → Move it out of the estate before it grows

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Update the basic paperwork**: a will, a beneficiary form or a power of attorney that is missing or out of date, like one written before a marriage, a divorce or a birth.
- **Nothing more needed**: a will, beneficiary forms and a power of attorney all up to date, an estate below the tax-free limit or with nothing at stake, and nothing worrying about the people.
- **Give some away each year**: an estate above the tax-free limit, more than the owner will need to live on, and nothing in it expected to shoot up in value.
- **Move it out of the estate before it grows**: something the owner holds, such as a business or land, that is expected to shoot up in value, and an estate that is or will then be above the tax-free limit.
- **Family rules for the money**: a risk in the people who will get or run the money, like an heir in a failing marriage or who has struggled with money, or heirs who do not speak to each other.

**To carry away**

- Before any fix, ask what could go wrong at the handover, and find the words in the story that show it. If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.
- Check the papers first. A will, *a beneficiary form* or *a power of attorney* that is missing, or wrong for the person’s life now, is the answer whatever else the story holds. It is the cheapest thing to fix, and everything else rests on it.
- Tax has a limit. Below it, the tax is nothing. Above it, ask first whether something the owner holds is about to shoot up in value: if it is, that rise is the answer. If nothing is, and there is money to spare, it is **“Estate tax, with money to spare”**.
- A risk in the people is its own answer. It needs words that show it, and it is met by written rules, agreed early.
- **“Nothing: the papers are up to date and nothing else is at stake”** is a real answer, and a common one. It needs words that show the papers are current. If you cannot find anything that could go wrong, do not invent it, and do not buy *a trust* or a company for a problem the story does not have.
- Holding money in *a trust* is a tool, not an answer. It is used for **Move it out of the estate before it grows**, and it can be used for other things.
