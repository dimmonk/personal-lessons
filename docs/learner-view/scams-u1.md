## Part 1 of 3: Two habits, then news and sign-in requests

### 1. Before you tap, call, pay or reply, check what the message asks

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 1 of 25*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient-gate`]

Before you tap a link, call a number, pay or reply, check what the message actually asks you to do. It is always one of the five things below, it is written in the message, and you can read it before you do anything.

Your phone buzzes with a text that says it is from your bank. Before you have read to the end, you are already asking yourself what it wants: for you to tap something, call someone, pay something, or only to know something.

That is the right question, and a scam is built to make you skip it. It wants you asking who it is from and how bad the problem is. A real bank and a copy of it can use the same name, logo and words. What the message asks you to do is the one thing you can read in it, and it shows what you could lose: your phone or computer, an account, your money, or facts about you.

The message can be real or a copy. This unit does not decide that. It teaches the question that comes first.

What is it asking you to do right now?
- Install something, open a file, or share your screen → a way onto your phone or computer
- Sign in, give a code, or allow an app → a way into one of your accounts
- Pay or send money → money out of your account
- Tell them about yourself → facts about you
- Nothing: it only tells you something → news that asks nothing of you

The unit has three parts, and you can stop after any of them.

1. Two habits, then news and sign-in requests
2. Your device, your money, and facts about you
3. Putting it together, then the drill

Each starts from a real story. After each one you answer a quick question, and the reason is shown right away. Nothing here is graded. A miss only decides what comes back.

### 2. Where a number, a link or an app comes from

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 2 of 25*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-already`]

One idea comes first, because every answer in this unit uses it.

*The number on the card*

> Mina gets a text: 'This is Halbrook Bank. We have stopped a payment on your account. Call (800) 555-0134 now.' She takes her bank card out of her wallet. The number printed on the back of the card is (800) 555-0178. She calls that number instead, and the bank tells her there is nothing wrong with her account.

Anyone can put any number in a text, so Mina could not know the first one was the bank’s. The number on her card was in her wallet before the text arrived, so she could trust it.

Numbers, links and apps come in two sorts: the ones that came with the message, and the ones that were yours first. Only the ones that were yours first lead to the real company. Anything that came with the message does not count, even if you are the one who dials it or taps it.

**The word for this.** *A way you already had*: a phone number, an app or a web address that was yours before the message arrived: the number on your card, bill or contract, an app you installed yourself, an address you type in or bookmarked, or the company’s own office in person. A number, link or app that came with the message is never one, even if you are the one who dials it.


### 3. What to do when you are not sure

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 3 of 25*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-check`]

When you are not sure about a message, *a way you already had* gives you one dependable way to settle it. That way has a name.

*The manager’s email*

> An email arrives that looks as if it is from Tom's manager: 'I am in a meeting and cannot reach the bank. Please wire $2,000 to this account for a supplier today.' Tom does not reply to it and does not wire anything. He calls his manager at the number in the staff directory and asks whether she sent it. She did not.

Tom did not have to judge whether the email looked real. He did not do what it asked and he did not reply to it. He called his manager on the number in the staff directory, which is *a way you already had*, because only the person an email names can say whether she wrote it.

A real person or company does not mind being asked this way. If someone gets angry, or hurries you, when you want to ask, that tells you something too.

**The word for this.** *The check*: stopping before you do what a message asks, and contacting the company or person yourself through a way you already had, to ask whether the request is real.


### 4. Nothing: it only tells you something

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 4 of 25*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-nothing`]

First: a message that asks nothing of you. It only gives you news, and it is the easiest one to forget.

*The delivery update*

> A text arrives on Ruth's phone from the bookstore where she ordered a birthday present: 'Hartley Books: ⟦your order is out for delivery today. Expected between 1pm and 4pm⟧.' The text says nothing else.

Ruth’s text is news: something will happen, the text says so, and it stops. News can be about a delivery, an appointment, a closed office, a new sign-in on your account or a refund that went through. It can sound serious, and it can mention money. If you are not asked to do anything about it, it belongs here.

It may suggest a next step, as long as the step uses only *a way you already had*, like your own app or the number on your card.

Real warnings land here. If you treated every message as suspicious, you would stop reading the real ones too.

**How to spot it**

1. **Look for a request: Ruth’s text only says her order is out for delivery.** News says what is happening, and a request says what you must do.
2. **Look for a link, number or app that came with it: Ruth’s text has none.** That is how a message gets you to act.
3. **Ask what happens if you do nothing: Ruth’s order still arrives.** If nothing depends on you, nothing is being asked.

This is **“Nothing: it only tells you something”**. Ruth could ignore the text and nothing would change.

### 6. The six-digit number your phone shows you

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 6 of 25*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-code`]

The next answer is about a way into one of your accounts. Two words describe the ways, and the first comes now.

*The code that works once*

> Ana changes the password on her email account. A moment later her phone buzzes: 'Your code is 902 114. It works once, for the next ten minutes.' She types the six numbers into the box on the page, and the page lets her in. If she tried the same numbers an hour later, they would no longer work.

A number like this is sent when you do something important: sign in, change a password or pay. It shows that whoever types it also has your phone or your email.

It works once, for a few minutes, so it is useless a day later. For those few minutes it works for anyone who has it, which is why scammers want it.

**The word for this.** *One-time code*: a short number that a company texts or emails you to prove that it really is you signing in or paying. It works once, for a few minutes.


### 7. The box that asks you to press Allow

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 7 of 25*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-permission`]

The second word is about a way into an account that uses no password at all.

*The flight bookings app*

> Leo wants an app called Notewise to pick out his flight bookings from his email. His email provider shows him a box: 'Notewise would like to read your mail and your calendar. Allow or Cancel.' He presses Allow. He has not typed his email password into Notewise.

Leo never typed his password into the app. The box came from his email provider, and pressing Allow gave the app its own way into his account.

This is how most apps connect to accounts, and it is useful. But Allow opens a door that stays open until you shut it, even if you never sign in again.

**The word for this.** *Permission screen*: a screen shown by your email or another account that asks you to press Allow, so that an app can read or use your account without knowing your password.


### 8. Sign in, give a code, or allow an app

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 8 of 25*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-access`]

Second: a request for a way into one of your accounts.

*The retirement plan sign-in*

> Tariq wants to look at his retirement plan. He types the retirement plan company's web address into his browser himself, and the page says: '⟦Sign in with your username and password⟧.' He types them in.

Tariq typed the company’s address himself, and the page asks for his username and password. That is a request for a way into his account. A message can ask for it in three ways: to sign in, to give a *one-time code* by typing it, reading it out or passing it on, or to press Allow on a *permission screen*.

A way in is worth a lot. Whoever gets into your email can read it, and can use it to reset the password of nearly every other account you have. And a way in can be used again and again until someone shuts it.

**How to spot it**

1. **Look for a password: Tariq’s page asks for his username and password.** Whoever has them can sign in as him.
2. **Look for a code you are asked to type, read out or pass on: Tariq is asked for none.** Whoever holds the code can open the account.
3. **Look for an Allow button: Tariq sees none.** Allow lets an app into your account without a password.

This is **“Sign in, give a code, or allow an app”**. Tariq’s sign-in is real, and a copy would ask for exactly the same thing.

### 10. Sign in, give a code, or allow an app or Nothing: it only tells you something: telling them apart

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 10 of 25*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-access-nothing`]

A real notice and a copy of it can read almost the same. These two answers are easy to mix up in just that way.

**Story A**

> Priya opens her Hartley Mail app and finds this notice in its own message list: 'A new device signed in to your account at 2:02 p.m. today. If this was you, you do not need to do anything. If it was not, open this app and choose Security.'

**Story B**

> Priya gets an email that looks the same: 'A new device signed in to your Hartley Mail account at 2:02 p.m. today. If this was not you, sign in here to secure your account: hartleymail-secure.com/signin'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about Priya’s email account and a sign-in from a new device. Compare one thing: does the message ask her to do something, or only tell her?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Sign in, give a code, or allow an app”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

Story A is a notice inside Priya’s own mail app. It only tells her what happened, and if it was not her, she can open the app she is already in and choose Security. No link, number or sign-in page came with it. That is **“Nothing: it only tells you something”**.

Story B is an email that says almost the same, then adds “sign in here to secure your account”, with an address. Now she is asked to sign in, and the way to do it came with the message. That is **“Sign in, give a code, or allow an app”**.

You cannot tell them apart by how they start, which company they name or how serious they sound. Look at what each one asks, and whether what it asks you to use came with the message.

**How to tell them apart**

Does anything in it ask you to sign in, give a code or press Allow, through a link, a button or a caller? Or does it only tell you what happened, and leave you to use your own app or number?

## Part 2 of 3: Your device, your money, and facts about you

### 11. Letting someone watch what you are doing

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 11 of 25*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-screenshare`]

The next answer is about your phone or computer itself. One request there is hard to picture until you have seen it.

*The help desk*

> Jo's laptop will not connect to the office printer. She calls the company help desk at the number printed on her work badge. The helper says: 'Open the meeting app and press Share my screen, so that I can see what you see.' For the next ten minutes he watches her laptop and tells her what to click.

Many real help desks work this way, and it is a lot to hand over. From the moment Jo pressed the button, the helper could see everything she saw, including her email and her bank pages whenever she opened them, until she stopped it.

**The word for this.** *Screen-sharing*: letting someone else see, or even control, your phone or computer screen from far away, through an app or a code you type in.


### 12. Install something, open a file, or share your screen

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 12 of 25*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-device`]

Third: a request about your phone or computer itself.

*The support call*

> A man calls Diane and says he is from her internet company. 'Your router has been sending out errors,' he says. 'Please open your browser and ⟦download the repair program from the address I am about to read out⟧. Then I can fix it from my end.'

Diane’s caller says her router has a fault, and asks her to download a program from an address he reads out. That is a request about her computer itself. It can come in three ways: to install a program or an app, to open or run a file such as an attachment, or to let someone watch or control your device from far away (*screen-sharing*).

This reaches further than any other answer. Once a program is installed, or someone can watch your screen, the risk is not one account but everything on the device, and it can last after the call ends.

**How to spot it**

1. **Look for a program to install, a file to open or a screen to share: Diane is told to download a repair program.** Those are the three requests about a device.
2. **Look for an offer to fix your device: a man calls Diane about her router.** The person you reach will ask you to install something or share your screen.
3. **Look at what is not asked: no password, no money, no facts about Diane.** Then what is left is something going onto her computer.

This is **“Install something, open a file, or share your screen”**. The caller could even be a real engineer, and the answer would be the same.

### 14. Install something, open a file, or share your screen or Sign in, give a code, or allow an app: telling them apart

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 14 of 25*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-device-access`]

In both of these, a box asks you to allow something, and the two boxes look alike.

**Story A**

> Ravi has downloaded Pixelwise, a photo editor, from the maker's own website. He opens the installer, and a box says: 'Install Pixelwise on this computer? Do you want to allow this app to make changes to your device?' with Yes and No.

**Story B**

> Ravi would rather use the web version. He opens Pixelwise Online, and his email account shows a box: 'Pixelwise Online would like to read and send mail for you. Allow / Cancel.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about Ravi and a photo editor. Compare one thing: would pressing the button put a program on his computer, or let an app into his account?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Install something, open a file, or share your screen”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story A.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A, Ravi has the program file from the maker’s website, and the box asks him to install it on his computer. That puts a program on his device. That is **“Install something, open a file, or share your screen”**.

In Story B, he uses the web version, and the box comes from his email account and asks to let an app read and send his mail. Nothing goes onto his computer: an app is let into an account. That is **“Sign in, give a code, or allow an app”**.

Both boxes say “allow” and name the same app. One puts something on the device. The other opens an account to an app.

**How to tell them apart**

Would pressing it put something onto my phone or computer, open something on it, or let someone watch it? Or would it open one of my accounts to a page, an app or a caller?


### 15. Pay or send money

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 15 of 25*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-money`]

Fourth: a request for money. It is the one most people think of first.

*The rent email*

> Dan's landlord emails him on the 25th: 'Hi Dan, ⟦October's rent of $850 is due on the 1st. Please pay it into the same account as usual⟧.'

Dan’s landlord asks for $850 by the 1st, into an account. That is a request to pay. Every way of paying counts: a transfer, a card, cash, crypto, gift cards, or a payment page you reach from a link.

Money gets its own answer because it is the hardest thing to get back. An account can be shut and a program removed, but money sent by transfer, in cash or on gift cards is usually gone.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the amount and the date: $850 by the 1st.** A request for money names a sum, or tells you to send one.
2. **Find how to pay: into the same account as usual.** A transfer, a card, cash, crypto and gift cards all count.
3. **Look at what is not asked: no password, no program, no facts about Dan.** Then the only request is for money to leave his account.

This is **“Pay or send money”**. Dan’s rent is real, and a scam can ask for money in just the same way.

### 21. The one question to ask first

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 21 of 25*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-gate`]

Here is the question and its five answers in one place.

**The question:** **“What is it asking you to do right now?”**

**Its answers**

- **“Install something, open a file, or share your screen”**
  - Give this answer when it asks you to install something, open or run a file, or let someone see or control your screen, or it warns of a device problem and gives you someone to call.
- **“Sign in, give a code, or allow an app”**
  - Give this answer when it asks you to sign in, to give a one-time code by typing it, reading it out or passing it on, or to press Allow so that an app can use one of your accounts.
- **“Pay or send money”**
  - Give this answer when it asks you to pay or send money, by any means, now or by a date.
- **“Tell them about yourself”**
  - Give this answer when it asks for facts about you: a document, an ID or card number, your date of birth or address, or your work, home and family.
- **“Nothing: it only tells you something”**
  - Give this answer when it tells you something has happened or will happen and asks you to do nothing, and anything it suggests uses only what you already had, like the number on your card.

**Why it matters**

Each one puts something different at risk: your whole phone or computer, an account, your money, or facts about you. Who it says it is from and how bad the problem sounds do not change the answer; what it asks you to do does, and you can read that in the message or hear it on the call. If it asks for two of these, give the one higher in the list: a program or a look at your screen reaches everything on the device, a way into an account reaches what the account holds, and money is gone once it is sent.

Skip this question and you end up judging how real a message looks, which is the one thing a scam is built to get right.

**How to answer it**

1. **Read the whole message, last sentence included.** The request is often at the end, after the reason it gives.
2. **First look for “Install something, open a file, or share your screen”.** It reaches everything on your phone or computer.
3. **Next look for “Sign in, give a code, or allow an app”.** It reaches what the account holds, money included.
4. **Next look for “Pay or send money”.** Money is usually gone once it is sent.
5. **Next look for “Tell them about yourself”.** Facts about you get used later.
6. **If none of those four is there, it is “Nothing: it only tells you something”.** Then nothing is being asked.
7. **Find the exact words that show your answer.** If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Some messages ask for two things at once: the refund call asked Harold to let a stranger watch his screen and then to send money back. Each message gets one answer, and the one higher in the list wins. The test for each pair is below.

- Sign in, give a code, or allow an app or Nothing: it only tells you something: Does anything in it ask you to sign in, give a code or press Allow, through a link, a button or a caller? Or does it only tell you what happened, and leave you to use your own app or number?
- Install something, open a file, or share your screen or Sign in, give a code, or allow an app: Would pressing it put something onto my phone or computer, open something on it, or let someone watch it? Or would it open one of my accounts to a page, an app or a caller?
- Pay or send money or Tell them about yourself: Is an amount named for me to pay? Or am I only asked to tell them facts about myself, like my name, my date of birth or my card number?
- Install something, open a file, or share your screen or Pay or send money: Am I asked to install, open or share something on my phone or computer, whatever else is asked? Or is paying the only thing asked?
- Sign in, give a code, or allow an app or Pay or send money: Am I asked to sign in, give a code or press Allow first, whatever the payment is for? Or is paying the only thing asked?
- Tell them about yourself or Nothing: it only tells you something: Is there a question about me in it, like my name, my date of birth, my work or where I live? Or does it only tell me something?
- Install something, open a file, or share your screen or Nothing: it only tells you something: Does it ask me to install, open or share something, perhaps with a file, an update or a number of its own? Or does it only tell me that something will happen?

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **“Install something, open a file, or share your screen”**: a request to install something, open a file, or let someone see or control your screen, or a warning telling you to call someone to fix your device.
- **“Sign in, give a code, or allow an app”**: a request to type your password into a sign-in page, to type, read out or pass on a one-time code, or to press Allow on a permission screen.
- **“Pay or send money”**: a request to pay or send money, by any means: a transfer, a card payment, cash, crypto or gift cards.
- **“Tell them about yourself”**: a request for facts about you: papers or numbers that prove who you are, your date of birth or address, or your work, home and family in a friendly chat.
- **“Nothing: it only tells you something”**: news about something that has happened or will happen, and no request: nothing to install, sign in to, pay or tell, and no number or link of its own.

**To carry away**

- Before you tap, call, pay or reply, find the words that show what the message asks you to do. If you cannot find a request, you do not have an answer yet.
- The answer is not a verdict. Each of the five can be real or a copy, and how a message looks, who it says it is from and how well it is written change nothing. Telling real from copy is the job of what comes next, and of *the check*.
- A message that only tells you something is **“Nothing: it only tells you something”**. Treating it as a threat is a mistake too.
- A number, a link or an app that came with the message is never *a way you already had*, even when you are the one who dials or taps it.
- When a message asks for two things, the one higher in the list wins: your device, then an account, then money, then facts about you.
