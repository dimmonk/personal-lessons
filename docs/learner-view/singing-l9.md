# Singing: A breath that lasts the line

Revision 1, live. Part: A breath that lasts the line. Where the subject ends up: Sing a tune you have just heard, or a line of a song you know, in tune and at a height that suits your voice, with high notes that come out light instead of shouted; and when a line goes wrong, say which of a few things went wrong and what to try, or that nothing did.

## The why

You may have been told there is one right way to breathe for singing: belly out, shoulders still. Studies of trained singers found no such thing. Good singers breathe in very different ways, and each is steady in their own.
So judge a breath by what it does: does the air last the line, with no gasp and no squeezing out the last of it?

## Step 2: What the air can do

This is the fifth check, after the notes are right. Trained singers start a line with a good breath, not the most they can hold, and they stop well before the air is gone. Filling right to the top, or singing on until nothing is left, takes more effort.

In a line, the air can do five things:
Lasts the line: some air is left at the end, with no gasp. Leave it alone, however your body moves.
A gasped breath: snatched in a hurry at the last moment, small and noisy, and gone before the line ends.
Pressing on with no air left: you keep singing after the air is used up, and the last words come out thin or tight.
Breathing in the middle of words: full breaths, but taken wherever you happen to run out.
Air leaking out with the sound: you can hear air with your voice, soft and whispery, and the breath goes fast. A rough first try, not a tested fix: aim for a clearer sound with less air in it. If the airy sound is new, or does not clear, it counts as a hoarse voice: rest it, and see a doctor if it is not getting better in 7 to 10 days.

For the gasp, the empty end and the breath in a word, the sensible plan is the same: plan your breaths. Read the words first, mark a breath at the end of a line, and take it there, before you run out.

| A | B |
|---|---|
| A gasped breath: the breath itself is small and hurried. | Breathing in the middle of words: the breath is full, the place is wrong. |

Compare: Look at the breath first, then at where it was taken.

## Step 3: A worked example

Tom sings a song he loves, and every note matches the recording. His breaths are full and quiet, but he takes them wherever he runs out: in the chorus he breathes in the middle of the word “forever”.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**What is the air doing?**
- A breath that lasts the line
- A gasped breath
- Pressing on with no air left
- Breathing in the middle of words
- Air leaking out with the sound

The working, one step at a time:
1. Are the notes right? They match the recording, so the earlier checks are clear.
1. Is the breath itself the trouble? No: each one is full and quiet.
1. Where does he breathe? Wherever he runs out, even in the middle of a word.

Then the answer: Breathing in the middle of words. Full breaths in the wrong places: breathing in the middle of words. Read the words first and mark a breath at the end of each line, then breathe only there.

## Step 5: One breath, one long note

Now take one easy breath and hold a note for 6 seconds, on “oo”, at an even loudness. First with the line, then without it, saying where your note ended before you see it. The app measures only the note: whether it stayed on for the 6 seconds. Whether you had air left at the end is yours to notice. If the air ran out before 6 seconds, do not squeeze: breathe a little earlier and a little fuller next time.

#### First: Find your range (a sung question; nothing to match)

**Find your range. You will hum a slide up, then a slide down. Keep it easy: stop at the notes that still feel comfortable.**

- Before the tap: one button, "Start". The microphone is off and nothing sounds. The screen says: The microphone listens only while you sing. The sound is checked on this device and thrown away: nothing is recorded or sent anywhere.

- The two windows say: "Slide up to the highest note that is easy, and hold it." then "Now slide down to the lowest note that is easy, and hold it."
