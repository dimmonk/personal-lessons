## Part 1 of 2: Nothing to compare with, and a group picked at its worst

### 1. Before you believe “it worked”, check what else could explain it

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

When a headline, an ad or a friend says one thing caused another, look for other explanations before you believe it or copy it. There are four other explanations to look for, and each needs a different check.

You hear this all the time. “Since we started the program, results are up, so it works.” “People who take the stairs live longer, so take the stairs.” “Everyone who tried the cream says it cleared up their skin.” Each of these might be right.

But a result can happen in more than one way. This unit teaches the four other ways, how to spot each one in the words of a claim, and what a claim looks like when none of them is open: groups formed by a draw can be believed.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“Where does this claim first go wrong?”** Its answers:

- **“Who was counted”**: give this answer when the people or things the figure came from do not match the group the claim is about, or are too few to trust.
- **“What the number counts”**: give this answer when the figure could rise, fall or differ without the real thing it is taken to show doing the same.
- **“What it is compared with”**: give this answer when the figure is a percentage change, a test’s accuracy, or a total beside another total, and the claim leaves out what you need beside it to read it fairly.
- **“What caused what”**: give this answer when the claim says one thing made another happen, and the same result could have come about another way. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“Nothing goes wrong”**: give this answer when every part the claim uses checks out in order, and none of them goes wrong.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

*(One tap on any of these lines opens the card in Unit One that taught it.)*

What else could explain the result?
- It would have happened anyway → No comparison group
- Something else causes both → Confounding
- The second thing causes the first → Reverse causation
- Picked at its worst or best, it drifts back to normal → Regression to the mean

- No comparison group: nothing to show what would happen without it
- Regression to the mean: picked at its worst or best, then drifting back to normal
- Confounding: something else behind both
- Reverse causation: the result causing the thing, not the other way around

2. Groups that picked their own side, a result that came first, and one whole claim

### 2. No comparison group

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-nocontrol`]

Someone says an app, a program or a habit worked. Before you believe it, ask: **“What else could explain the result?”**

*The sleep app*

> A sleep clinic says: 'Our new bedtime app works. Of the 80 patients who used it for a month, 64 now sleep better.' All 80 patients answered the clinic’s follow-up questions. ⟦The clinic did not follow any patients who went without the app⟧.

The clinic says 64 of 80 patients sleep better after a month with the app. But people go to a sleep clinic when they are sleeping badly, and bad stretches of sleep often end by themselves. If most of them would have slept better anyway, 64 out of 80 tells you very little about the app.

Suppose the clinic had also followed 80 similar patients who went without the app. If 24 of them slept better, the app added a lot: from 3 in 10 up to 8 in 10. If 56 did, it added little: from 7 in 10 up to 8 in 10. The clinic’s 64 out of 80 looks the same in both worlds, so it cannot tell you which one is real. That does not make the app useless. It means these figures cannot show that it works.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the claim that it worked: “Our new bedtime app works.”** Words like “works”, “helped” and “since we started” are where this check begins.
2. **Find who the result is for: the 80 patients who used the app.** Check whether it covers only the people who got the thing, or only one place before and after.
3. **Look for a group that went without: there is none.** Without one, nobody can say how many would have slept better anyway.

This is **No comparison group**. A comparison group is a second group, like the first, that did not get the thing and was counted the same way.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Ask what happened to people who did not get it, such as patients who went without the app.** Their result is the one to set beside the clinic’s 64 out of 80.
2. **If the claim does not say, treat it as not shown.** Not shown is not the same as false.

You may also hear this called “no control group”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **No comparison group**.

### 4. Regression to the mean

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-regression`]

Next: a group picked because it was doing badly, and then given something.

*The class quiz*

> A teacher gives her ten students a quiz. ⟦The three lowest scorers, who average 46, get a week of extra tutoring⟧. On an equally hard quiz the next week, the three tutored students average 55. 'The tutoring worked,' she says. 'They gained 9 points.'

The three students were picked because they scored lowest. That alone makes their rise hard to trust. A quiz score is what a student knows plus how the day went, and some of those three had a bad day. The next quiz is a new day, so their scores move back up toward where they usually are, with no tutoring at all.

Here is what happens with no tutoring. A class of ten sat the same quiz two weeks running, with no help in between. The three lowest first scores (40, 46 and 52) averaged 46. The same three scored 50, 55 and 60 the next week, an average of 55: a gain of 9 points with nobody doing anything. The three highest averaged 88, then 82. The whole class stayed at about 67. So the teacher’s 9 points is what any low-scoring group shows, and her figure cannot tell tutoring from no tutoring.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find how the group was picked: the three lowest scorers.** A group picked for its worst (or best) result is the one that moves back.
2. **Find the change afterward: the three went from 46 to 55.** A move back toward usual is what you would see with nothing done.
3. **Check who gets the credit: “The tutoring worked.”** The move back is being read as the effect of what was done in between.

This is **Regression to the mean**. “The mean” is the plain average, the usual level, and “regression” means going back.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Ask how the group was picked.** If it was picked for its worst or best result, expect it to move back with nothing done.
2. **Ask for a group just as extreme that was left alone, such as three other low scorers with no tutoring.** Only that group shows how much of the rise is the tutoring.

You may also hear this called “drifting back to normal”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Regression to the mean**.

## Part 2 of 2: Groups that picked their own side, a result that came first, and one whole claim

### 7. Confounding

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 7 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-confound`]

Next: two groups side by side, where people picked their own side.

*The recovery shake*

> A supplement company says: 'Lifters who take our recovery shake add 38 pounds more to their squat in a year than other lifters, so the shake builds strength.' The figures are from 400 lifters at one gym chain. The 100 who chose to buy the shake gained an average of 70 pounds on their squat, and the 300 who did not gained an average of 32. ⟦The gym’s logs show that 75 of the 100 shake buyers train four or more days a week, and 45 of the 300 others do⟧.

The lifters who bought the shake are mostly frequent trainers: 75 of the 100 train four or more days a week, against 45 of the other 300. Frequent training builds a strong squat on its own, whatever you drink afterward. So the shake and the big gain both come with frequent training, and the figures cannot say which one did the work.

Compare lifters who train alike. Among the 120 who train four or more days a week, shake buyers gained 85 pounds and the others 83. Among the 280 who train less, buyers gained 25 and the others 23. The company’s 38 pounds shrinks to 2. About 36 of the 38 was the training. The shake may be worth 2 pounds, but the company’s figure cannot show even that.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the two groups: 100 lifters who bought the shake and 300 who did not.** The claim sets one group beside the other.
2. **Check who put them there: each lifter chose whether to buy.** When people choose, the groups can differ in more than the thing.
3. **Look for something else that differs: 75 of the 100 buyers train four or more days a week, against 45 of the 300.** If that could produce the result by itself, the claim has not shown the thing did.

This is **Confounding**. To “confound” is to mix up: the shake and the training are mixed up in the figures.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Name one other way the two groups differ, such as how often they train.** That is the thing that could have produced the result.
2. **Ask for people who are alike in that other thing, or for a test where a draw formed the groups.** Then the only difference left is the thing itself.
3. **If the claim does not rule it out, do not act on the claim.** It has not earned your belief yet.

You may also hear this called “a third factor” or “a confounding factor”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Confounding**.

### 9. Reverse causation

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 9 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-reverse`]

Next: two things that go together, where the arrow may point the wrong way.

*The security cameras*

> A neighborhood study finds that on the 12 streets where over half the homes have a security camera, 9 had a break-in last year. On the 12 streets where fewer than 1 in 10 homes have one, 3 did. A local columnist writes: 'Cameras bring burglars to a street.' On 7 of the 9 camera-heavy streets with a break-in, ⟦residents say the first cameras went up after the break-in⟧.

When someone says one thing caused another, they draw an arrow from the first to the second. The figures do not draw it: they are a snapshot. 9 of 12 camera-heavy streets had a break-in, against 3 of 12 streets with few cameras, so the two really do go together. But a snapshot shows no order.

The residents supply the order. On 7 of the 9 camera-heavy streets with a break-in, the first cameras went up after it. Those 7 break-ins cannot be the cameras’ doing, because the cameras were not there yet. The arrow runs the other way: a break-in makes neighbors buy cameras.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the two things that go together: camera-heavy streets and break-ins.** The figures show they go together, not which one leads.
2. **Find the claim that the first caused the second: “Cameras bring burglars to a street.”** That is the arrow the claim draws.
3. **Look for the order: on 7 of the 9 streets, the cameras went up after the break-in.** What came later cannot have caused what came earlier.

This is **Reverse causation**. “Reverse” means the arrow runs the other way: the break-ins led to the cameras.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Ask which came first, and how anyone knows.** A date, a record or a follow-up shows the order. The figures alone do not.
2. **If nothing shows the order, do not act on the arrow.** Treat the claim as not shown.

You may also hear this called “the cause running the other way”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Reverse causation**.

### 11. No comparison group or Confounding: telling them apart

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 11 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-nocontrol-confound`]

Both of these start with people who chose to take part. The test is whether anyone who did not take part is counted.

**Story A**

> A gym says: 'Our personal-training program works. The 90 members who signed up for it lost an average of 6 pounds in a year.' The gym has no weight figures for any member who did not sign up.

**Story B**

> A gym says: 'Our personal-training program works. The 90 members who signed up for it lost an average of 6 pounds in a year, and the 410 who did not lost an average of 1 pound.' Of the 90 who signed up, 70 joined the gym this year, against 60 of the 410 others, and new members lose weight fastest in their first year.

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the same gym and the same personal-training program, and in both the members who signed up lost weight. Compare one thing: whether anyone who did not sign up is counted beside them.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Something else causes both”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the gym counts only the 90 members who signed up. Nobody who went without is counted, so nothing shows what they would have lost anyway. The answer is **“It would have happened anyway”**, which is **No comparison group**.

In Story B the gym also counts the 410 members who did not sign up, and they lost 1 pound. Now two groups are side by side, and something else differs between them: 70 of the 90 are new members, against 60 of the 410, and new members lose weight fastest. The answer is **“Something else causes both”**, which is **Confounding**.

**How to tell them apart**

Is anyone who went without counted beside the people who got the thing? If so, does something else differ between the two groups?


### 12. Confounding or Reverse causation: telling them apart

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 12 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-confound-reverse`]

In both of these, figures show two things going together, and someone says the first caused the second. What the story adds beside the figures tells them apart.

**Story A**

> A city study finds that streets with many trees have higher home prices: homes on the 20 tree-lined streets sell for an average of $480,000, against $350,000 on the 20 streets with few trees. A columnist writes: 'Planting trees raises home prices.' Most of the tree-lined streets are in the city’s older neighborhoods, where the lots are twice as large, and large lots sell for more and have room for trees.

**Story B**

> A city study finds that streets with many trees have higher home prices: homes on the 20 tree-lined streets sell for an average of $480,000, against $350,000 on the 20 streets with few trees. A columnist writes: 'Planting trees raises home prices.' The city’s planting records show that it planted street trees on a street only after its home prices passed $400,000, because the city pays for trees out of property tax.

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the same city, the same trees and the same home prices. Compare one thing: what the story shows beside the figures. Is it something else that differs between the streets, or the order things happened in?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“The second thing causes the first”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A, most of the tree-lined streets are in older neighborhoods, where the lots are twice as large. Large lots sell for more and have room for trees, so lot size could produce the high prices on its own. The answer is **“Something else causes both”**, which is **Confounding**.

In Story B, the city planted trees on a street only after its prices passed $400,000. The high prices came first and led to the trees, and nothing else is needed. The answer is **“The second thing causes the first”**, which is **Reverse causation**.

**How to tell them apart**

Could something else that differs between the groups produce the result by itself? Or could the result have come first and led people to the thing?


### 13. Confounding or A fair test: telling them apart

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 13 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-confound-fair`]

In both of these, the group in the program did better. What differs is who decided who got in.

**Story A**

> A health department says: 'Smokers who sign up for our text-message program are twice as likely to have quit a year later, so the program helps smokers quit.' Of the 300 smokers who signed up, 120 had quit a year later (40 in 100), and of the 700 who did not, 140 had (20 in 100). The intake forms show that 240 of the 300 who signed up had already picked a quit date, against 70 of the 700 who did not.

**Story B**

> A health department had 400 places in a text-message program for 800 smokers who asked to join, and drew 400 names from a hat. A year later it reached all 800 by phone. 120 of the 400 in the program had quit (30 in 100), against 80 of the 400 not in it (20 in 100). 'The program helps smokers quit,' the department says.

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the same health department and the same quit-smoking text program, and in both the program group did better. Compare one thing: who decided which smokers were in the program.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Nothing goes wrong”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the smokers signed up themselves, and 240 of the 300 who signed up had already picked a quit date, against 70 of the 700 who did not. Being ready to quit differs between the groups and could produce the result alone. So Story A does not get **“Nothing goes wrong”**. It gets **“What caused what”**, and then **“Something else causes both”**.

In Story B a draw from a hat decided who got the 400 places, so being ready to quit is no likelier in one group than the other. All 800 were reached a year later and counted the same way, so 30 in 100 against 20 in 100 is a difference the claim can rest on. The answer is **“Nothing goes wrong”**, because chance formed the groups.

If the department had counted only the smokers in the program, with no one beside them, this would be **No comparison group**, not **A fair test**: there would be nothing to measure the 30 in 100 against.

**How to tell them apart**

Who decided which group each person was in: they did, their circumstances did, or a lottery did?


### 14. The question to ask every time

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 14 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-cause`]

Here is the question and its four answers in one place.

**The question:** **“What else could explain the result?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other three.

- **“It would have happened anyway”**
  - Give this answer when the claim says something worked, the result is only for those who got it or only from before and after, and no group without it shows what happens anyway.
  - It leads to **No comparison group**.
- **“Something else causes both”**
  - Give this answer when the claim compares those who did one thing with those who did not, the groups were not split at random, and something else between them could cause the result.
  - It leads to **Confounding**.
- **“The second thing causes the first”**
  - Give this answer when the claim says the first thing caused the second, but the second could come first and lead to the first.
  - It leads to **Reverse causation**.
- **“Picked at its worst or best, it drifts back to normal”**
  - Give this answer when the people or things were picked because they were at their worst or best, and the change afterward is a drift back toward their usual level.
  - It leads to **Regression to the mean**.

**Why it matters**

A result shows a cause only if nothing else explains it as well. The four names differ by what else explains it, and each is ruled out by a different check: a group that went without, groups alike in the other thing, which came first, an equally extreme group left alone.

One test settles all four at once: groups formed by a draw, one given the thing and one not, both counted the same way afterward. If you put all four answers to a claim and none fits, go back to the first question: the claim may be **“Nothing goes wrong”**.

**How to answer it**

1. **Read the whole claim and the sentences after it.** The words that decide it are usually after the claim, not in it.
2. **First ask how the group was picked: “Picked at its worst or best, it drifts back to normal”?** If it was picked for its worst or best result and measured again, this answer wins over the others.
3. **Next ask whether anyone went without: “It would have happened anyway”.** With no one to compare, nothing shows what would have happened anyway.
4. **Two groups that picked their own side? Look for something else that differs: “Something else causes both”.** That other thing could produce the result by itself.
5. **Two things that go together? Ask which came first: “The second thing causes the first”.** The result may have come first and led to the thing.
6. **Find the exact words that show your answer.** If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Some stories show two at once, such as a group picked at its worst with no one to compare it with. Use the order above. The test for each pair is below.

- No comparison group or Regression to the mean: How was the group picked? Was it everyone who got the thing, or only the worst or the best?
- No comparison group or Confounding: Is anyone who went without counted beside the people who got the thing? If so, does something else differ between the two groups?
- Confounding or Reverse causation: Could something else that differs between the groups produce the result by itself? Or could the result have come first and led people to the thing?

### 17. What to carry away

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 17 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap`]

You have now run the last question on your own.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What else could explain the result?
- It would have happened anyway → No comparison group
- Something else causes both → Confounding
- The second thing causes the first → Reverse causation
- Picked at its worst or best, it drifts back to normal → Regression to the mean

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **No comparison group**: a claim that something worked, results only for those who got it or only from before and after, and no group that went without it to show what happens anyway.
- **Regression to the mean**: people or things picked at their worst or best, a drift back toward their usual level afterward, and that drift credited to something done in between.
- **Confounding**: people or places that did one thing beside ones that did not, not split at random, a difference in results, and something else between the groups that could cause it.
- **Reverse causation**: two things that go together, a claim that the first caused the second, and a way the second could come first and lead to the first.

**To carry away**

- When a claim says one thing caused another, ask **“What else could explain the result?”** and find the words that show your answer.
- A result for the people who got a thing, or for one place before and after, is not yet a result for the thing. You need a group that went without, counted the same way.
- Look at how a group was picked. One picked at an extreme will be nearer usual next time, whatever was done, and with no one to compare it with the answer is **“Picked at its worst or best, it drifts back to normal”**.
- Groups that picked their own side differ in more than the thing: the shake’s 38 pounds became 2.
- Two things that go together can have happened in either order. Ask which came first, and how anyone knows.
- Finding another explanation does not make a claim false. It tells you what else could explain the figures, and what would settle it. When a draw formed the groups, the first question gets the answer **“Nothing goes wrong”**.
