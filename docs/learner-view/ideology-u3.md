## Part 1 of 2: The whole nation, and ordinary people against a few at the top

### 1. Before you call a speech “fascist” or “just patriotic”, check what it asks for

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

Before you call a speech “fascist”, “just patriotic” or “populist”, check two things in it: who it speaks for and against, and what it wants done about elections and people who disagree.

Someone online calls a politician “a fascist”. Someone else answers, “That’s just patriotism.” A third says, “They’re all populists.” All three are guessing, because nobody has checked what the speech actually asks for.

Five different things hide behind those words. They all put one people first, and two questions tell them apart. Tone never does: a speech can be loud and proud and leave the vote alone, or sound gentle and take it away.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“Whose side is it on?”** Its answers:

- **“Workers against owners”**: give this answer when the text splits people into those who work for a wage and those who own the businesses (or got rich owning them), and takes the workers’ side.
- **“The nation, or its ordinary people”**: give this answer when the text speaks for one people, marked by its country, culture or blood, and puts it first: the whole nation as one, or its ordinary people against a few at the top. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“The old ways”**: give this answer when the text holds up ways handed down from the past (a faith, home life, old customs, or an old order of crown, church and rank) as what should guide the country.
- **“Everyone’s rights”**: give this answer when the text puts first what every person is owed: the freedom to speak, believe, own and trade, a fair start in life, or fair treatment whatever their group.
- **“No side at all”**: give this answer when the text only says who holds power and how they keep it, or how to handle one practical matter, and takes none of the other four sides.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. Who “the few at the top” are

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-elite`]

Several of the names in this unit set ordinary people against a group at the top. First, a word for that group.

*The quarry town*

> In the town of Brack, three families own the quarry, the only bank and the newspaper. Between them they decide who gets a loan, which stories are printed and who is hired. The other four thousand people in Brack have no say in any of it, unless one of the three families happens to take their side.

Three things set those families apart: there are only three of them, they have far more money and power than anyone else in town, and what they decide reaches everyone else.

When a speaker blames “the few at the top”, this is who they mean. The word does not say the group is wicked or how it got there. In real speeches it is whoever the speaker says is at the top: ministers, officials, bankers, newspaper owners.

**The word for this.** *Elite*: a small group at the top, with far more money, power or say than everyone else.


### 3. Nationalism

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 3 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-nationalism`]

Unit One sorted texts by who they put first. This unit takes one answer, **“The nation, or its ordinary people”**, and splits it into five names. Start with the plainest.

*The anniversary speech*

> From a speech on Founding Day in the market square of Vessany: 'Farmers and clerks, nurses and bakers, those who voted for us and those who did not: ⟦today we are one country, with one flag and one future⟧. ⟦Nothing that divides us is as strong as what holds us together⟧, and our first loyalty is to this nation. In October the voters will choose who governs it, and every party is free to stand against us.'

The speaker talks to everyone in the country at once: farmers, clerks, nurses, bakers, and people who voted against the speaker’s party. Nobody is called an enemy, and what holds people together is said to matter more than what divides them.

The speech also leaves the vote alone. It tells the voters they will choose in October, and says every party is free to stand against the speaker. A text that goes another way sets ordinary people against a few at the top, ranks people by blood, or takes the vote away.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find who it speaks for: everyone, “those who voted for us and those who did not”.** If it speaks for everyone as one, nobody in the country is left out.
2. **Check for an enemy inside the country: there is none.** Blaming a few at the top is a different thing, coming up next.
3. **Check what it says about voting: “every party is free to stand against us”.** Leaving the vote in place keeps it apart from the harsher names.
4. **Check that nobody is ranked: farmers, clerks, nurses and bakers all count the same.** Ranking people by blood is a separate thing, covered last.

This is **Nationalism**. People often use the word only for something harsh, but the harsher versions have names of their own.

You may also hear this called “patriotism”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Nationalism**.

### 5. Fascism

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 5 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-fasc`]

The last speech spoke for everyone and left the vote alone. A speech can speak for everyone in just the same way and treat the vote very differently.

*The rally speech*

> From a speech at a rally in the main square of Tolvar: 'We are one people, with one will, and I am its voice. The old parties have talked for thirty years and the country has gone nowhere. ⟦From tomorrow there is one movement, and the other parties will be closed⟧. ⟦Newspapers that print their complaints will be shut⟧. A nation does not need an argument. It needs a leader.'

At first it sounds like the anniversary speech: “We are one people, with one will.” Then it turns. The speaker says “I am its voice”, so nobody else gets to speak for the people. The other parties will be closed and the papers that print their complaints will be shut, and after that nobody is left who can say no. That is what the second question asks: **“What does it want done with elections and critics?”**

A speech can also get here another way: blame a few at the top, put the country’s own industry first, and then take the vote away. This speech speaks for everyone instead. Either way the name comes from the same thing: elections, other parties and critics pushed aside.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find who it speaks for: “one people, with one will”.** That is the same start as the anniversary speech.
2. **Find who may speak for them: “I am its voice”.** One person speaking for everyone leaves no room for anyone else.
3. **Look for the vote, parties or papers being ended: the other parties “will be closed” and the papers “shut”.** This step is what separates it from **Nationalism**.
4. **Check nobody is ranked by blood: the speech is about one nation and one leader.** Ranking people by blood would make it something else, covered last.

This is **Fascism**. The word comes from a real movement, but every text in this unit is invented. Experts argue about where its edges are, so this unit draws the line you just saw: the vote and the critics taken away.

You may also hear this called “ultranationalism”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Fascism**.

### 7. Nationalism or Fascism: telling them apart

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-nationalism-fasc`]

Both speak for everyone, and both can sound proud and sure of themselves. They are easy to mix up.

**Story A**

> At the opening of the new national hospital in Aldmere, the prime minister said: 'Every patient who comes through these doors, from every town and every trade, is one of us, and this country looks after its own. The health budget will be debated in parliament next month, and the opposition will have its say on every line of it.'

**Story B**

> At the opening of the new national hospital in Aldmere, the Leader said: 'Every patient who comes through these doors, from every town and every trade, is one of us, and this country looks after its own. The health budget is mine alone to decide. Doctors and papers that question the Leader's hospitals will be closed, and the opposition has no more place here.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the opening of a new hospital, and the first half of each speech is word for word the same. Compare one thing: what each speech wants done with the opposition and with people who question the hospitals.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Push them aside, so one leader speaks for everyone”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the prime minister says parliament will debate the health budget and the opposition will have its say on every line. The vote and the right to disagree stay, so the answer is **“Leave them in place”** and this is **Nationalism**.

In Story B the Leader says the budget is his alone to decide, and that doctors and papers that question his hospitals will be closed. The answer is **“Push them aside, so one leader speaks for everyone”**, so this is **Fascism**.

Warmth and pride tell you nothing here. What each speech does with the people who might say no does.

**How to tell them apart**

Look at what the text asks for about elections, other parties and people who disagree. Does it keep them, or want them ended?


### 8. National populism

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 8 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-natpop`]

Every speech so far spoke for the whole nation as one. Now a leaflet that splits the country in two.

*The steelworks leaflet*

> A leaflet from a movement in Brevia: '⟦The people who built this country's steelworks have been told by a handful of ministers and bankers that the works must be sold to a foreign firm⟧. Nobody asked us. ⟦A Brevian steelworks should belong to Brevia, and its jobs should be Brevian jobs⟧. Vote for us in March and we will take back what they sold.'

This leaflet does not speak for everyone. It names two sides: the ordinary people who built the steelworks and now lose the jobs, and “a handful of ministers and bankers” who sold the works without asking. It is on the side of the first and angry at the second. A small group at the top like that is called an *elite*, and these ones are inside the country, not foreigners.

It also says what the country should have: “A Brevian steelworks should belong to Brevia.” That is the nation’s own industry put first. And the way to win it back is an election: “Vote for us in March.”

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the two sides: the people who built the works, and “a handful of ministers and bankers”.** In **Nationalism** nobody inside the country was the other side.
2. **Find what the country should have: “A Brevian steelworks should belong to Brevia, and its jobs should be Brevian jobs.”** That puts the nation’s own industry, culture or borders first.
3. **Check what it wants done about voting: “Vote for us in March”.** The vote is how the few at the top get removed, so it stays.

This is **National populism**. The “national” half means the nation’s industry, culture or borders come first. The “populist” half means ordinary people against a few at the top. Both halves have to be in the text.

You may also hear this called “right-wing populism”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **National populism**.

### 10. Populism on its own

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 10 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-pop`]

The steelworks leaflet was angry at a few at the top, then said what the country should have. A text can stop after the anger.

*The podcast*

> From a podcast: '⟦The people at the top, the bankers, the ministers, the ones who always land on their feet, have been playing the rest of us for years⟧. We pay and they collect. ⟦This is the moment for ordinary people to throw them out⟧. On polling day, every ordinary person in Tolvar has the power to do it.'

It has the same two sides as the steelworks leaflet: ordinary people, and “the bankers, the ministers, the ones who always land on their feet”. It is angry at the second group and on the side of the first. Then it stops: nothing about the country’s borders, culture or industry, and nothing about any people being above another. Its only plan is the vote: “On polling day, every ordinary person in Tolvar has the power to do it.”

This bare version is very common. A slogan like “they’ve had their turn, now throw them out” says who it is angry at and no more. It has a name of its own so that it does not get forced into a name that needs more.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the two sides: ordinary people, and “the bankers, the ministers”.** These are the same two sides as in **National populism**.
2. **Look for what the country should have: there is nothing about borders, culture or industry.** If the text asked for any of that, it would be **National populism**.
3. **Check the plan: “On polling day, every ordinary person in Tolvar has the power to do it.”** The vote stays in place.

This is **Populism on its own**. It means ordinary people set against a few at the top, with nothing else added. If the text adds anything more, it has a different name.

You may also hear this called “thin populism”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Populism on its own**.

### 12. Nationalism or National populism: telling them apart

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 12 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-nationalism-natpop`]

Both put the nation first and leave the vote in place, and both can be angry about how the country is run.

**Story A**

> The transport minister told parliament: 'The northern line will close, and I know what it means for the north. We are one country, and the south will help carry what the north loses. The closure can be challenged in the courts and at the ballot box, and I expect the opposition to do both.'

**Story B**

> A campaign leaflet: 'The ministers in the capital are closing the northern line, and none of them has ever ridden it. Our railways should be run for our own people, with our own engineers and our own steel. Vote us in on the third and the line will reopen.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the closing of the northern railway line. Compare one thing: whether anyone inside the country is named as the other side.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Ordinary people against an elite, and the nation first”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the transport minister says “we are one country”, and that the south will help carry what the north loses. Nobody inside the country is the other side. The answer is **“The whole nation, as one people”**, so this is **Nationalism**.

In Story B the leaflet blames “the ministers in the capital, none of whom has ever ridden it” for closing the line, and wants the railways run for the country’s own people. The answer is **“Ordinary people against an elite, and the nation first”**, so this is **National populism**.

The closing is the same, and so is the anger. What differs is whether the speech speaks for everyone, or for ordinary people against a few at the top.

**How to tell them apart**

Is anyone inside the country named as the other side, a few at the top set against everyone else? Or does the text speak for everyone alike?


### 13. National populism or Populism on its own: telling them apart

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 13 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-natpop-pop`]

Both set ordinary people against a few at the top. The difference is small and easy to miss.

**Story A**

> From a speech by Joran Pell of the Homeland List: 'The ministers and the bankers they dine with have spent our taxes rescuing a bank, and it has been sold to a foreign group. Our savings should stay in our own banks, run for our own people. Vote Homeland List on Sunday and we will take them back.'

**Story B**

> From a speech by Joran Pell of the People's List: 'The ministers and the bankers they dine with have spent our taxes rescuing a bank, and not one of them has paid a price. They rescue each other and bill the rest of us. Vote People's List on Sunday and we will throw them out.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about a bank rescue paid for out of taxes, and the first lines are the same. Compare one thing: what each speech says the country should have, after the anger at those at the top.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Ordinary people against an elite, and nothing more”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the speaker is angry at the ministers and bankers, then says “our savings should stay in our own banks, run for our own people”. That puts the country’s own industry first. The answer is **“Ordinary people against an elite, and the nation first”**, so this is **National populism**.

In Story B the speaker is angry at the same people and asks for a vote to throw them out. Nothing is said about what the country should have. The answer is **“Ordinary people against an elite, and nothing more”**, so this is **Populism on its own**.

Cut the sentence about savings from Story A and what is left is almost Story B. The difference can be that thin.

**How to tell them apart**

After the text sets ordinary people against those at the top, does it say anything about the country’s borders, culture or industry? If not, there is nothing more.


### 14. A text that blames a few at the top and still ends the vote

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 14 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `exception`, id `exc-elitefasc`]

Ordinary people against a few at the top, with the vote left in place, is **National populism**. A text can start the same way and end somewhere else.

*The broadcast against the bankers*

> From a broadcast by the leader of the Black Lantern movement in Calder: 'The bankers and ministers in the capital have sold this country's farms and mills to foreigners and left our people poor. Calder is one people with one will, and the movement is its voice. When we take power, there will be no more elections to be bought by the rich, the other parties will be closed, and any paper that defends the bankers will be shut.'

So far this broadcast sounds like **National populism**: it blames a few at the top (“the bankers and ministers in the capital have sold this country’s farms and mills”) and wants the country’s own industry kept. Yet it is **Fascism**.

**You are asked:** This looks like **National populism**. Before you read why it is **Fascism**, tap the words in the story that settle it.

The pieces you can tap:
1. “The bankers and ministers in the capital have sold this country's farms and mills to foreigners and left our people poor”
2. “Calder is one people with one will, and the movement is its voice”
3. “When we take power, there will be no more elections to be bought by the rich, the other parties will be closed, and any paper that defends the bankers will be shut”

**Shown as soon as you tap.** The words are “When we take power, there will be no more elections to be bought by the rich, the other parties will be closed, and any paper that defends the bankers will be shut”.
- If you tapped “The bankers and ministers in the capital have sold this country's farms and mills to foreigners and left our people poor”: That is half of **National populism**: a few at the top blamed, and the country's own industry put first. It says nothing about the vote.
- If you tapped “Calder is one people with one will, and the movement is its voice”: That speaks for the whole nation as one. It still says nothing about the vote.

**Why this is Fascism and not National populism**

The first half is **National populism**. If it stopped there and asked the voters to throw those few out, that is the name it would get.

It does not stop. The movement says it is “the voice” of one people, that there will be no more elections, that the other parties will be closed and that any paper that defends the bankers will be shut. That is not a vote to remove those at the top. It takes the vote away from everyone, so that the movement alone speaks.

The second question decides it. In **National populism** the vote stays, and it is the way to remove those at the top. Here the answer is **“Push them aside, so one leader speaks for everyone”**, so this is **Fascism**.

**How to tell them apart**

Does the text ask the voters to remove those at the top? Or does it go further and take away elections, other parties or the right to object?

The broadcast also speaks of one people with one will, which fits **“The whole nation, as one people”**, and blames a few at the top, which fits **“Ordinary people against an elite, and the nation first”**. When a story shows both, the answer to the first question is **“Ordinary people against an elite, and the nation first”**. Either way, the second question gives the name.

Every text so far put a people first without saying it was worth more than any other. This one does say so.

*The pamphlet of the old blood*

> From a pamphlet of the Iron Banner in Dorn: '⟦There are the Dornlings of the old blood, and there are the others who came later, and they are not equal⟧. Blood decides what a people can do. The old blood built every road and every church in Dorn, and ⟦it is higher than the later peoples⟧. Dorn is for the old blood first, and the later peoples will serve it or be kept apart from it. There will be one party in Dorn, the party of the old blood.'

The pamphlet sorts people by descent into “the Dornlings of the old blood” and “the others who came later”, and says they “are not equal”. The old blood is “higher”, comes first and rules. The rest “will serve it or be kept apart from it”. Blood here means descent: who your parents and grandparents were.

This is the new thing. In every speech so far a people came first, but nobody was said to be worth less by birth. Here people are ranked at birth, and that alone decides the name. The pamphlet also wants one party only, but a text that ranked people this way and kept the elections would get the same name.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find how people are sorted: by blood, “the old blood” and “the others who came later”.** Birth, not anything a person does, decides which group you are in.
2. **Find the ranking: the old blood is “higher than the later peoples”.** One people is said to be worth more than another.
3. **Find who ends up on top and who ends up below: the old blood rules, and the rest “serve it or be kept apart”.** In **National populism** the other side is a few at the top, but here it is whole peoples.

This is **Nazism**. The word comes from a real movement, but every text in this unit is invented. Experts argue about where its edges are, so this unit draws the line at the ranking, because that is what a short text can show.

You may also hear this called “National Socialism” or “neo-Nazism” or “racial supremacism”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Nazism**.

### 17. Fascism or Nazism: telling them apart

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 17 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-fasc-nazi`]

These two can look almost the same on the page. Both can push the vote and critics aside, and both can be full of marches and talk of one nation.

**Story A**

> At a youth parade in Marren, the Leader said: 'Every child of Marren marches today as one people with one will. The old parties are dissolved, and the papers that spoke for them are shut. Marren has one voice, and it is mine.'

**Story B**

> At a youth parade in Marren, the Leader said: 'Every child of the first blood marches today, and the later peoples may watch from the side, as is fitting for a lower people. The old parties are dissolved, and the papers that spoke for them are shut. Marren has one voice, and it is mine.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about a youth parade in Marren, and in both the old parties are dissolved. Compare one thing: whether the speech ranks people by blood.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“One people by blood, ranked above the others”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A “every child of Marren marches today as one people with one will”. The old parties are dissolved and the Leader has one voice. Nobody is ranked, so the answer to the first question is **“The whole nation, as one people”**. With the vote pushed aside, this is **Fascism**.

In Story B the children of the first blood march, and the later peoples “may watch from the side, as is fitting for a lower people”. The old parties are dissolved here too. People are ranked by blood, so the answer is **“One people by blood, ranked above the others”** and this is **Nazism**.

The second question gets the same answer in both, so it cannot tell them apart. The first question can: are people ranked by blood?

**How to tell them apart**

Does the text rank peoples by blood or birth, with its own above the others? Or does it speak of one nation, or of its ordinary people, with nobody ranked by blood?


### 18. The first question: whom does the text speak for, and against whom?

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 18 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-who`]

You have seen this question under each new name, with one answer. Here it is with all four answers in one place.

**The question:** **“Who is it for, and who is it against?”**

**Its answers**

- **“The whole nation, as one people”**
  - Give this answer when the text speaks for the whole nation as one people with one future, and treats what divides it, like rich and poor, as smaller than what holds it together.
  - Keeps **Nationalism** and **Fascism**. Rules out **National populism**, **Populism on its own** and **Nazism**.
- **“Ordinary people against an elite, and the nation first”**
  - Give this answer when the text sets the country’s ordinary people against an elite at the top, like politicians or bankers, and wants the nation’s borders, culture or industry put first.
  - Keeps **National populism** and **Fascism**. Rules out **Nationalism**, **Populism on its own** and **Nazism**.
- **“Ordinary people against an elite, and nothing more”**
  - Give this answer when the text sets ordinary people against an elite at the top, and puts nothing of the nation first: no borders, culture or industry, and no ranking of peoples.
  - Keeps **Populism on its own**. Rules out **Nationalism**, **Fascism**, **National populism** and **Nazism**.
- **“One people by blood, ranked above the others”**
  - Give this answer when the text sorts people by blood or birth into higher and lower peoples, and puts its own people above the rest.
  - Keeps **Nazism**. Rules out **Nationalism**, **Fascism**, **National populism** and **Populism on its own**.

**Why it matters**

All five love one people, and differ in who that people is set against. The whole nation has no enemy inside it; ordinary people have an elite above them; a people by blood has peoples it ranks lower.

This question goes first because it separates the most. **Nationalism**, **National populism**, **Populism on its own** and **Nazism** each get a different answer, and **Nazism** is settled by this question alone, whatever the second question says. It cannot split **Fascism** from **Nationalism** or **National populism**, because **Fascism** can speak for the whole nation or for ordinary people against a few at the top. For those two pairs you need the second question.

**How to answer it**

1. **Read the whole text, and look first for people ranked by birth: “the old blood” against “the later peoples”.** If it ranks peoples by blood with its own on top, the answer is **“One people by blood, ranked above the others”** and you can stop.
2. **Ask whether anyone inside the country is named as the other side: ministers, officials, bankers.** If nobody is, and the text speaks for everyone, the answer is **“The whole nation, as one people”**.
3. **If a few at the top are named, check whether the text also says what the country should have: its borders, culture or industry.** If it does, the answer is **“Ordinary people against an elite, and the nation first”**.
4. **If it only blames them and says nothing more, stop there.** That is **“Ordinary people against an elite, and nothing more”**.
5. **Take names like “the establishment” or “the people in the capital” as the few at the top.** They mean a few at the top whoever says them.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Some texts seem to fit two answers. Each pair below was set side by side earlier in this unit, with the one question that separates it.

- Nationalism or National populism: Is anyone inside the country named as the other side, a few at the top set against everyone else? Or does the text speak for everyone alike?
- Nationalism or Populism on its own: Does the text speak for everyone, or set ordinary people against a few at the top? If the second, does it add anything about what the country should have?
- Nationalism or Nazism: Does the text rank peoples by blood or birth, with its own above the others? Or does it speak for everyone in the country as equals?
- National populism or Populism on its own: After the text sets ordinary people against those at the top, does it say anything about the country’s borders, culture or industry? If not, there is nothing more.
- National populism or Nazism: Who is the text against: a few at the top, or peoples it ranks lower by blood or birth?
- Populism on its own or Nazism: Is the only line the one between ordinary people and a few at the top? Or are peoples ranked by blood or birth?
- Fascism or Nazism: Does the text rank peoples by blood or birth, with its own above the others? Or does it speak of one nation, or of its ordinary people, with nobody ranked by blood?

### 20. The second question: elections and people who disagree

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 20 of 23*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-elections`]

The first question left some pairs unsorted: **Nationalism** and **Fascism** can both speak for the whole nation, and **National populism** and **Fascism** can both set ordinary people against a few at the top. This question sorts them.

**The question:** **“What does it want done with elections and critics?”**

**Its answers**

- **“Push them aside, so one leader speaks for everyone”**
  - Give this answer when the text wants elections, lawmakers, other parties or critics shut down, silenced or crushed, so that one leader or one movement speaks for the whole people.
  - Keeps **Fascism** and **Nazism**. Rules out **Nationalism**, **National populism** and **Populism on its own**.
- **“Leave them in place”**
  - Give this answer when the text keeps elections, other parties and the right to disagree, asks voters for power, or says nothing against them.
  - Keeps **Nationalism**, **National populism**, **Populism on its own** and **Nazism**. Rules out **Fascism**.

**Why it matters**

Several names put the nation first. Shutting down elections and silencing critics is what makes one of them rule alone, so whether elections and critics stay is what to check.

**Fascism** is the only name that wants them gone and ranks nobody by blood. **Nazism** appears under both answers on purpose: a text that ranks peoples can end elections or keep them, and the name does not change, because the first question has already decided it.

**How to answer it**

1. **Look for what the text wants done, not how it sounds.** Angry or insulting words about opponents do not count, only what the text asks for.
2. **Look for words that leave things in place: “Vote for us”, “let the voters decide”, “any party may stand against us”.** These keep the vote and the critics, so the answer is **“Leave them in place”**.
3. **Look for words that end them: other parties closed, the vote canceled, papers shut, critics “dealt with”.** Any one of these gives **“Push them aside, so one leader speaks for everyone”**.
4. **If the text says nothing about elections or critics, answer “Leave them in place”.** Nothing is asked for, so nothing is taken away.
5. **Do not take banned parties or a controlled press as proof of the name.** Almost every dictatorship does these whatever it believes, so they only answer this second question.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Some texts seem to fit two answers. Each pair below was set side by side earlier in this unit, with the one question that separates it.

- Nationalism or Fascism: Look at what the text asks for about elections, other parties and people who disagree. Does it keep them, or want them ended?
- National populism or Fascism: Does the text ask the voters to remove those at the top? Or does it go further and take away elections, other parties or the right to object?

**This unit’s questions and answers**

Who is it for, and who is it against?
- The whole nation, as one people → Nationalism · Fascism

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Nationalism**: the whole nation as one people, put first, with no enemy inside it, no peoples ranked by blood, and elections, other parties and critics left alone.
- **Fascism**: the nation or its ordinary people put first, and elections, other parties or critics shut down or crushed so that one leader or movement speaks for everyone.
- **National populism**: the country’s ordinary people set against an elite at the top, the nation’s borders, culture or industry put first, and elections and critics left alone.
- **Populism on its own**: ordinary people set against an elite at the top, and nothing more: no borders, culture or industry to put first, no ranking of peoples, and elections left alone.
- **Nazism**: people sorted by blood or birth into higher and lower peoples, and the text’s own people put above the rest, to rule them or to be kept apart from them.

**To carry away**

- Ask whom the text speaks for and against whom, and find the words that show it. If you can’t find them, you don’t have an answer yet.
- Then ask what it wants done about the vote and about people who object, and find those words too. A text that says nothing about them has not asked for them to go.
- The story never decides it, and neither does the tone. A text can be loud and proud and leave the vote alone, or sound gentle and take it away.
- Ranking people by blood gives **Nazism**, whatever the text says about the vote. Pushing the vote aside, with the nation spoken for as one or ordinary people set against a few at the top, gives **Fascism**.
- Anger at a few at the top is not a name until you ask what else the text says. With nothing more it is **Populism on its own**. With the country’s borders, culture or industry put first it is **National populism**.
