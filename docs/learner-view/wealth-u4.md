## Part 1 of 2: Bills paid by selling, a bill on a date, and money that is safe

### 1. Before you protect your money from a fall, check what the fall would hit

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

Before you sell, buy “protection” or panic because prices fell, check what the fall would actually hit. It is one of three things, or nothing at all, and each needs a different fix, including doing nothing.

Four people own much the same investments. One spring, prices drop by a quarter.

Mo lives on his investments. Every month he sells a little to pay his bills, and now each sale takes a bigger slice of what he owns. What he sold is not there when prices come back.

Jade has $24,000 to pay on June 1, and the money for it is in shares. In March it is worth $18,000. The bill is still $24,000.

Lukas chose a plan years ago and never looked at it again. After years of rises, most of his money is in shares, far more than he chose. The fall takes a bigger bite than he agreed to.

Anneke also lives on her investments. But she keeps three years of spending in a savings account, and she paid every bill from it that spring. She sold nothing.

The fall was the same for all four. Three were hit, each in a different way, and Anneke was not hit at all. So do not ask “did prices fall?” Ask what the fall would hit.

Every number in this unit is an example to show how an idea works. None of them is a forecast, and nobody can say what prices will do next.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“What could lose this money?”** Its answers:

- **“Money going out every year”**: give this answer when money goes out of the pot every year: fees charged by funds or an adviser, tax on the investments, or a sum taken out to spend.
- **“One thing that could wipe it out”**: give this answer when most of the pot depends on one thing: one company or property that is most of it, a claim that could reach everything they own, or a lender who could force a sale.
- **“Prices falling at the wrong time”**: give this answer when a fall in prices would hurt because of what the money has to pay for or how it is split: living costs, a bill due on a set date, or a mix drifted from its plan. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“Handing it over”**: give this answer when the money passes to others when the owner dies, can no longer manage it or gives it away, and the question is who gets it, the tax on it, the papers, or the people.
- **“Nothing could lose it”**: give this answer when money is being kept, and none of the other four show up: no needless fee or tax, nothing most of it depends on, no bill or drifted mix, and no handover coming.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. Why the order of good and bad years matters

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-sequence`]

One idea sits under every name in this unit, so here it is first, with numbers.

*Ingrid and Paul*

> Ingrid and Paul retired on the same day, each with $500,000 invested in the same fund of shares. Each takes $25,000 out on the first day of every year to live on. Over their first four years the fund's price changes by the same four amounts: down 20%, down 10%, up 10% and up 20%. For Ingrid they come in that order, falls first. For Paul they come in the opposite order, rises first.

Ingrid ends with $358,740. Paul ends with $393,640, which is $34,900 more. They had the same money, the same fund and the same withdrawals. Only the order of the good and bad years was different.

Why? Ingrid took out her $25,000 after prices had fallen, so each withdrawal took a bigger slice of her fund, and that slice was gone when prices rose. If neither had taken anything out, both would have ended with $475,200.

**The word for this.** *Sequence risk*: the harm done when a fall comes early, while money is being taken out, rather than later: more has to be sold at low prices, and what is sold is not there when prices come back.

This is *sequence risk*. It is not about whether prices fall, because both stories had falls. It is about whether a fall comes while you are taking money out. Each name in this unit is a way of being caught by it, or of not being caught.


### 3. Years of spending in cash

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 3 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-cashbuffer`]

The last card showed what a fall does when money is being taken out. Here is someone it is happening to.

*Alan’s monthly sales*

> Alan is 66 and retired two years ago. His $600,000 is all in two funds of shares. He needs $2,000 a month to live on, and ⟦he gets it by selling about $2,000 of the funds on the first of every month. He keeps no cash set aside for it⟧. This year prices fell by 30%.

Every month Alan sells funds to pay his bills, and prices are down 30%. Say one unit costs $10 before the fall and $7 now. A year of bills, $24,000, used to mean selling 2,400 units. Now it means about 3,430, which is 1,030 more for the same year of living, and those units are not in the funds when prices come back. That is *sequence risk*.

A fall costs nothing to someone who sells nothing, so the fix is cash for the bills. Alan could keep three years of spending, $72,000, in a separate savings account. In a down year he pays from it and sells no funds, and in an up year he sells some funds to fill it up again. Cash has a cost: if shares grew 5% a year and the account paid 1%, $72,000 in cash gives up $2,880 a year. Those rates are examples. What the cash buys is time for prices to recover, and a fall that outlasts the cash still hurts.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find where the bills are paid from: Alan sells about $2,000 of funds on the first of every month.** Bills paid by selling are what a fall catches.
2. **Check whether anything is set aside to spend instead: Alan keeps no cash.** Cash is what lets you leave the funds alone in a down year.
3. **Check that the money can fall: all of Alan’s is in funds of shares.** Money in cash, or in *a bond* that repays on time, is out of a fall’s reach.

This is **Years of spending in cash**. The fix is cash for the bills, kept apart from the investments.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Ask: "If prices fell by a third this year, where would next year’s bills come from?"** If the answer is "I would sell some of what has fallen", you are in Alan’s position.
2. **Add up one year of spending from your bank statements.** That is the number the cash has to cover.
3. **Decide how many years of it to hold in cash, and count the cost in dollars.** Cash earns less than shares in a good year, so more years cost more.
4. **Keep it in a savings account at an FDIC-insured bank, apart from the investments.** Then a fall cannot reach it.
5. **Write the rule: spend from it in a down year, and fill it up again in an up year.** A rule written now beats a decision made in a panic.

You may also hear this called “a cash buffer” or “a spending reserve”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Years of spending in cash**.

### 5. Already covered

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 5 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-covered`]

Alan was caught by the fall because every bill was paid by selling. Here is a couple who lived through the same fall and were not caught.

*Ruth and Gil’s savings account*

> Ruth and Gil, both 68, have $640,000. $75,000 of it is in a savings account, and the rest is in funds of shares. They need $2,000 a month to live on, which is $24,000 a year, so the savings account holds a little over three years of spending. Last year prices fell by 30%. ⟦They have paid every bill from the savings account since the fall, and have not sold any of the funds⟧. Their plan is to fill the account up again by selling some of the funds in a year when prices are up.

Put Ruth and Gil beside Alan. The fall was the same, 30%, and both are living on their money. Alan sold funds at the low price every month. Ruth and Gil spent cash and sold nothing, so the fall changed what their funds are worth and changed nothing about what they sold. If prices stayed down for more than three years the cash would run out and they would be hurt too, but until then the cash gives prices time.

This has a name because the right thing to do about a fall that would catch nothing is nothing. A fix bought for a problem you do not have costs money every year and solves nothing. The same goes for a bill whose money is already in cash or in *a bond* that repays by the day, and for a mix that is still inside the limits of its plan.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find where the bills are paid from: Ruth and Gil’s savings account.** Money in cash does not move with prices.
2. **Check what they have sold since the fall: none of the funds.** A fall costs nothing real if nothing has to be sold in it.
3. **Check how long the cash lasts: a little over three years of bills.** That is the time prices have to come back.

This is **Already covered**. It does not tell you to do something. It tells you there is nothing to do, and that is as real an answer as any other.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Say so, and leave it alone.** A fix for a problem you do not have costs money and solves nothing.
2. **Write one line: what the money is for, where it is held, and when it is needed.** It shows at a glance that the money is safe.
3. **Check the one fact that keeps it safe: the cash will not run out before it is needed, the bond repays before the bill is due, or *the mix* is inside its limits.** That fact is the whole reason nothing needs doing.
4. **Put a date on your calendar to look again.** Cash gets spent and mixes drift.
5. **If someone offers a product to protect you from a fall you can already ride out, ask which part of your situation it answers.** If they cannot name one, decline.

### 7. Years of spending in cash or Already covered: telling them apart

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-cashbuffer-covered`]

These two are easy to mix up, because in both the people live on their money and prices have fallen.

**Story A**

> Colm and Fay, both 65, have $500,000 in funds of shares. Their living costs are $1,700 a month, and they pay them by selling about $1,700 of the funds on the first of every month. They have no savings account. This year prices fell by 20%.

**Story B**

> Colm and Fay, both 65, have $500,000, of which $62,000 is in a savings account and the rest in funds of shares. Their living costs are $1,700 a month, and they pay them from the savings account. This year prices fell by 20%, and they have not sold any of the funds.

**What to compare.** Both stories are about Colm and Fay, who have $500,000 and the same bills, in the same year of falling prices. Compare one thing: where each month’s bills are paid from.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“They live by selling investments that can fall”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story A.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A every month’s bills are paid by selling about $1,700 of the funds, and nothing is set aside. That is **Years of spending in cash**.

In Story B the bills are paid from a savings account of $62,000, a little over three years of $20,400, and none of the funds has been sold. The fall changed what the funds are worth, and changed nothing about what Colm and Fay sold. That is **Already covered**.

The couple, the money and the fall are the same. You can never name a story from the fall alone.

**How to tell them apart**

When the next bills are due, where does the money for them come from: selling shares or funds, or cash or bonds put aside for them?


### 8. Bills paid by selling, but the same sum for years

*Dolores’s fixed sum*

Dolores sells units of her funds every month to pay her bills, with no cash set aside, and prices have fallen. That looks like **Years of spending in cash**. **Shown as soon as you tap.** The words are “She set herself $36,000 a year to live on, which was 4% of the money then, and she has taken exactly that every year since, by selling units of the funds each month”.
- If you tapped “Dolores retired six years ago with $900,000 in funds of shares”: That is where the money sits and how much there was. It does not yet show what the story is about.
- If you tapped “She keeps no cash set aside”: True, and it is why this looks like **Years of spending in cash**. It is not the part that decides it.
- If you tapped “Prices have fallen, and the funds are now worth $540,000”: That is the fall, only half of what settles it. The words asked for are about the sum.

**Why this is Spend a percentage of the pot and not Years of spending in cash**

$36,000 was 4% of her $900,000. It has not changed, but her funds are now worth $540,000, so $36,000 is about 6.7% of them. Every year the same sum comes out of a smaller pot. Alan’s $24,000 was a fair share of what he had, and his only trouble was raising each month’s bills on bad days.

More cash would not fix Dolores’s story: three years of $36,000 in cash would only move the problem three years later. What has to change is the sum. Set it each year as a percentage of what is left, not as a figure fixed years ago.

**How to tell them apart**

Is the sum taken out a fair share of what is left, or has it stayed the same while what is left shrank? Would it still be a heavy sum if prices stopped falling today?

When a story shows both **“Prices falling at the wrong time”** and the same sum taken out every year from a pot that has shrunk, or a sale to fix the mix that would bring a tax bill new money could avoid, the answer is **“Money going out every year”**.

The story shows two things: bills raised from falling funds, and a fixed sum from funds that have shrunk. When it shows both, the answer is the second.


### 9. A bond for each bill

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 9 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-ladder`]

The first two names are about money spent every month. This one is about a single payment, and where its money is kept.

*The wedding venue*

> Tobi and Ada's daughter is getting married next summer. The venue has been booked, and ⟦the whole fee, $24,000, is due on June 1, eleven months from now⟧. The $24,000 that Tobi and Ada have set aside for it ⟦is in a fund of shares⟧. Prices have just fallen by 25%.

On June 1, Tobi and Ada must have $24,000. After the fall their fund holds $18,000, which is $6,000 short. By June 1 prices may have come back or fallen further, and nobody can say. What is certain is that the bill will still be $24,000. An amount that can move is being asked to cover an amount that cannot.

The fix is to put the bill’s money where its worth on the day is known: *a bond* from a borrower very unlikely to fail to pay, such as the US government, that repays $24,000 on or just before June 1. Held to that date, it pays exactly that, whatever its price did on the way. It might cost about $23,200 today, and the $800 difference is the interest it earns. That is a price paid for certainty: the same $23,200 in shares might have grown by more, or by less. With several bills on several dates, such as tuition every September for six years, you buy one bond for each bill, repaying on that bill’s date.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the bill: $24,000, due on June 1.** Its size and its date do not move, whatever the market does.
2. **Find where its money is held: in *a fund* of shares.** Shares can be worth less on the day than they are today.
3. **Check it is one payment, not living costs every month.** Bills every month call for cash, and one bill on one day calls for *a bond*.

This is **A bond for each bill**. Each bond repays on the day its bill is due.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **List every bill of a known size on a known date over the next several years.** Each one needs its own answer.
2. **Find where each bill’s money is held today.** Only money in shares or funds is at risk.
3. **For each one held in shares or funds, buy a Treasury bond that repays the amount on or just before the date, and hold it to that date.** The US government is very unlikely to fail to pay, and the amount is fixed.
4. **Check the price and the repayment date before you buy.** The repayment must land before the bill is due.
5. **Leave money that is not tied to a date where it is.** Bonds pay only fixed interest, which can be less than shares earn in a good year.

You may also hear this called “a bond ladder”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **A bond for each bill**.

## Part 2 of 2: A mix that has drifted, telling them apart, then the drill

### 12. Rebalance by a written rule

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 12 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-rebalance`]

The first three names are about money you need. This one is about money you do not need for years, but that is no longer split the way you decided.

*Marek’s retirement savings*

> Marek is 49. When he began saving for retirement he chose a plan: 60% of his money in shares and 40% in bonds. After eight years of rises his savings are $800,000. ⟦Shares are now $624,000 of that, 78%⟧. He will not need to take any money out for fifteen years, and he has not changed the mix since he chose it.

Nothing has gone wrong yet, and nobody decided to take more risk: *the mix* moved while Marek was not looking. Now suppose shares fall 30%. On his plan, shares would be $480,000, and the fall would take $144,000, which is 18% of everything he has. With $624,000 in shares, the same fall takes $187,200, which is 23.4%. That is $43,200 more than he chose to risk, from exactly the same fall.

The fix is a written rule, made while things are calm. For example: "Each January, if shares are more than 5 points away from 60%, sell or buy until they are back at 60%." For Marek that means selling $144,000 of shares and buying $144,000 of bonds. It has to be written down because the moment to act feels wrong: selling what has been rising feels like giving up something that works, and buying after a fall feels like adding to a loss. The 5 points and the January date are examples, and your own plan sets its own.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the plan he chose: 60% in shares and 40% in bonds.** Without a plan there is nothing to drift from.
2. **Find today’s split: 78% in shares.** A statement shows the real number, and memory does not.
3. **Work out the gap: 18 points above his plan.** A few points is normal, and this many changes how hard a fall bites.
4. **Check that nothing is being sold to pay for anything: no bill and no living costs for fifteen years.** If something were, that would come first.

This is **Rebalance by a written rule**: put *the mix* back to what you chose, by a rule you wrote down in advance. The same name applies when it drifts the other way, after shares fall and bonds do not.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Find the plan you chose, or write one now: for example 60% in shares and 40% in bonds.** Without a plan there is nothing to drift from.
2. **Set how far you will let it move, such as 5 points either side.** The limit tells you when to act.
3. **Find today’s split on your 401(k) or account statement.** You need the real number to compare.
4. **Write the rule: a date to look, and what you do when a part is outside its limit.** You will not feel like acting on the day, so decide now.
5. **Before you sell shares, check which account they are in: in *a sheltered account* a sale raises no tax, but in a taxable account work out the tax on the gain first, and ask whether new money could put the split back instead.** A sale you do not need can bring a tax bill you do not need.
6. **Follow the rule on its date, whatever prices have done since.** A rule only works if you keep it.

You may also hear this called “rebalancing”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Rebalance by a written rule**.

### 17. The question you have been answering all along

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 17 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-why`]

Here is the question and its four answers in one place.

**The question:** **“Why would a fall in prices hurt right now?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other three.

- **“They live by selling investments that can fall”**
  - Give this answer when the person lives on money from selling shares or funds whose prices can fall, and has no cash set aside to spend from while prices are down.
  - It leads to **Years of spending in cash**.
- **“A known bill on a known date, paid from investments that can fall”**
  - Give this answer when a bill of a known size is due on a known date, and the money for it is still in shares or funds whose prices can fall.
  - It leads to **A bond for each bill**.
- **“The mix has drifted from its plan”**
  - Give this answer when the person chose a mix, and it has drifted well away from it, so that a fall would take more, or less, than they chose.
  - It leads to **Rebalance by a written rule**.
- **“It would not: the money needed soon is already safe”**
  - Give this answer when the money for living costs or a coming bill is already in cash or in bonds that repay in time, or the mix is within its plan, and none of the other three show up.
  - It leads to **Already covered**.

**Why it matters**

Each answer has its own fix, and none of them is a guess about where prices go next. When the money needed soon is already safe, a fall does no lasting harm, because nothing has to be sold while prices are down.

Two people with the same investments and the same fall can get different answers, and one of them may be told there is nothing to fix. The fall does not decide it, and neither does the fund. What decides it is what the money has to pay for, and where that money is held.

**How to answer it**

1. **Do not answer from the fall alone: prices fell 30% in Ruth and Gil’s story, and nothing needed doing.** Falls come to everyone, so what a fall would catch is what counts.
2. **First ask: is anything paid for by selling shares or funds, month after month? That is Years of spending in cash.** Money you need soon comes before everything else.
3. **Next ask: is a bill of a known size due on a known date, with its money in shares or funds? That is A bond for each bill.** Living costs that keep coming are a different thing from one bill on one day.
4. **Next ask: is the only thing in the story a mix that has moved? That is Rebalance by a written rule.** It is what is left when nothing is needed soon.
5. **None of these? Then the bills’ money is already in cash or in bonds that repay in time, or *the mix* is inside its limits. That is Already covered.** A fall would force no sale, so nothing needs doing.
6. **Find the exact words that show your answer.** If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Sometimes a story shows two at once, such as a drifted mix beside bills paid by selling. Use the order above. The test for each pair is below.

- Years of spending in cash or Already covered: When the next bills are due, where does the money for them come from: selling shares or funds, or cash or bonds put aside for them?
- A bond for each bill or Already covered: Where is the bill’s money held, and could its price drop before the day the bill is due?
- Rebalance by a written rule or Already covered: Does the story give the plan and how far *the mix* may move from it, and is today’s split outside that distance?
- Years of spending in cash or A bond for each bill: Is the money needed for living costs that keep coming, or for one bill of a known size on a known day?
- Years of spending in cash or Rebalance by a written rule: Are shares or funds being sold to pay for something? Or is the only thing in the story a mix that has moved?
- A bond for each bill or Rebalance by a written rule: Is a bill of a known size due on a known date, with its money in shares or funds? Or is the only thing in the story a mix that has moved?

**This unit’s questions and answers**

Why would a fall in prices hurt right now?
- They live by selling investments that can fall → Years of spending in cash
- A known bill on a known date, paid from investments that can fall → A bond for each bill
- The mix has drifted from its plan → Rebalance by a written rule

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Years of spending in cash**: living costs paid by selling shares or funds whose prices can fall, and no cash set aside to spend from while prices are down.
- **Already covered**: living costs, a bill or a mix that a fall could hit, already safe: the money needed in cash or bonds that repay in time, or the mix within its plan’s limits.
- **A bond for each bill**: a bill of a known size due on a known date, and the money for it still in shares or funds whose prices can fall.
- **Rebalance by a written rule**: a mix the person chose, and the mix now well away from it, so that a fall would take more, or less, than they chose.

**To carry away**

- A fall is not the thing to look for, because falls come to everyone. Look for what a fall would catch: bills paid by selling, a bill on a date with its money in shares, or a mix that has drifted far from its plan. Find the words in the story that show it.
- Where the money is held decides it. Cash, and bonds that repay before the day, are out of a fall’s reach. Shares and funds are not. The same bill, person and fall can give **A bond for each bill** or **Already covered**, and only where the bill’s money sits tells them apart.
- Money you need soon comes first. When a drifted mix sits beside bills paid by selling, or beside a bill on a date, the bills win.
- Count the cost in dollars before you act. **Already covered** is a real answer: when what you need is already out of a fall’s reach, do nothing, and say why.
- A sum that never changes while the money shrinks, and a sale whose tax bill new money could avoid, both belong to **“Money going out every year”**. For those, the first question wins over the fall.

### 21. A plan, if you want one

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 21 of 21*

This last card is optional, and it is the only one that asks you to decide something. If you want to, write one line you can keep.

A plan is one sentence in two parts: what you will notice, and what you will then do. These are examples to start from. You can change them or write your own.

- I live on money taken out of my investments, I work out one year of spending, and I check how many years of it sit in cash that I do not have to sell from.
- I know a bill of a set size is due on a set date, I look up where the money for it is held, and what it could be worth on the day.
- a statement shows how my money is split, I compare it with the plan I chose, and I follow my written rule if it is outside its limits.
- someone offers me something to protect me from a fall in prices, I ask what in my own situation a fall would hit, and if I cannot name anything, I leave it alone.

Or write your own: If …, then I will …
