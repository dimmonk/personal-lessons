# IT Help Desk: Getting the story

Revision 1, live. Part: Getting the story. Where the subject ends up: Take a ticket you have never seen (a user's email, chat or call about a computer, phone, printer, network, account or security problem) and work it the way a help desk technician does: sort it and set its priority, get the user's story and what changed, try the known fix with a limit, then narrow it down with the cheapest test that splits what works from what does not, fix one thing at a time, confirm with the user and write it up, or hand it on with everything you found; and know every fact and scenario on both CompTIA A+ exams well enough to pass them.

## The why

The email says: "The printer is broken. Nothing comes out. Fix please." Four words about the fault, and a dozen faults they could mean.
Here you learn to get the story before you touch anything: let them tell it, narrow it with an open question, say it back, and search what the desk already knows.

## Step 1: Their words first

A user's first message is a label, not a story: "broken", "slow", "can't get on". The story is what they were doing, what they saw in their own words, and whether it has happened before. Get it before you try anything.
Let them finish without interrupting. Then use an open question, one that cannot be answered yes or no: "What did you see when you pressed Print?" It narrows the problem and lets the user describe it. Yes-or-no questions have their place, but after the story, to confirm one detail: "Does it happen every time?" As a first reply, "Is it plugged in?" gets a yes or a no and little else.
If the answer is still a label ("it does not work") or the user's own idea of the cause ("it's the server"), question them again, openly, until you hear what they saw: a message, a number, a moment. Never argue, brush off what they say, or blame them. What they say is the only evidence you have so far.
A message can hold several problems at once. Let them finish, then find out which one is stopping their work right now. Each problem gets its own ticket, and you start with the one stopping their work.
This desk's rule: when a user clicked past a browser warning on a site they did not mean to open, the ticket also goes to the desk's security team.

## Step 2: Say it back, in plain words

Once you have the story, say it back in a sentence: "So you pressed Print, it says it went to Printer 3-North, and nothing comes out on floor 2. Is that right?" If you got something wrong, they will say so before you act on it.
Use the user's words, not the desk's. Terms and abbreviations the user does not know, and slang, leave them unable to correct you. A wrong guess said as a fact ("So the tray is empty") sounds like an answer, and they may believe it. So does a fact the user never gave, slipped into the sentence: say back only what they told you.

## Step 3: Then search what the desk already knows

The desk keeps a knowledge base: articles that earlier technicians wrote about faults they fixed. Search it early, and again as you learn more, using the words of the fault, such as the exact message on the screen and the program's name. A greeting or a deadline does not describe the fault. Neither does how the fault feels, such as "slow" or "weird".
Each article says what the issue is and the environment it happened in: the kind of device, the program, the version. Read the environment line before you trust the article. One for a Windows laptop will not help on a Mac, even if the symptom is the same. You are only finding the article now. Trying its fix comes later, in the fourth move.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: Start with the usual fix: "Have you tried turning it off and on again?"
The right line: A fix before the story is a guess. It can throw away what was on the screen, and if it works you learn nothing. A restart is a fair first try once you have the story and what changed, but not before. Get the story first, then search, then try the known fix.

## Step 4: A worked example

An email: "Maya Lindqvist", Pharmacy · Monday 9:02 AM.

> Subject: **Printer broken**
>
> The printer is broken. Nothing comes out. Fix please.

An email: "Maya Lindqvist", Pharmacy · Monday 9:08 AM, after your question.

> Subject: **Re: Printer broken**
>
> I press Print and it says it went to Printer 3-North. Nothing comes out here on floor 2.
> I moved up from floor 3 last Friday.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**What do you write back first?**
- Tell me what you see on the screen when you press Print.
- I will have someone check the printer for a jam.

The working, one step at a time:
1. Notice your first guess.: "The printer is broken" suggests a jam or no paper. That is a guess made from four words, so you do not act on it yet.
1. Question her openly.: You put it to her openly: what does she see when she presses Print? She answers in her own words.
1. Let her answer rule your guess out.: It says the page went to Printer 3-North, and she sits on floor 2. A broken printer on floor 2 does not fit that, so the first guess goes.
1. Say the story back.: You repeat what she did and saw, in her words, and let her correct it.
1. Search what the desk already knows.: Next you search the knowledge base with her own words: "prints to wrong printer after moving floors". What changed comes next.

Then the answer: Tell me what you see on the screen when you press Print. / You pressed Print and it says it went to Printer 3-North. Nothing comes out on floor 2, and you moved up last Friday. Is that right?. The reply to "broken" is an open question, not a fix. Her answer names a printer on floor 3 and a move on Friday, which a guess about a broken printer on floor 2 would never have found. Saying it back checks that you have it right before you search or touch anything.
