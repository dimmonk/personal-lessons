## Part 1 of 2: Who made the rule

### 1. Whose rule is it, and what else covers it?

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

When someone says “the town can’t do that”, “Washington already covers that” or “every state sets its own”, check two things before you believe it: who made the rule, and whether a federal law or a right in the Constitution also covers it.

You hear these every week. “You need a permit from the town.” “It varies from state to state.” “The federal government already regulates that.” “The state can require more than the federal minimum.” “They can’t make that illegal.” Each one is about a rule made close to home, and each can end up somewhere different.

You live under three governments at once: the federal government, your state, and your city, town or county. Each makes rules, sometimes about the same thing. Whether a rule stands depends on who made it and on what else covers the same thing. Every story in this unit ends with a decision by a state, a city, a town or a county, and the unit teaches the two questions that come next.

Two words come up all the way through. A rule is anything a state, a city, a town or a county decides that people must follow: a law its lawmakers pass, an order from its governor or mayor, a fee its council sets. Federal means belonging to the government of the whole country, not of one state or city: a federal law applies in every state.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“Who makes the final call?”** Its answers:

- **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**: give this answer when the final call is a vote in the House or Senate, or someone asks Congress for one, such as on a law or money. A law stays Congress’s call even once the President signs it.
- **“The President or a federal agency”**: give this answer when the final call is the President’s or a federal agency’s, or someone is asking them for one, such as an agency enforcing a law or the President refusing to sign one.
- **“A judge, in any court”**: give this answer when the final call is a judge’s, in any court, federal or state, or someone is asking a judge to decide.
- **“A state, city or county government”**: give this answer when the final call is made by a state’s lawmakers, governor or agencies, or a city, town or county, or someone asks them for it. A state court judge counts as a judge. **This unit is about these stories.**

### 2. Left to the states

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-police`]

Start with the simplest story: a state makes a rule, and nothing else gets in the way.

*The deposit law*

> Tenants in the state of Brenmore complained that landlords kept their security deposits for months after the tenants moved out. In March ⟦the Brenmore legislature passed a law⟧ saying that a landlord must return a tenant’s deposit within thirty days of the tenant moving out.

Brenmore’s legislature, the people who make the laws for that one state, decided how fast a landlord must give a deposit back. No city made the rule, no federal law covers deposits, and the rule takes away no right. So the state decides.

Why can a state decide this? The Constitution gives the federal government a short list of jobs: taxes, trade between the states and with other countries, the mail, the army, making money, the rules for becoming a citizen, the federal courts. Renting a home is not on it. Whatever is not on the list stays with the states, and the Tenth Amendment says so.

That is why so much of daily life changes when you cross a state line: deposits, driver’s licenses, marriage, public schools, most crimes, which jobs need a license.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find who made the rule: the Brenmore legislature.** A state’s legislature, its governor and its own offices all count as the state itself.
2. **Find what the rule is about: how soon a landlord must return a deposit.** Renting a home is not on the federal list, so it stays with the states.
3. **Check that nothing else covers it: no federal law on deposits, and no right taken away.** If something did, the state would not decide alone.

This is **Left to the states**: the state decided because nothing else covers the matter. If you hear “the police power”, it does not mean officers. It means the state’s wide power over health and safety.

You may also hear this called “reserved powers” or “the police power”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Left to the states**.

### 4. Local control

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-localgov`]

A state is not the only one that makes rules. Cities, towns and counties make them too: parking, fences, trash, parks.

*The fence rule*

> In the town of Ashby, neighbors kept arguing about tall fences that blocked their front windows. ⟦The state’s law on towns lets each town set rules for its own streets and buildings⟧. Using that power, ⟦the Ashby town council voted⟧ that a front-yard fence may be no taller than four feet.

Ashby’s town council, not the state legislature, voted on the fence rule, and it covers only Ashby. The council could do that because a state law lets each town make rules for its own streets and buildings.

A town’s power does not come from the Constitution. It comes from its state, which hands some of its own power down, usually in a state law or in a charter, the city’s founding document. So the state can usually widen that power, narrow it or take it back.

A rule made by a city, a town or a county is often called an ordinance. Most are about everyday local matters: streets, parking, zoning (which buildings may go where), permits, trash, parks, libraries.

Everything else is the same as the deposit rule: no federal law covers the matter, and no right is taken away. Only the maker is different.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find who made the rule: the Ashby town council.** A city, a town or a county, not the state’s own lawmakers.
2. **Find where it got the power: the state’s law on towns.** A town has only the power its state handed down.
3. **Check the matter is local and nothing else covers it: front-yard fences, no federal law, no right taken away.** A local matter that nothing else covers is the town’s to decide.

This is **Local control**: the town used power its state handed down.

You may also hear this called “a city ordinance” or “home rule”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Local control**.

## Part 2 of 2: A federal law or a right, then the drill

### 9. Federal law wins

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 9 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-preempted`]

So far nothing else covered the rule. Now put a federal law beside it.

*The state immigration scheme*

> ⟦Congress has written detailed laws on who may live in the country and for how long, and those laws are meant to be the only rules⟧. The Lorne legislature then passed its own scheme: state officers must check the immigration status of anyone they stop, and the state will fine anyone it decides has no right to be in the country.

Two governments made rules about the same thing. Congress wrote laws on who may live in the country and for how long, and meant them to be the only rules. Then Lorne wrote a scheme of its own.

Which one counts? The Constitution calls federal law the supreme law of the land. When Congress has power over a matter and has used it, a state rule that gets in the way has to give way. Immigration is a federal power, so a state has no room to add rules of its own.

This does not mean a federal law always beats a state rule. The state’s rule gives way only if Congress meant its law to be the only rule, or if the two rules clash so that nobody could obey both. If no federal law covers the matter, there is nothing for the state’s rule to give way to.

What sinks the scheme is not that a state made it. It is the federal law on the same thing. A city’s rule would give way too.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the state’s rule: Lorne’s scheme to check immigration status.** It is the rule that may have to give way.
2. **Find a federal law on the same matter: Congress’s laws on who may live in the country.** With no federal law on the same matter, there is nothing for it to give way to.
3. **Check that the federal law is meant to be the only rule: the story says so.** Look for “the only rule”, “no state may”, or two rules nobody could obey at once.

This is **Federal law wins**: the federal law pushed the state’s rule aside.

You may also hear this called “preemption” or “the Supremacy Clause”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Federal law wins**.

### 11. Both rules stand

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 11 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-concurrent`]

A federal law does not always shut the states out. Sometimes it sets the least that must be done and lets the states ask for more.

*The minimum wage*

> ⟦A federal law sets a minimum wage that every employer in the country must pay⟧, and ⟦says that a state may set a higher one⟧. The Calder legislature passed a law that employers in Calder must pay a higher minimum wage than the federal one.

Again a federal law and a state rule cover the same thing, and again it is pay. But this time they do not clash. The federal law sets a floor, the least an employer may pay, and says a state may set a higher one. Calder did.

Test it by asking whether one employer can obey both. An employer in Calder who pays the Calder minimum is paying more than the federal minimum, so one payment obeys both rules. Neither is in the other’s way, so neither gives way. If you ever face two rules like this, follow the one that asks for more.

The only difference from the last story is how much room the federal law leaves. There it was the only rule. Here it is just a minimum.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find what the federal law says about the states: it sets a minimum wage and says a state may set a higher one.** Look for “at least”, “a minimum” or “a state may require more”.
2. **Find the state’s rule: Calder’s higher minimum wage.** It asks for more, not for something different.
3. **Check that one payment obeys both: the Calder wage is also above the federal one.** If you can obey both at once, neither has to give way.

This is **Both rules stand**. A city’s rule can stand beside a federal law that leaves room, just as a state’s does.

You may also hear this called “concurrent powers” or “a federal floor”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Both rules stand**.

### 15. Blocked by a right

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 15 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-protected`]

One more thing can stop a state’s, a city’s or a county’s rule, and it needs no federal law: a right in the Constitution.

*The mayor leaflets*

> In the city of Redwick, a group handed out leaflets that said the mayor had wasted money on a new parking lot. The Redwick city council then passed an ordinance ⟦making it a crime to hand out leaflets that criticize the mayor⟧.

Redwick’s council did not tidy anything. It made handing out some leaflets a crime because of what they say about the mayor.

The Constitution protects some rights so strongly that no government may take them away: to speak, to worship, to publish and to gather peacefully. They are in the First Amendment, and criticizing the mayor is speech. The first ten amendments, the Bill of Rights, were first written to limit only the federal government. After the Civil War the Fourteenth Amendment was read to carry those limits to the states, so today they protect you against your state, your city and your county too.

Rights have edges. A city may still set neutral rules about when and where an event happens, such as a noise limit, as long as the rule does not aim at what is said. This ordinance aims at what is said, so it is stopped.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find what the rule does: it makes handing out leaflets that criticize the mayor a crime.** Look at what the rule bans or punishes, not only at who made it.
2. **Check whether that is a protected right: leaflets are speech.** The protected ones are speaking, worshiping, publishing and gathering peacefully.

### 19. The second question: what else covers the matter

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 19 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-else`]

You have answered this at the end of each card. Here it is with its four answers in one place.

**The question:** **“Does a federal law or a right in the Constitution also cover this?”**

**Its answers**

- **“No, neither covers it”**
  - Give this answer when the rule is about something the Constitution leaves to the states, like schools or local streets, no federal law covers it, and it takes away no protected right.
  - Keeps **Left to the states** and **Local control**. Rules out **Federal law wins**, **Both rules stand** and **Blocked by a right**.
- **“A federal law meant to be the only rule”**
  - Give this answer when a federal law on Congress’s own job covers the same thing, and either says no state may set its own rule, or nobody could obey both it and the state or local rule.
  - Keeps **Federal law wins**. Rules out **Left to the states**, **Local control**, **Both rules stand** and **Blocked by a right**.
- **“A federal law that leaves room for it”**
  - Give this answer when a federal law covers the same thing but sets only a minimum, or lets the states act too, so obeying the state or local rule also obeys the federal one.
  - Keeps **Both rules stand**. Rules out **Left to the states**, **Local control**, **Federal law wins** and **Blocked by a right**.
- **“A right the rule takes away”**
  - Give this answer when the rule takes away a right the Constitution protects, like free speech or worship, and those rights apply to every state, city and county too.
  - Keeps **Blocked by a right**. Rules out **Left to the states**, **Local control**, **Federal law wins** and **Both rules stand**.

**Why it matters**

A state or local rule depends on what else covers the same thing. A federal law meant to be the only rule wins. A federal minimum leaves room. A right the Constitution protects blocks the rule, whoever made it. And when none of these covers it, the state decides, or the city or county it gave the power to.

Six pairs of names have no card of their own, and this question alone separates them. The list below puts each pair side by side.

With **Left to the states** or **Local control**, nothing else covers the matter. With the other three, something does: a federal law that is the only rule, or a right, stops the rule, and a federal minimum lets it stand.

**How to answer it**

1. **Start with what the rule is about.** A matter off the federal list, such as schools or licenses, usually has no federal law on it.
2. **If a federal law is named, check that it covers the same matter.** A story can mention Congress or a federal tax in passing and then go on to something else.
3. **Then read what that law says about the states: no state may add to it, or a state may ask for more.** The first is **“A federal law meant to be the only rule”** and the second is **“A federal law that leaves room for it”**.
4. **Whatever you found, ask whether the rule takes away a right to speak, worship, publish or gather peacefully.** That is **“A right the rule takes away”**, and it needs no federal law.
5. **If none of these covers it, the answer is “No, neither covers it”.** Then who made the rule gives the name: **Left to the states** for a state, **Local control** for a city, town or county.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

- Left to the states or Federal law wins: Has Congress already written a law on this same matter, and is it meant to be the only rule?
- Left to the states or Both rules stand: Is there a federal law on this same matter at all?
- Left to the states or Blocked by a right: Does the rule take away a right to speak, to worship, to publish or to gather peacefully?
- Local control or Federal law wins: Does a federal law already cover this matter and say that it is the only rule?
- Local control or Both rules stand: Is the local rule alone on this matter, or is there a federal law beside it?
- Local control or Blocked by a right: Does the rule aim at what people say, believe or publish, or does it only say where, when or how?
- Federal law wins or Both rules stand: Does the federal law say that it is the only rule, or that it is a minimum with room for the states?
- Federal law wins or Blocked by a right: What stops the rule: another law on the same matter, or a right that the rule takes away?
- Both rules stand or Blocked by a right: Does the rule stand beside the federal side, or does it take away something that the federal side protects?

### 22. What to carry away

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 22 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap`]

You can now ask both questions on your own.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

Who made the rule: the state, or a city or county?
- The state made it → Left to the states · Federal law wins · Both rules stand · Blocked by a right
- A city, town or county made it → Local control · Federal law wins · Both rules stand · Blocked by a right

Does a federal law or a right in the Constitution also cover this?
- No, neither covers it → Left to the states · Local control
- A federal law meant to be the only rule → Federal law wins
- A federal law that leaves room for it → Both rules stand
- A right the rule takes away → Blocked by a right

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Left to the states**: a rule the state makes on something the Constitution leaves to the states, like schools or licenses, with no federal law on it and no right it takes away.
- **Local control**: a rule a city, town or county makes, with power its state handed down to it, no federal law on the same thing, and no right the rule takes away.
- **Federal law wins**: a state or local rule, and a federal law on the same thing that is meant to be the only rule, or that the state or local rule contradicts.
- **Both rules stand**: a state or local rule, a federal law on the same thing, and room for both: the federal law sets only a minimum, or lets the states act too.
- **Blocked by a right**: a state or local rule, and a right the Constitution protects (to speak, to worship, to gather) that the rule takes away.

**To carry away**

- Ask both questions of every rule you hear about, and find the words that answer each: who made the rule, and what else covers the matter. If you can’t find them, you don’t have an answer yet.
- How a story sounds never decides it. A matter that sounds local can be covered by a federal law, as cribs were. What sounds like a right can be a neutral time limit, as in the park.
- Who made the rule separates only two names, **Left to the states** and **Local control**. For the other three, a state’s rule and a city’s rule get the same name.
- A federal law in the story is not enough. It has to cover the same matter, and then you ask whether it is the only rule or leaves room. “Federal law always beats state law” is wrong.
- A city, a town or a county has only the power its state handed it. A right in the Constitution applies to a state, a city and a county just as it applies to Congress.
