## Part 1 of 3: The people behind a number, how it is counted, what it is set beside

### 1. Before you believe a number, find where it could fool you

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 1 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient-claim`]

Before you share, believe or act on a number in a headline, an ad or a forwarded message, find the first place it could be fooling you, or see that it holds up.

A headline says, "Nine in ten parents want school to start later." A neighbor posts, "Burglaries on our road are up 200%!" An ad says, "People who take our vitamin catch fewer colds." Each one is a claim: a sentence with a number in it, which the lessons call its figure.

A claim is built in four parts, and each rests on the one before. First someone counts some people or things. Then the count is read as showing something real. Then it is set beside something. Sometimes the claim also says one thing caused another. So you look for the first part that goes wrong, and sometimes the answer is that none does.

Where does this claim first go wrong?
- Who was counted → the wrong people counted, or too few
- What the number counts → the figure can move without the real thing moving
- What it is compared with → something you need beside the figure is missing
- What caused what → something else could explain the result
- Nothing goes wrong → every part checks out

The unit has three parts, and you can stop after any of them.

1. The people behind a number, how it is counted, what it is set beside
2. Cause, claims that hold, and the question to ask first
3. One whole claim, then the drill

Each starts from a real story. After each one you answer a quick question, and the reason is shown right away. Nothing here is graded. A miss only decides what comes back.

### 2. Who was counted

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 2 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-counted`]

First: who the number comes from.

*The golf club*

> A reporter ⟦stood outside the town golf club one Saturday morning and asked 50 people⟧ whether the town needs a new golf course. Forty-five said yes. Her article is headed: 'Nine in ten townspeople want a new golf course.'

The reporter added up her answers correctly, and the 45 people did say yes. The trouble is who she asked. People outside a golf club on a Saturday morning are mostly golfers, and golfers want a new golf course more than most. The number may be true of golfers and tell you almost nothing about the town.

The people behind a number can fail in two ways. They may not look like the group the claim talks about, as here. Or there may be so few that luck moves the number: if three of four people say yes, a fifth could turn "three in four" into "four in five".

**How to spot it**

1. **Find who the number comes from: 50 people outside the golf club.** Every number comes from some people or things, and the claim often does not say who.
2. **Find who the claim is about: everyone in town.** The claim speaks for them, not only for the people asked.
3. **Ask whether the first group looks like the second: golfers do not look like the whole town.** If they do not, the number is about the wrong people.
4. **Check there are enough of them: 50 is fine here, but with 3 people one more would change the number.** Too few is the other way to go wrong.

This is **“Who was counted”**. Check it first, because every later part of a claim rests on the people behind the number.

### 4. What the number counts

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 4 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-measure`]

Next: what the number really measures.

*The emergency rooms*

> A hospital group announces: 'Waiting time in our emergency rooms has fallen from six hours to four.' This year the group changed when the clock starts. ⟦It used to start when a patient walked in. It now starts when a nurse first sees them⟧.

Every patient is in the number in both years, so the people are fine. What changed is the clock. It used to start when a patient walked in. Now it starts when a nurse first sees them, so the wait in the waiting room no longer counts. The number can fall from six hours to four while every patient waits exactly as long as before.

The same thing happens when a new form counts more or fewer things as one sort, when a new gadget reads higher than the old one, or when people are paid on the number and work on the number instead of the real thing. The number moves and the real thing stays put.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the real thing the number stands for: how long patients wait.** A claim uses a number to stand in for something you care about.
2. **Find how the number is counted: the clock starts when a nurse first sees them.** The way it is counted decides what it can show.
3. **Look for a change in the counting: last year the clock started at the door.** A change in the counting can move the number by itself.
4. **Ask whether the number could move with no real change: it could fall with every patient waiting as long as before.** If it can, the number does not show the real thing.

This is **“What the number counts”**. It covers anything a number measures: hours, dollars, scores, reports.

### 6. Who was counted or What the number counts: telling them apart

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 6 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-counted-measure`]

These two are easy to mix up, because in both the number can be added up right and still mislead you.

**Story A**

> Willow School says its students' reading scores rose from 61 to 70 this year. The 70 is the average for the 11 students who volunteered to stay after class for an extra test. The school has 340 students, and last year's 61 was the average for all of them.

**Story B**

> Willow School says its students' reading scores rose from 61 to 70 this year. All 340 students took the test in both years. This year's test was the shorter version, with easier passages, which the test's maker brought out to replace the old one.

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the same school and the same rise in reading scores. Compare one thing: is the trouble in who is in the number, or in how the number is counted?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“What the number counts”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the test is the same, but this year’s average comes from 11 volunteers out of 340 students, and last year’s came from all of them. Volunteers for an extra test are not a fair picture of the school. That is **“Who was counted”**.

In Story B every student took the test both years, so nobody is missing. But this year’s test is shorter, with easier passages, so scores can rise with every student reading as well as before. That is **“What the number counts”**.

Check who is in the number first. Only then ask what it counts.

**How to tell them apart**

Is the trouble in who the number comes from, or in how it is counted? Check the people first. If they are fine, ask whether the way it is counted could move it on its own.


### 7. What it is compared with

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 7 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-compare`]

Next: what the number is set beside.

*The burglaries*

> A neighborhood newsletter says: '⟦Burglaries on Elm Road are up 300% this month⟧!' It does not say how many burglaries there were last month or this month. The police log counts every burglary the same way each month.

"Up 300%" sounds like a crime wave. But it means four times as many, and the newsletter never says four times what. One burglary becoming four is up 300%. So is 20 becoming 80. The words are the same and the news is completely different.

The same hiding happens elsewhere. "Cuts your risk by half" is tiny if the risk was 2 in 10,000. A test that is "right 99 times in 100" leaves out how rare the thing is. Two totals side by side leave out that one is made of harder jobs. In each, the number comes without what you need beside it.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the percentage, the test score or the total: up 300%.** Those are the forms that hide the numbers behind them.
2. **Ask what it is a percentage of: how many burglaries last month?** A percentage means nothing until you know what it is out of.
3. **Look for the numbers behind it: the newsletter gives none.** Without them you cannot tell one burglary becoming four from 20 becoming 80.
4. **If they are missing, treat the claim as unreadable until you find them.** It may be true, but you cannot tell whether it matters.

This is **“What it is compared with”**. A number means nothing until it is set beside something, and this claim leaves that out.

## Part 2 of 3: Cause, claims that hold, and the question to ask first

### 9. What caused what

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 9 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-cause`]

Last of the four parts: a claim that something worked, or caused something. It comes last because it rests on the other three.

*The music class*

> At Kent High School, the 120 students who take the music class average 71 on the math exam. The 380 who do not take it average 62. The principal says: '⟦Music lessons raise math scores⟧.' The class costs $30 a term, and nearly all of the music students' families also pay for extra math coaching.

The numbers show that music students score higher on the math exam. The principal says the music lessons did it. That is a bigger claim than the numbers, and the numbers alone cannot carry it. Almost all the music students also pay for math coaching, and the coaching alone could lift their scores.

Two things can go together without one causing the other. To test a claim of cause, look for another way to get the same numbers. Music lessons may well help. The numbers just cannot show it.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the word that says one thing made the other happen: "raise".** Words like "raise", "works", "because" and "led to" belong to the speaker, not to the numbers.
2. **Find how people ended up in each group: the students chose music, and their families chose the coaching.** When people sort themselves into groups, the groups differ in other ways too.
3. **Look for another way to get the same numbers: the music families also pay for math coaching.** If something else fits the numbers, they cannot prove the cause.

This is **“What caused what”**. It does not say the claim is false, only that the numbers cannot show it.

### 12. Nothing goes wrong

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 12 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-holds`]

Last answer: a claim that checks out in every part. It is what is left when none of the other four fits.

*The county survey*

> A county health office phoned 1,100 adults whose numbers were ⟦drawn by lottery from the full list of landline and cell phone numbers in the county. It tried each number up to six times and reached 1,000 of them⟧. Thirty-one percent of the 1,000 said they smoke. The office reports: 'About three in ten adults in the county smoke, give or take three points.'

The health office did its homework. It phoned adults whose numbers were drawn by lottery from the whole county, and it kept trying until almost all of them answered. It asked one question, the same way, of everyone. And it claims only how many adults smoke, not why and not whether that is rising.

This does not mean the number is exactly right: the office itself says "give or take three points". It means the claim is safe to use for what it says, and it goes no further.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check who is in the number: phone numbers drawn by lottery, nearly all reached.** Nobody was picked by hand, and few were missed.
2. **Check what is counted: whether a person says they smoke, asked the same way every time.** Nothing about the counting changed, and nobody could push it.
3. **Check what it is set beside: nothing, because the claim is only how many smoke.** A claim that stays with its own number needs nothing beside it.
4. **Check for a claim of cause: there is none.** With no cause claimed, there is nothing for something else to explain.
5. **Find the exact words that show each part holding.** You can only find nothing wrong by looking.

This is **“Nothing goes wrong”**. It is not a failure to find an answer: it tells you the claim is safe for what it says.

### 14. Who was counted or Nothing goes wrong: telling them apart

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 14 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-counted-holds`]

A sound claim and a shaky one can sound the same. The way to see the difference is to take the same claim with and without the problem.

**Story A**

> Brindle Café sent a survey to the 800 customers on its mailing list. Seventy-two replied, and 65 of them said they love the new menu. Nobody was asked again. The owner says: 'Nine in ten of our regulars love the new menu.'

**Story B**

> Brindle Café sent a survey to the 800 customers on its mailing list, then rang everyone who had not replied. In all, 720 answered, and 648 of them said they love the new menu. The owner says: 'Nine in ten of the customers on our mailing list love the new menu.'

**Story A**

### 16. The question to ask first

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 16 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-gate`]

Here is the question and its five answers in one place.

**The question:** **“Where does this claim first go wrong?”**

**Its answers**

- **“Who was counted”**
  - Give this answer when the people or things the figure came from do not match the group the claim is about, or are too few to trust.
- **“What the number counts”**
  - Give this answer when the figure could rise, fall or differ without the real thing it is taken to show doing the same.
- **“What it is compared with”**
  - Give this answer when the figure is a percentage change, a test’s accuracy, or a total beside another total, and the claim leaves out what you need beside it to read it fairly.
- **“What caused what”**
  - Give this answer when the claim says one thing made another happen, and the same result could have come about another way.
- **“Nothing goes wrong”**
  - Give this answer when every part the claim uses checks out in order, and none of them goes wrong.

**Why it matters**

A claim built on numbers is put together in order: someone counts some people or things, the figure stands for something real, it is set beside something else, and it may be said to show a cause. Each part rests on the one before, so the first part that goes wrong spoils the rest, and which part it is tells you what to ask next. If no part goes wrong, the claim holds as far as it goes.

If the people counted are the wrong people, a careful look at how the number was counted is a careful look at something that cannot hold. If you take a rise in a number for a real rise when the counting changed, you go hunting for the cause of something that never happened.

**How to answer it**

1. **Read the whole claim before you answer.** The sentence that says how people were picked, or that something changed, is often the last one.
2. **First look at the people behind the number: “Who was counted”.** If they are the wrong people, nothing built on them can hold.
3. **Then look at the counting: “What the number counts”.** The right people can still be counted with a clock that moved.
4. **Then look at what it is set beside: “What it is compared with”.** A percentage or a total can be right and still hide what you need.
5. **Then look for a claim of cause: “What caused what”.** A claim of cause is only as sound as the numbers under it.
6. **None of the four fits? It is “Nothing goes wrong”.** Then every part checks out, as far as the claim goes.
7. **Find the exact words that show your answer.** If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Some claims go wrong in two places at once, such as a claim that a program works that is built on the members who stayed in it. Use the order above: the earlier part wins, because everything after it rests on it. The test for each pair is below.

- Who was counted or What the number counts: Is the trouble in who the number comes from, or in how it is counted? Check the people first. If they are fine, ask whether the way it is counted could move it on its own.
- What the number counts or What it is compared with: Could the counting have changed, or been pushed? Or is it counted the same way throughout, with something you need beside the number left out?
- What it is compared with or What caused what: Is something you need beside the number missing, such as the numbers behind a percentage? Or are all the numbers given, and does the claim say one thing made the other happen?
- Who was counted or What caused what: Check who is in the number before you look at the claim. Are they a fair picture, and enough of them? If so, does the claim say one thing caused another, with another way to explain the result?
- Who was counted or Nothing goes wrong: How many of the people asked are in the number, how did they get in, and does the claim speak for more than they stand for?
- What the number counts or Nothing goes wrong: Was the number counted the same way all the way through, with the same tool and the same effort to find things? Could anyone raise it without more of the real thing?
- What it is compared with or Nothing goes wrong: Are the numbers behind the comparison given, are the two things alike, and were they counted the same way? Is anything you would need beside the number missing?
- What caused what or Nothing goes wrong: Who decided which group each person or thing was in: they did, their circumstances did, or a lottery did? Is there another way to explain the result?
- What the number counts or What caused what: Could the number have risen without the real thing moving? If not, does the claim say one thing caused another, with another way to explain the result?
- Who was counted or What it is compared with: Check who is in the number before you look at its form. Are they a fair picture, and enough of them? If so, does the form leave out something you need beside the number?

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 19 of 20*

You have now answered the first question on your own.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

Where does this claim first go wrong?
- Who was counted
- What the number counts
- What it is compared with
- What caused what
- Nothing goes wrong

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **“Who was counted”**: the people or things the figure came from, and either a reason they do not match the group the claim is about, or so few that one or two more would change it.
- **“What the number counts”**: a figure taken to show something real (good service, safe roads, how much illness there is), and something else that could push it up or down.
- **“What it is compared with”**: a percentage change, a test’s accuracy, or a total next to another total, and something left out that you need beside it to read it fairly.
- **“What caused what”**: a claim that one thing made another happen, and another way the same result could have come about.
- **“Nothing goes wrong”**: nothing wrong at any step: a fair picture of the group, a figure that moves only with the real thing, a fair thing to set it beside, and for a cause, a random split.

**To carry away**

- Before you give an answer, find the exact words that show it. If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.
- Check the parts in order and stop at the first one that goes wrong: the people behind the number, how it is counted, what it is set beside, and whether one thing is said to cause another.
- A number can be added up right and still be unable to show what the claim says. Checking the sum is not checking the claim.
- **“Nothing goes wrong”** is an answer too, and the most useful one when it is true. Give it only after you have checked every part.
- Finding a problem does not make a claim false. It tells you what the number cannot show, and what you would need to see.
- Where a claim appeared changes nothing. A famous journal and a neighbor’s newsletter get the same checks.
- When a claim goes wrong in two places, the earlier one wins, because everything after it rests on it.
