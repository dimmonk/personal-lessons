# Wealth Preservation: The full match, then the order of saving

Revision 1, live. Part: The full match, then the order of saving. Where the subject ends up: Run a planner's yearly check on your own US accounts: list everything, see what money you need soon is exposed and what single loss could ruin you, find the certain leaks (yearly costs in dollars, a missed match, tax in the wrong place, foreign filings) and the risks you did not choose (one big bet, a drifted mix), check who inherits and who can act, and change only what the check calls for.

## The why

Nina’s employer adds 50 cents for each dollar she puts into her 401(k), on up to 6% of her pay. She puts in 3%, and $960 a year of her employer’s money never comes.
Here you find the rate that takes the whole match, the dollars left behind, and then where the next dollar of saving goes.

## Step 1: The match

The match rule is in your plan’s summary, not on your pay stub. It says what the employer adds for each dollar you put in (50 cents, say), and on how much of your pay (up to 6%, say). Put in at least that much.
Some rules match in steps: one dollar per dollar on the first 3% of pay, then 50 cents on the next 2%. The whole match then takes 5%, where the last step ends.
What you leave each year: the match at the rate where it stops, less the match at your rate. Your stub may show dollars: what goes in each paycheck ÷ the gross pay that paycheck × 100 is your rate.
Vesting says when the employer’s money becomes yours; leave the job before, and you may lose some of it. It does not change the rate to put in. Your own money is always yours.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “The match is 50 cents on the dollar up to 6%, so putting in 3% is good enough.”
The right line: The 50 cents is what the employer adds per dollar; the 6% is what you must put in to get all of it. At 3%, half the match is left behind.

## Step 2: A worked example

A pay stub, "Northgate Medical: pay stub for Nina" (a tab named "Pay stub", one of a household's papers: one open at a time, the first at the start, and the one with a marked line after the answer):

> Line  |  Amount  (the column heads)
> Gross pay this period  |  $2,461.54
> Yearly salary  |  $64,000
> 401(k), this period  |  $73.85

A match rule, "Northgate Medical 401(k): the employer match" (a tab named "Match rule", one of a household's papers: one open at a time, the first at the start, and the one with a marked line after the answer):

> Rule  |  What it says  (the column heads)
> The employer adds  |  50 cents for each dollar you put in
> On what you put in  |  up to 6% of your pay
> Vesting  |  All of it is yours after 3 years of work
> (From the plan’s summary. Your money goes in from each paycheck; the employer’s follows it.)

The working, one step at a time:
1. Turn the stub’s dollars into a share of pay. $73.85 ÷ $2,461.54 × 100 = 3%.
1. Find where the match stops on the match rule. The employer adds 50 cents per dollar, on what Nina puts in up to 6% of pay. So 6% takes it all.
1. Work out the match at both rates. At 6%: $64,000 × 6% × 0.50 = $1,920.00. At 3%: $960.00. Left each year: $960.00.
1. Change the rate only if it is under where the match stops. Raise the rate to 6%. Vesting changes when the money is yours, not the rate.

Then the answer: 3% / 6% / $960.00 a year / Change it. The match stops at 6% of pay and Nina puts in 3%, so $960.00 a year of the employer’s money is left. Raising the rate to 6% takes it all.

## Step 5: The order of saving

A common order, not a law; versions differ on the first step. Cash for about a month, so a surprise does not go on a card. The whole match: money no fund can promise. A credit card at a high rate, far above what savings pay: paying it off earns that rate for sure. Then cash up to three months.
Then the accounts with tax help. An HSA, if your health plan allows one: money goes in untaxed, grows untaxed, and comes out untaxed for medical bills. An IRA: you pick the fund, so its cost can be lower than your plan’s list. More in the 401(k), up to its higher limit. A brokerage account last: no tax help. “Fill” means up to the yearly limit below.
Leave out a step that is done, that you owe nothing on, or that is not open to you.
