# IT Help Desk: When the user is upset

Revision 2, live. Part: When the user is upset. Where the subject ends up: Take a ticket you have never seen (a user's email, chat or call about a computer, phone, printer, network, account or security problem) and work it the way a help desk technician does: sort it and set its priority, get the user's story and what changed, try the known fix with a limit, then narrow it down with the cheapest test that splits what works from what does not, fix one thing at a time, confirm with the user and write it up, or hand it on with everything you found; and know every fact and scenario on both CompTIA A+ exams well enough to pass them.

## The why

Rui writes: this is the third time this week their laptop has locked them out, nobody there knows what they are doing, and they have a client call at 2. Almost anything you could say first makes it worse: the earlier tickets were closed because they did not answer, or it is probably nothing.
You learn here what to say in the first minute with an upset user: show you heard them, say what happens next and when, and keep the time you gave, without promising a result you do not control.

## Step 1: Three things not to do

A user who cannot do their work is often upset. Three replies feel natural and make it worse:
Arguing or defending the desk (“the earlier tickets were closed because you did not reply”). Dismissing the problem (“it is probably nothing”). Judging the user (“you should have restarted when we told you”).
The A+ list for dealing with difficult users names the same three: do not argue or be defensive, do not dismiss their problem, and do not be judgmental.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “I know why it happened, so I will explain whose fault it was.”
The right line: Telling them whose fault it was is arguing, even when you are right. First show you heard them, then say what you will do and when.

## Step 2: What to do instead

Let them finish, and do not interrupt. Then say back what they told you, including what it has stopped them doing (“three lockouts this week, and a call at 2”), and say you are sorry for the trouble. “I understand” alone is not saying it back: it repeats nothing they said. Being sorry for what happened to them is not the same as saying the desk was at fault.
Put an open question if you need more, one that needs more than yes or no (“tell me what you saw when it dropped”). When you do not yet know enough to act, the question can be your whole first move. Otherwise say what happens next and when, in words they know: your next step, and the time you will write or call.
Before you reset a password or unlock an account for a caller or a chat, confirm that they are who they say, with the desk’s own check, which uses something the caller did not supply: the number on file, the company directory, a manager looked up there. This is the desk’s own rule against impersonation. An upset person asking for a reset in a hurry is exactly what someone pretending to be them sounds like. If the check fails, say sorry, say what you can do instead, and give a time.
Promise your steps and the times you will report back, never a result that is not in your hands (“it will never happen again”). Then keep the time, even if all you have is “no news yet, and here is what I am doing”.
A polite check is not arguing. If the user says to close the ticket, you can still tell them you need one minute for them to try the fix themselves.

## Step 3: What backs this up

The evidence is limited and comes from shops and banks, not help desks: an apology together with a good explanation raised satisfaction, and an employee’s explanation of what went wrong lowered anger. So when the user wants to know why, give the cause in plain words (“the roller that pulls paper in is worn, so it jams”); do not use an explanation to defend the desk (“the earlier tickets were closed because you did not reply”). The first tells them what is wrong with the printer; the second tells them whose fault the history was. The A+ exam list names the same three things not to do.

> Subject: **Locked out AGAIN**
>
> This is the THIRD time this week my laptop has locked me out.
> Nobody there knows what they are doing.
> I have a client call at 2 and I cannot even sign in.

Rui has three tickets this week for the same lockout.

> Rule  (the column heads)
> The same fault on three tickets in a week is raised as a problem.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**What is your first reply to Rui?**
(the options come in an order that depends on the seed of the question)
- Three lockouts in a week, with a client call at 2, is a real problem and I am sorry. I need to check it is you with the desk’s usual questions; then I unlock you, and I will write you by 1:00 either way.
- I am sorry about the trouble, Rui, but the earlier tickets were closed because nobody answered our follow-up. After the desk’s questions I unlock you, and I will write you by 1:00 either way.
- I am sorry about the three lockouts, Rui. After the desk’s questions I unlock you now, and I guarantee it will never lock you out again. I will write you by 1:00 either way.

The working, one step at a time:
1. Do not defend the desk.: They are right that it has happened three times. Explaining why the earlier tickets were closed would be arguing, and it does not get them to the call. Say that you heard them.
1. Confirm it is them.: They are upset and in a hurry, which is how someone pretending to be them would sound too. Before any unlock, the desk’s own check comes first: its usual questions, answered correctly.
1. Say what happens next, and when.: They need to know that someone has it and when they will hear. Your steps and a time you will report back are in your hands. A result such as “it will never lock you out again” is not.
1. Follow up at the time you said.: At 1:00 you write to them, even if you have no answer yet. A user who hears nothing feels ignored a second time. The desk’s rule raises the same fault on three tickets in a week as a problem, and Rui has three, so you hand the pattern on as a problem.

Then the answer: Three lockouts in a week, with a client call at 2, is a real problem and I am sorry. I need to check it is you with the desk’s usual questions; then I unlock you, and I will write you by 1:00 either way. They are upset because their work is stopped. The reply says that you heard it (three times, a call at 2), says you are sorry for the trouble, does the desk’s check, and gives your next steps and a time. It does not argue about the earlier tickets and it does not promise a result you do not control.
