When the police stop you, when tax comes out of your pay, or when a letter calls you to a courthouse, you know what the law gives you, what it asks of you, and what nobody promised. You can say each fact without looking it up, at an interview or at a kitchen table.

### 2. A right holds the government back from you

*Unit Eight · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 54*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-speak`]

Most of the rights in the Constitution are limits on the government. The first group has five of them.

*Hye-won’s month*

> Hye-won came here two years ago on a student visa and is not a citizen. In one month she goes to her place of worship every Friday, writes a letter to the town newspaper that criticizes the mayor’s plan to close the library, joins a peaceful rally outside the town hall against the plan, and signs a petition asking the council to keep the library open. The mayor reads her letter and is angry, and he wishes that he could make her stop. He cannot. Nobody asks whether she is a citizen.

The mayor was angry about her letter, her rally and her petition, and he could do nothing about any of them. Nobody even asked if she was a citizen. That is what a right is in this unit: something the Constitution says the government may not do to a person.

The First Amendment (an amendment is a change added to the Constitution) stops the government from punishing you for five things: what you say, what you believe, what you print, gathering peacefully, and asking the government to fix something wrong. Like most of the first ten amendments, the Bill of Rights, it says “no person”, “the people” or “the accused”, never “citizens”. So it protects everyone here, whatever their immigration status. After the Civil War the Fourteenth Amendment made the same limits apply to states and cities.

You have already met two names for this. When a story shows Congress taking one of these rights away, the name is **Beyond Congress’s power**. When a state or a city does, the name is **Blocked by a right**. This group is the five rights themselves.


### 3. The five freedoms of the First Amendment

*Unit Eight · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 3 of 54*

[reviewers only: card kind `facts`, id `facts-speak`]

These are the five rights of the First Amendment. Each comes with how it shows up in real life.

| The fact | The answer |
|---|---|
| Which right stops the government from punishing you for what you say? | The right to free speech |
| Which right protects your choice to follow a religion, or none? | The right to practice a religion, or none |
| Which right stops a mayor from having a newsstand pull a magazine he dislikes? | The right to a free press |
| Which right lets people gather peacefully, for example at a rally in a public park? | The right to assemble peacefully |
| Which right lets you ask the government to fix something wrong? | The right to petition the government |

**How each fact fits the idea**

- **The right to free speech**: You can criticize the mayor out loud and he cannot punish you for it. The law says “no person”, so a visitor is covered the same as a citizen.
- **The right to practice a religion, or none**: The government cannot punish you for the religion you follow, or for following none. Hye-won can go to her place of worship every Friday.
- **The right to a free press**: “The press” means newspapers and magazines. No government, not even a city’s, can stop them from being printed or sold because it dislikes what they say.
- **The right to assemble peacefully**: A law from Congress banning every political rally in a public park would take this right away. Hye-won’s rally outside the town hall is protected.
- **The right to petition the government**: Signing a petition asking the council to keep a library open is using it, and everyone here can.

### 10. When the government says you committed a crime

*Unit Eight · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 10 of 54*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-accused`]

The First Amendment holds the government back from your words and beliefs. Four more rights hold it back when it accuses you of a crime.

*Kofi’s trial*

> Kofi is here on a student visa. Police ask a judge for a warrant, which is the judge’s written permission, and search his apartment with it. They find a stolen bicycle and charge him with theft. At the police station he tells them that he wants a lawyer and that he will say nothing until he has one. He cannot pay for a lawyer, so the court appoints one for him. Some months later his trial is held in public, before a jury of ordinary people, within the time that the court set.

Every step of Kofi’s story is the government held back, or held to a promise, because it says he committed a crime. The Fourth, Fifth and Sixth Amendments, like the First, never say “citizen”. They say “the people” and “the accused”. So Kofi has the same right to a lawyer, and the same right to stay silent, as a citizen charged with the same crime.

The Constitution asks for fair steps before the government can take away a person’s freedom. People call this due process. When a judge is asked whether an accused person got these steps, the name is **The rights of the accused**. This group is four of those steps.


### 11. Four rights if you are accused of a crime

*Unit Eight · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 11 of 54*

[reviewers only: card kind `facts`, id `facts-accused`]

These are the four rights in Kofi’s story. Each comes with how it showed up for him.

| The fact | The answer |
|---|---|
| Which right protects your home from a search without a warrant or a good reason? | The right to be safe from unreasonable searches |
| Which right means you cannot be forced to speak against yourself when police question you about a crime? | The right to remain silent |
| Which right means the court appoints a lawyer for you if you are charged with a crime and cannot pay for one? | The right to a lawyer |
| Which right means a jury of ordinary people decides, in public and without long delay, when you are charged with a serious crime? | The right to a speedy public jury trial |

**How each fact fits the idea**

- **The right to be safe from unreasonable searches**: This is why police generally need a warrant, a judge’s written permission, before they search. Kofi’s police asked a judge first.
- **The right to remain silent**: No one can be forced to testify against themselves. Kofi tells the police he will say nothing until he has a lawyer.
- **The right to a lawyer**: The Sixth Amendment says “the accused”, never “citizens”. Kofi gets a lawyer the same way a citizen would.
- **The right to a speedy public jury trial**: Kofi’s trial was in public, in front of a jury, within the time the court set. Anyone accused of a crime here gets the same.

### 17. A duty is something the law asks of you

*Unit Eight · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 17 of 54*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-duty`]

The last two groups were about the government held back from you. The other half of living here is what the law asks of you.

*Ravi’s first year*

> Ravi is 24 and is here on a work visa. His first payslip shows tax taken out of his wages, and each spring he files a tax return. He keeps to the speed limit on the road to work. A colleague tells him that every man between 18 and 25 who lives here has to register for Selective Service, the list that the country would use if it ever ordered a draft. Ravi looks up the rule, and registers.

Ravi’s story has no right in it. A right holds the government back. A duty goes the other way: the law asks something of you. The three in this group fall on everyone here, citizen or not, because of what a person does: lives here, earns wages here, or is a man between 18 and 25.

Everyone here must obey the law. Everyone here must pay tax on income earned here. A man aged 18 to 25 who lives here must register for Selective Service. That is the list the country would use to call people up for the armed forces, which is called a draft, if one were ever ordered.

Not knowing a requirement is no excuse for missing it. If you are unsure, ask the *agency* that runs the rule.

### 28. A right is not a promise to give you something

*Unit Eight · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 28 of 54*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-promise`]

Rights hold the government back, and duties ask something of you. Many newcomers also expect a third thing: that the government will give them something.

*Ines looks for the promise*

> Ines moved here from a country whose constitution promises every person free medical care and a job. After she loses her job, she reads the United States Constitution from start to finish, looking for the page that promises her either one, or a home. She finds long lists of what government may not do, and no promise of work, a home or medical care. At a community center a neighbor tells her that the program called Medicare exists because Congress passed a law, and that people argue about what it should cover at every election.

Ines went looking for a promise and found a list of limits. The Constitution mostly lists what the government may not do to you. It does not promise a job, a home or medical care, even though the constitution of her old country does.

When the government does provide one of these, a law or a state or city program created it. Medicare exists because Congress passed a law, and what one law gives, a later law can change. A right in the Constitution is a limit on the government, not a program.

### 33. Criminal case or civil case

*Unit Eight · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 33 of 54*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-hearing`]

The group on the accused promised a lawyer to a person charged with a crime. This group is about a hearing where that promise works differently.

*Luis and Pavel*

> On the same Monday, two men who cannot pay for a lawyer are at a hearing. Luis is charged with stealing a bicycle, and the court appoints a lawyer to defend him. Pavel is in an immigration hearing. That is all this course holds. It does not say which other rights still apply in an immigration hearing. If you face a real hearing, ask someone trained in immigration law.

### 39. The oath that makes you a citizen

*Unit Eight · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 39 of 54*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-oath`]

So far this unit is about living here under the Constitution. The oath is the moment a person promises to live under it as a citizen.

*Fatima’s ceremony*

> Fatima passed her test last month. This morning she stands in a courtroom with a hundred other people from forty countries. They raise their right hands and say the same words together: that they give up loyalty to other countries, and that they will support and defend the Constitution and the laws of the United States. When they finish, an official declares them citizens. That evening Fatima’s daughter, who was born here, tells her that every morning at school the class stands and says a few lines to the flag.

Becoming a citizen ends with the Oath of Allegiance, a promise you make once, at the ceremony that makes you a citizen. “Allegiance” means loyalty. In it you give up loyalty to other countries, swear to support and defend the Constitution and the laws of the United States, and promise to serve the country in the armed forces or in civilian work of national importance when the law requires it.

The oath is not the Pledge of Allegiance, the few lines schoolchildren say to the flag. The pledge is no part of becoming a citizen.

Whether the country you came from treats giving up loyalty as giving up its citizenship depends on its own law. Ask its authorities before the ceremony, and do not assume.

### 47. The citizenship test, and who sets its details

*Unit Eight · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 47 of 54*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-test`]

The oath comes at the end of the process, and the test comes before it. Who decides how the test works is something you have already learned.

*Chidi’s interview*

> Chidi has his interview. The officer asks him questions out loud about the government and the history of the United States, and he answers out loud. The officer also has him read and write a sentence in English. He had studied from a list of questions that the immigration service publishes, and he knew in advance that the questions would come from it. His friend Dana, who went through the same interview two years ago, studied an older version of the list and warns him that the list has changed since then. Chidi checks the official website, uscis.gov, for the version that applies to his own application.

Chidi’s interview shows what stays the same about the test: it is spoken, the questions come from a published list, and his English is assessed alongside it.

Congress wrote the law that says an applicant must show knowledge of civics and English. But the immigration service, an *agency*, runs the interview and sets the details: the question list, how many questions are asked and the pass mark. That is **Carrying out the law**, and it is why the details can change without Congress passing anything.

This unit skips the details, because they change. Get the official list from the immigration service’s own website, uscis.gov, for the version that applies to you. Answers that depend on the date or on where you live, such as who the current President is or who your state’s governor is, are left out on purpose. Look them up fresh.

**The five freedoms of the First Amendment**

- **Which right stops the government from punishing you for what you say?** The right to free speech

- **Which right means the court appoints a lawyer for you if you are charged with a crime and cannot pay for one?** The right to a lawyer
- **Which right means a jury of ordinary people decides, in public and without long delay, when you are charged with a serious crime?** The right to a speedy public jury trial

**To carry away**

- A right holds the government back from you, and a duty is the law asking something of you. Most rights in the Bill of Rights protect everyone here.
- Only a few things are kept for citizens: voting in federal elections, running for federal office, and serving on a federal jury.
- Obeying the law, paying tax on income earned here and, for a man aged 18 to 25, registering for Selective Service fall on everyone here.

- The oath is made once, at the ceremony that makes you a citizen. The pledge to the flag is not part of it.
- A law requires the test, and the immigration service decides how it works. Get the official question list from uscis.gov.
