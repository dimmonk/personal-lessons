## Part 1 of 3: Someone you know only online, money waiting for you, money you lost

### 1. Before you pay, find out which of nine requests this is

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 1 of 32*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

Before you hand money to anyone, you can tell which of nine things the request is: eight scams, or the one real request that they copy. Then you know what to do on the spot, before any money leaves your account.

A text says a package is waiting and you owe $2.99. Your builder emails a bill with a new bank account. A man you only know online needs money for his daughter in the hospital. A caller says you owe tax. A buyer for your old bike pays you too much. Every one asks for money, and every one could be real or fake.

Money sent by wire transfer, in cash, in gift cards or in crypto is very hard to get back, so the time to be careful is before you send it. Two questions do the work: what does the request say the money is for, and what does it ask you to do with it?

One of the nine is not a scam. A real bill, fine or deal has a name too, because someone who suspects every request soon stops checking any of them.

Looking real does not make a request real, and looking odd does not make it fake. The test that works on every one is *the check*: contact the person or company yourself, through *a way you already had*.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“What is it asking you to do right now?”** Its answers:

- **“Install something, open a file, or share your screen”**: give this answer when it asks you to install something, open or run a file, or let someone see or control your screen, or it warns of a device problem and gives you someone to call.
- **“Sign in, give a code, or allow an app”**: give this answer when it asks you to sign in, to give a one-time code by typing it, reading it out or passing it on, or to press Allow so that an app can use one of your accounts.
- **“Pay or send money”**: give this answer when it asks you to pay or send money, by any means, now or by a date. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“Tell them about yourself”**: give this answer when it asks for facts about you: a document, an ID or card number, your date of birth or address, or your work, home and family.
- **“Nothing: it only tells you something”**: give this answer when it tells you something has happened or will happen and asks you to do nothing, and anything it suggests uses only what you already had, like the number on your card.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. Romance scam

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 2 of 32*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-romance`]

First, the slowest scam of all: money asked for by someone you have never met.

*The engineer abroad*

> In February Ana accepted a message on a dating site from a man called Daniel, who said he was an engineer working abroad. They wrote to each other every day, and by the summer she thought of him as her partner. Whenever she suggested a video call, the connection failed or he had to go. In October he wrote: '⟦My daughter has been in a car accident, and the hospital will not treat her until $4,200 is paid⟧. Can you send it to the hospital's account today? I will pay you back when I land.'

Ana has never met Daniel. For eight months he wrote to her every day and asked for nothing. Then came an emergency, far away and urgent, that only she could fix. The warm months are the method, and they take time on purpose.

Notice what never happens: every time Ana asks for a video call, the connection fails or he has to go.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check how you know them: Daniel is a name on a dating site, and Ana has never seen him on a live call.** Someone you only know through messages could be anyone.
2. **Find what the money is for: his daughter’s hospital bill.** In this scam the emergency is always theirs, never yours.
3. **Notice the hurry: “send it today”.** A hurry stops you from thinking and from asking anyone.

The name for this is **Romance scam**. The relationship is the tool: the money is asked for in the name of a partner who feels real.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Send nothing today, however urgent it sounds.** A scammer needs you to act before you think.
2. **Ask for a live video call that they start now.** A real partner can do it in a minute, and a scammer always has a new excuse.
3. **Tell someone who knows you in person.** If you were told to keep it secret, that is your answer too.

You may also hear this called “dating scam” or “sweetheart scam”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Romance scam**.

### 4. Pig-butchering scam

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 4 of 32*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-pigbutcher`]

The next scam starts the same way, with someone you only know online, but the money goes into an app.

*The wrong number*

> In March a stranger texted Lena: 'Sorry, wrong number! But you sound nice.' She replied politely, and they began to chat every day. The man, who gave his name as Kai, said he was a surveyor who traded currencies in his spare time. After six weeks he showed her how much he had made on a trading app and told her to download it. She put in $200 as a test, watched it grow to $260 in a week, and took out $100 without any trouble. Then Kai wrote: 'The platform has an offer for larger accounts this week. ⟦Put in $6,000 and your profits will triple⟧.'

Kai began as a wrong-number text, and he and Lena have chatted every day for weeks. He showed her a trading app. She put in $200, watched it grow and took out $100. That payout was real, and it was on purpose: a small amount that comes out makes the big deposit feel safe.

The app is not a real market. The profits are just numbers the scammer puts on the page. When Lena tries to take her profit out, it will ask for a fee or a tax first, and that is still this scam.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check how you know them: Kai is a stranger who texted a wrong number.** Someone you only know online is where both of these scams begin.
2. **Find where the money is to go: “Put in $6,000” into the app Kai showed her.** Here the money goes into a site, not to the person.
3. **Look at the proof: she took out $100 and it worked.** A small payout is bait, so the big deposit feels safe.

The name for this is **Pig-butchering scam**. You are fed with attention and small wins, like an animal before the end, and the word is the one the news uses.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Put nothing into any site or app that someone you only know online showed you.** It can look as if it works, because it is built to.
2. **Look the firm up yourself in a regulator’s register, such as FINRA BrokerCheck (brokercheck.finra.org).** A firm that is not listed is a reason to stop.
3. **Never pay a fee or a tax to take money out.** That fee is the scammer’s next move.
4. **If you have already paid, call your bank at the number on your card.** Some transfers can be recalled in the first hours.

You may also hear this called “investment scam” or “crypto romance scam” or “fake trading platform”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Pig-butchering scam**.

### 6. Pig-butchering scam or Romance scam: telling them apart

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 6 of 32*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-pigbutcher-romance`]

Both ask someone you have never met for a large sum, and here the same man asks in two ways.

**Story A**

> Mara has chatted with a man called Theo for four months on a photo-sharing app, and they have never met. He writes: 'I have been using a trading app that has doubled my money. Put $3,000 of your savings into it today, and you will see the same.'

**Story B**

> Mara has chatted with a man called Theo for four months on a photo-sharing app, and they have never met. He writes: 'My sister needs an operation today, and the hospital wants $3,000 before it will start. Please pay it into this account. I will repay you as soon as I am back.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about Mara and Theo, and in both he asks for $3,000. Compare one thing: where the money is to go.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Put it into a trading site or app that they showed you”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story A.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the $3,000 goes into a trading app that Theo showed her, and he mentions no trouble of his own. That is **“Put it into a trading site or app that they showed you”**, so the name is **Pig-butchering scam**.

In Story B the $3,000 pays for his sister’s operation. That is trouble he says is his, so the name is **Romance scam**.

Where the money goes decides it, not how warm Theo sounds.

**How to tell them apart**

Is the money to help them out of trouble, or to go into a trading app or site they recommended?


### 7. Advance-fee scam

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 7 of 32*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-advancefee`]

A different reason to pay: money that is said to be waiting for you.

*The drawing nobody entered*

> Marta, who has never entered a competition in her life, opens an email from the 'Continental Prize Office': '⟦Your email address was drawn in our monthly drawing and you have won $250,000⟧. To release your prize, you must first pay a $340 insurance and handling fee by wire transfer to the account below. The prize will be paid out within 24 hours of receipt.'

Marta never entered a competition, yet the email says she won $250,000. The prize is made up. The only real money in the story is the $340 she is told to pay first, and it goes to the scammer.

It does not have to be a prize: a grant, a loan, an inheritance or a refund can be the bait. The fee may be called insurance, handling, tax or a release code. If you pay once, you are usually told something else has come up, and asked for a second fee.

**How to spot it**

1. **Ask whether you entered or applied: Marta never entered a drawing.** Nobody gives away money to people who never asked for it.
2. **Find the money said to be waiting: $250,000.** Big, easy money is the bait.
3. **Find the fee you must pay first: $340 by wire transfer.** Paying before you receive is the whole scam.

The name for this is **Advance-fee scam**. “Advance” means ahead of time: you pay first, and the money never comes.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Pay nothing.** A real prize, grant or inheritance never needs a fee paid to a stranger first.
2. **To find out whether you are really owed something, contact the organization yourself, through *a way you already had*.** A number or link in the message leads to the scammer.
3. **Do not reply.** A reply tells the sender a real person reads this address.

You may also hear this called “advance fee fraud” or “419 scam” or “lottery scam” or “inheritance scam”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Advance-fee scam**.

### 9. Recovery scam

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 9 of 32*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-recovery`]

The last scam offered money you were never owed. This one offers money you had, and lost.

*The recovery email*

> Malik lost $3,000 to a fake trading website in March, and he told nobody. In November an email arrives from a 'fund recovery service': '⟦We have traced the money you lost and can have it returned to your account⟧. Our fee is $450, payable in advance by wire transfer, and refundable once your funds are released.'

Malik lost $3,000 to a fake trading website and told nobody. Now an email says it has traced his money and will get it back for a $450 fee. Someone who has been robbed wants the money back more than almost anything, and the sender knows it. Often the people who took the first payment keep a list of everyone who paid, or sell it.

Real help exists: your bank, the police, a licensed attorney. They do not contact you first, they do not promise a result, and they do not ask you to pay by transfer to a personal account before they start.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check that you lost the money: Malik lost $3,000 in March.** The offer only works on people who were robbed.
2. **Notice who made contact: the email came to him.** Real help does not chase you.
3. **Find the fee that comes first: $450 by wire transfer.** A fee up front, for a result nobody can promise, is the whole scam.

The name for this is **Recovery scam**. “Recovery” means getting something back; here the fee is real, and the recovery is not.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Pay nothing, and do not reply.** Anyone who contacts you first about your loss is a warning in itself.
2. **Report the loss to your bank at the number on your card, and to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.** They are the real places to get help, and they do not charge you.
3. **If you looked for help through a search, do not trust the top result.** It may be a paid ad, and a scammer can buy that place.

You may also hear this called “recovery room scam” or “fund recovery scam”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Recovery scam**.

> Imran gets an email: 'You are owed $6,000 in compensation, held for you by our claims office. It will be released once you pay a $150 release fee.' He has never made a claim of any kind.

> Imran lost $6,000 last year to a fake car-insurance broker. Now an email arrives: 'We have recovered the $6,000 you lost. It will be released once you pay a $150 release fee.'

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

Ask where the money came from: out of nowhere, or your own money that was taken.

**How to tell them apart**

Is the money that is said to be waiting something that was never mine, or something I lost earlier?

## Part 2 of 3: A bill, a fine or a deal: the real request and four copies

### 12. Real payment request

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 12 of 32*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-realpayment`]

Next, the real thing. A real bill, fine or deal gives the same reasons as the scams that copy it, so you need to know the real one first.

*The agency rent reminder*

> Hana signed a one-year lease at a rental agency's office that she walked into herself. The agreement says rent of $950 is due on the 1st, paid into the agency's client account, and prints the account name, routing number and account number on page two. On the 25th the agency's own app, which she installed when she signed, shows: 'Please pay $950 by the 1st, to the account on page two of your agreement. Questions? ⟦Call the office at the number on your agreement⟧.' It is ⟦the same amount, to the same account, as every month⟧, and nobody mentions any deadline but the 1st.

Hana walked into the agency and signed her lease herself. Now the agency’s app asks for the rent she agreed, into the account printed in her agreement, and says to call the number on her agreement if she has questions. Nobody hurries her, and nothing is hidden.

A request is not suspicious just because it asks for money. What makes this one real is that Hana can check it herself, by calling a number she already had, and the agency will confirm it. That step is *the check*: contacting them through *a way you already had*. A copy can look exactly like this message, but it cannot answer a call that Hana makes to the number in her own agreement.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check who started it: Hana walked in and signed the lease herself.** A real request grows out of something you began.
2. **Check the amount and the account: $950, into the account on page two of her agreement.** Both match what she was given at the start.
3. **Check the pressure: there is no hurry and nothing secret.** A real request gives you time, and lets you tell anyone.
4. **Do *the check*: call the number printed on her agreement.** A real request holds up when you ask them yourself.

The name for this is **Real payment request**. Nothing is wrong with it, and you pay it.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Pay it the normal way, and keep the confirmation.** You will want proof if anything goes wrong later.
2. **For a large payment, or a first payment to someone new, do *the check* once.** A real request passes it, so it costs you a few minutes.
3. **If the details change, someone hurries you, or you are told not to check, stop.** It has stopped being the real thing.

### 14. Invoice fraud

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 14 of 32*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-invoicefraud`]

The real request has a copy that looks identical: the same bill from the same person, with one thing changed.

*The landscaper's new bank*

> ⟦Every month for a year Joe has paid the invoice that his landscaper, Maeve, emails him⟧ on the last Friday, always into the same account. This month's invoice, for $1,850, arrives in the same email thread as before, with the same logo and signature. At the bottom it says: 'Please note that we have changed bank. From now on please pay all invoices, starting with this one, into the new account below.'

Joe has paid his landscaper’s invoice every month for a year. This month’s arrives in the same email thread, with the same logo and signature, and one new line: the landscaper has changed bank. The work is real and so is the amount. Only the account is new.

Someone got into a mailbox, or registered an address one letter off, and waited for a large invoice. It works because it is calm: nothing is hurried and nobody is threatened. The only way Joe can find out is to contact the landscaper himself.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the bill you already pay: Joe’s landscaper, every month for a year.** The scam rides on a bill you trust.
2. **Find the message about new details: “we have changed bank”.** This one line is the whole scam.
3. **Check where the new details came from: a message, not his contract or an earlier invoice.** Anyone can send a message with new details.

The name for this is **Invoice fraud**. An invoice is a bill that a business sends. The bill is real, and the fraud is in the bank details on it.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Before you pay into new details, call the sender at a number you already had, such as the one on the contract or an earlier paper invoice.** A number in the message that announced the change leads to the scammer.
2. **If you cannot reach them, wait.** A real bill can wait a day, and a thief cannot.

You may also hear this called “changed bank account details scam” or “payment diversion fraud” or “vendor email fraud” or “business email compromise”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Invoice fraud**.

### 17. Fake payment link

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 17 of 32*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-fakelink`]

The next copy is a small charge you do not owe, and it is one of the most common scams of all.

*The redelivery fee*

> A text arrives on Jonas's phone from a number he does not know: 'ParcelPoint: your package could not be delivered. ⟦Pay a $2.99 redelivery fee at parcelpoint-redeliver.example⟧ to arrange a new time, or it will be sent back.' Jonas is expecting a package.

Jonas is expecting a package, so a text saying it could not be delivered is believable. It asks for $2.99 on a page behind a link. The page looks like the courier’s, and it asks for his card number, the expiry date and the three digits on the back.

$2.99 is less than a coffee, which is how the scam slips past your attention. The charge is not the point. The card number is, and it is used right away for much larger payments. To find out whether there is a package, Jonas can look in the courier’s own app.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check who sent it: a number Jonas does not know.** Anyone can send a text that looks like a courier’s.
2. **Find the payment page: a link in the text.** The page at the end of the link is not the company’s.
3. **Notice the small amount: $2.99.** A small charge is the bait, and your card number is the prize.

The name for this is **Fake payment link**. The charge is the bait and the link is the trap.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Do not tap the link, and do not reply. Delete the message.** The page at the end of it is built to take your card.
2. **If the charge might be real, open the company’s own app, or type in its address yourself.** A real charge will be there.
3. **If you already entered your card details, call your bank now, at the number on the back of your card, and ask them to block it.** Blocking the card stops the larger payments that follow.
4. **If a caller then says he is from your bank and tells you to move your money to a safe account, hang up.** That is the next scam.

You may also hear this called “package delivery scam” or “fake fine text” or “smishing”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Fake payment link**.

### 20. Fake official scam

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 20 of 32*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-fakeofficial`]

So far nothing has frightened you. The next scam does: a threat from someone who says they have power over you.

*The tax caller*

> Dee's landline rings. ⟦A recorded voice says the IRS has issued a warrant for her arrest⟧. She presses 1, and a man who gives his name as Officer Grant says she owes $2,300 in unpaid tax and that officers will be at her door within two hours unless it is settled today. He tells her to buy gift cards at the supermarket and read him the numbers, and not to tell the store staff why, because 'they may be involved'.

Dee gets a call from a man who says he is an officer. She owes $2,300 in tax, he says, and officers will be at her door in two hours unless she pays today in gift cards. A frightened person does not stop to check, and the caller knows it.

Gift cards, crypto, cash and a transfer to an account the caller gives cannot be undone: a gift card number read down a phone is spent within minutes. No real IRS agent or police officer takes gift cards. Dee is also told not to tell the store staff, who are exactly the people who would say stop.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the claim to power: “an officer” and “a warrant for her arrest”.** The caller borrows an official’s authority to frighten her.
2. **Find the deadline and the payment: today, in gift cards.** A hurry and a payment that cannot be undone are what make it work.
3. **Find the order to keep quiet: do not tell the store staff.** The people who would stop her are the ones she is told to avoid.

The name for this is **Fake official scam**. The “official” is the role the caller claims: the IRS, the police, a court or your bank. The claim is the lie.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Hang up.** You do not owe the caller politeness, and a real official will not mind.
2. **Then do *the check*: call the IRS or your bank at a number you already had, such as the one on your card or a bill.** Some callers keep the line open, so use a different phone if you can.
3. **Never pay a debt by gift card, crypto or cash, and never move your money to a “safe account”.** No real official asks for either.
4. **Tell someone, even if you were told not to.** The order to keep quiet is part of the scam.

You may also hear this called “impersonation scam” or “safe account scam” or “tax scam” or “police impersonation scam”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Fake official scam**.

### 23. Overpayment scam

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 23 of 32*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-overpayment`]

The last copy is the only one in which the money comes to you first.

*The bike with a typo*

> ⟦Rafa lists his used bike online for $400⟧. Within the hour a buyer called Nick writes: 'I'll take it, no haggling.' A payment of $800 appears in Rafa's account. Nick writes again: 'Sorry, I typed the wrong amount. Please send the $400 difference to my courier's account, and he will collect the bike tomorrow.'

Rafa sells his bike for $400, and $800 lands in his account. The buyer says it was a typo and asks him to send the $400 difference to a courier’s account. It feels like honesty: he is only giving back what is not his.

But the $800 may come from a stolen card, a transfer the bank takes back, or a check that bounces. Rafa’s $400 goes out for real, and when the buyer’s payment disappears he has lost the $400, and the bike too if he has handed it over. A buyer who really overpaid can ask their own bank to reverse the payment, then pay again.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check that you are the seller: Rafa listed his bike for $400.** This scam only works on someone who is selling.
2. **Find the payment that is more than the price: $800 for a $400 bike.** The extra is what the scammer wants back.
3. **Find where the difference is to go: a courier’s account.** Money that is truly sent back goes back to where it came from.

The name for this is **Overpayment scam**. The buyer pays too much, and the scam is what you are asked to do with the extra.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Do not send the difference, and do not hand over the item.** Both are gone for good if the payment turns out to be fake.
2. **If the payment really was a mistake, tell the buyer to ask their own bank to reverse it.** That is the fair way, and a real buyer will do it.
3. **Ask your bank when the payment has cleared for good, and wait for that.** Money showing in your account is not yet yours, and it can take days.

You may also hear this called “fake buyer scam”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Overpayment scam**.

## Part 3 of 3: The two questions about money, one whole story, then the drill

### 26. The first question about money: what is it for?

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 26 of 32*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-m1`]

You have met all nine. Here is the first question, with its six answers, in one place.

**The question:** **“What does it say the money is for?”**

**Its answers**

- **“An investment or an emergency of someone you know only online”**
  - Give this answer when the request comes from someone you know only by messages and calls, often for weeks, and the money is for an investment they showed you or their own trouble.
  - Keeps **Pig-butchering scam** and **Romance scam**. Rules out **Advance-fee scam**, **Recovery scam**, **Real payment request**, **Invoice fraud**, **Fake payment link**, **Fake official scam** and **Overpayment scam**.
- **“A prize, an inheritance, a grant or a loan waiting for you”**
  - Give this answer when the request says that money is waiting for you (a prize, an inheritance, a grant, a loan, a refund or a payout) and the payment is part of getting it.
  - Keeps **Advance-fee scam**. Rules out **Romance scam**, **Pig-butchering scam**, **Recovery scam**, **Real payment request**, **Invoice fraud**, **Fake payment link**, **Fake official scam** and **Overpayment scam**.
- **“Getting back money you lost”**
  - Give this answer when the request is about money you lost earlier, often to a scam, and someone says they can get it back for you.
  - Keeps **Recovery scam**. Rules out **Romance scam**, **Pig-butchering scam**, **Advance-fee scam**, **Real payment request**, **Invoice fraud**, **Fake payment link**, **Fake official scam** and **Overpayment scam**.
- **“A bill from someone you already pay”**
  - Give this answer when the request is for a bill, rent or another payment you already make to someone you deal with, such as a landlord or a phone company.
  - Keeps **Invoice fraud**, **Fake payment link** and **Real payment request**. Rules out **Romance scam**, **Pig-butchering scam**, **Advance-fee scam**, **Recovery scam**, **Fake official scam** and **Overpayment scam**.
- **“A fine, a tax, or keeping your money safe”**
  - Give this answer when the request says it comes from an official, like the IRS or the police, or from your bank, and is about a fine, a tax, a debt, or a danger to your money.
  - Keeps **Fake official scam**, **Fake payment link** and **Real payment request**. Rules out **Romance scam**, **Pig-butchering scam**, **Advance-fee scam**, **Recovery scam**, **Invoice fraud** and **Overpayment scam**.
- **“Something you are buying, selling or booking”**
  - Give this answer when the request is part of a deal you are in: something you are buying, selling, renting, booking or waiting to have delivered.
  - Keeps **Overpayment scam**, **Fake payment link** and **Real payment request**. Rules out **Romance scam**, **Pig-butchering scam**, **Advance-fee scam**, **Recovery scam**, **Invoice fraud** and **Fake official scam**.

**Why it matters**

Each money scam comes with its own reason, and a real request comes with an ordinary one: a bill, a fine or a tax, or a deal you are in. The reason often leaves only one name; when it leaves a few, the next question settles it.

This question asks what the request says the money is for, not whether that is true. Read the reason it gives.

Some reasons are shared: a real request and several scams give the same ordinary reason, such as a bill, a fine or a deal. That is why there is a second question.

**How to answer it**

1. **Find the sentence that gives the reason for paying.** It is often near the start, before the amount.
2. **Pick the answer that fits it, and put your finger on the words.** If you cannot find the words, you do not have an answer yet.
3. **See how many names are left.** The first three answers leave one or two names, and the last three leave several, so the second question finishes the job.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Sometimes a request seems to give two reasons at once. Use this order. Money you lost comes before money waiting for you: a refund held for you after a scam is **“Getting back money you lost”**. Money waiting for you comes before an official: a tax refund that needs a fee is **“A prize, an inheritance, a grant or a loan waiting for you”**, so it is **Advance-fee scam**, not **Fake official scam**. Someone you know only online comes before a deal.

A sale is not a threat. A hurried buyer who wants a payment sent back, in secret, is still part of a deal, so the answer is **“Something you are buying, selling or booking”** and the name is **Overpayment scam**. A caller who threatens you with an official’s power is **Fake official scam**.

The very first question comes before both of these. If you are asked to install something, let someone watch your screen, or read out a code, it is not about money, whatever else is said. A “refund” that starts with installing a support tool is **Refund scam**, not **Overpayment scam**. A bank caller who wants a code read out is **One-time code scam**, not **Fake official scam**.

- Advance-fee scam or Recovery scam: Is the money that is said to be waiting something that was never mine, or something I lost earlier?
- Advance-fee scam or Fake official scam: Does the message say that money is owed to me and I must pay first, or that I owe money and will be punished unless I pay?
- Fake official scam or Overpayment scam: Is the money part of a deal that I am in, with a payment that has reached me, or does someone with an official’s power threaten me unless I pay?

  - Keeps **Invoice fraud**. Rules out **Romance scam**, **Pig-butchering scam**, **Advance-fee scam**, **Recovery scam**, **Real payment request**, **Fake payment link**, **Fake official scam** and **Overpayment scam**.
- **“Pay on a page reached from a link in the message”**
  - Give this answer when you are asked to pay, usually a small amount, by typing your card details into a payment page that you reach through a link in the message.
  - Keeps **Fake payment link**. Rules out **Romance scam**, **Pig-butchering scam**, **Advance-fee scam**, **Recovery scam**, **Real payment request**, **Invoice fraud**, **Fake official scam** and **Overpayment scam**.
- **“Pay at once, in a way that cannot be undone, and tell no one”**
  - Give this answer when you are told to pay at once, by gift cards, crypto or a transfer to an account they give you, often with a threat, and told not to tell your bank or family.
  - Keeps **Fake official scam**. Rules out **Romance scam**, **Pig-butchering scam**, **Advance-fee scam**, **Recovery scam**, **Real payment request**, **Invoice fraud**, **Fake payment link** and **Overpayment scam**.
- **“Send back money they say they paid you by mistake”**
  - Give this answer when someone has paid you, or seems to have paid you, more than they owe, and asks you to send the difference back or on to someone else.
  - Keeps **Overpayment scam**. Rules out **Romance scam**, **Pig-butchering scam**, **Advance-fee scam**, **Recovery scam**, **Real payment request**, **Invoice fraud**, **Fake payment link** and **Fake official scam**.
- **“Pay what you agreed or owe, to details that check out”**
  - Give this answer when the amount and details are what you agreed or always pay, nobody rushes you or asks for secrecy, and it holds up when you check through a way you already had.
  - Keeps **Real payment request**. Rules out **Romance scam**, **Pig-butchering scam**, **Advance-fee scam**, **Recovery scam**, **Invoice fraud**, **Fake payment link**, **Fake official scam** and **Overpayment scam**.

**Why it matters**

Each scam wants the money paid in its own way, and a real request asks for it in an ordinary way that holds up when you check it. You can answer before any money leaves your account.

When the first question leaves several names, they share the same reason, so the reason cannot separate them. What the request asks you to do with the money can. A bill from someone you pay looks the same whether it is real or a copy. What differs is where you are told to send the money.

That is why this question tells the real request from its copies. **Invoice fraud**, **Fake payment link** and **Overpayment scam** can all start with the same bill or deal, and they end in three different requests: pay into new details, pay on a link, and send some of it back.

**How to answer it**

1. **Find the sentence that says what you are to do with the money.** Everything else is the story around it.
2. **Pick the answer that fits those words.** Seven of the eight can be given from the message alone, before you do anything.
3. **For the real request, contact them yourself, through *a way you already had*.** The message alone cannot show that it is real, and this is *the check*.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Hurry and secrecy turn up in most of these scams, so a request often shows a hurry together with something more specific: a fee, a link, a trading app, an emergency, a payment to send back. The more specific one wins. **“Pay at once, in a way that cannot be undone, and tell no one”** is the answer only when nothing more specific shows, which is why it belongs to **Fake official scam** and to nothing else. A hurry with a link is **Fake payment link**.

A fee on a link goes to the fee, so it is **Advance-fee scam**. A fee to take money out of a trading app goes to the app, so it is **Pig-butchering scam**.

- Pig-butchering scam or Romance scam: Is the money to help them out of trouble, or to go into a trading app or site they recommended?
- Invoice fraud or Real payment request: Are the bank details the ones I was given at the start, or did a message just tell me that they changed?
- Fake payment link or Real payment request: Did the charge reach me in a message with a link to pay on, or can I find it myself in the company’s own app or on a page that I already use?
- Fake official scam or Real payment request: Am I being hurried and kept quiet, or given time and something that I can look up myself?
- Overpayment scam or Real payment request: Is the amount the one we agreed, or am I asked to send some of a payment back or on to someone else?
- Invoice fraud or Fake payment link: Am I asked to pay into a new account that the message names, or on a page that I reach through a link in it?
- Fake official scam or Fake payment link: Am I told to pay on a page behind a link, or to pay at once in a way that cannot be undone, with nothing more specific?
- Overpayment scam or Fake payment link: Has money reached me first, so that I am asked to send some of it back, or am I asked to pay on a page behind a link?

### 31. What to carry away

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 31 of 32*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap`]

You have now run the questions on your own. Here is the unit in one place.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What does it say the money is for?
- An investment or an emergency of someone you know only online → Pig-butchering scam · Romance scam
- A prize, an inheritance, a grant or a loan waiting for you → Advance-fee scam
- Getting back money you lost → Recovery scam
- A bill from someone you already pay → Invoice fraud · Fake payment link · Real payment request
- A fine, a tax, or keeping your money safe → Fake official scam · Fake payment link · Real payment request
- Something you are buying, selling or booking → Overpayment scam · Fake payment link · Real payment request

What does it ask you to do with the money?
- Put it into a trading site or app that they showed you → Pig-butchering scam
- Pay for an emergency of someone you have never met → Romance scam
- Pay a fee before the money reaches you → Advance-fee scam · Recovery scam
- Pay into new bank account details sent by message → Invoice fraud
- Pay on a page reached from a link in the message → Fake payment link
- Pay at once, in a way that cannot be undone, and tell no one → Fake official scam
- Send back money they say they paid you by mistake → Overpayment scam
- Pay what you agreed or owe, to details that check out → Real payment request

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Romance scam**: someone you know only online and have never met in person, and an emergency of theirs that you are asked to pay for.
- **Pig-butchering scam**: someone you know only online, a trading site or app they showed you, and a request to put money in, or to pay it before you can take money out.
- **Advance-fee scam**: money you are told is waiting for you (a prize, an inheritance, a grant, a loan, a payout), and a fee you must pay before any of it reaches you.
- **Recovery scam**: money you lost earlier, someone who says they can get it back, and a fee you must pay before they do.
- **Real payment request**: a bill or a deal you already had or started, the amount and details you agreed or always pay, and a request that holds up when you check through a way you already had.
- **Invoice fraud**: a bill or a payment you already make to someone, a message saying that their bank account details have changed, and new details to pay into.
- **Fake payment link**: a bill, a fine or a charge on something you are buying, a link in the message, and a payment page at the end of the link that asks for your card details.
- **Fake official scam**: someone saying they are an official or from your bank, a fine, a debt or a danger to your money, and an order to pay at once, by gift cards or to an account they name.
- **Overpayment scam**: something you are selling, a payment from the buyer that is more than the price, and a request to send the difference back or on to someone else.

**To carry away**

- Before you use a name, find the words that show what the request says the money is for, and then the words that show what it asks you to do with it.
- A real request and its copy can use the same story, the same bill and the same words. What separates them is what the request asks you to do with the money, and whether it holds up when you contact them yourself, through *a way you already had*. That is *the check*, and it works on every name here.
- Hurry, secrecy and a payment that cannot be undone turn up in most of these scams. They are a reason to stop, but they do not decide the name. The more specific answer does.
- When a request is real, pay it the normal way. Treating every bill as a scam is a mistake too, which is why **Real payment request** has a name.
- On the spot, nearly every time: do not pay yet, and contact them yourself. If you have already paid, call your bank at once, at the number on your card, because a transfer can sometimes be recalled in the first hours.
