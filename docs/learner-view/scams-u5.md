## Part 1 of 2: The same questions, from a real company and from a scammer

### 1. Before you hand over facts about yourself, check who started it

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

Before you give anyone your passport, your date of birth or your address, or keep chatting with a stranger who is curious about your life, check which of three things it is: **Real request for details**, **Identity theft** or **Friendly chat from a stranger**. Each one has its own sign, and you can spot it before you answer.

You are asked for facts about yourself all the time, and almost always it is fine. Your doctor asks for your date of birth. The county asks for your address. You do not think twice: you started each of those, you know who you are dealing with, and they ask for what you came for.

The same questions can come from the other side. A caller you did not expect asks you to confirm your date of birth. An email offers you a grant and wants a photo of your passport. The words can be exactly the same as at the doctor’s. What changed is who started it, and whether what they ask for is what the job needs.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“What is it asking you to do right now?”** Its answers:

- **“Install something, open a file, or share your screen”**: give this answer when it asks you to install something, open or run a file, or let someone see or control your screen, or it warns of a device problem and gives you someone to call.
- **“Sign in, give a code, or allow an app”**: give this answer when it asks you to sign in, to give a one-time code by typing it, reading it out or passing it on, or to press Allow so that an app can use one of your accounts.
- **“Pay or send money”**: give this answer when it asks you to pay or send money, by any means, now or by a date.
- **“Tell them about yourself”**: give this answer when it asks for facts about you: a document, an ID or card number, your date of birth or address, or your work, home and family. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“Nothing: it only tells you something”**: give this answer when it tells you something has happened or will happen and asks you to do nothing, and anything it suggests uses only what you already had, like the number on your card.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

*(One tap on any of these lines opens the card in Unit One that taught it.)*

What do they want to know about you?
- Your ID details: papers, ID or card numbers, date of birth, address → Identity theft · Real request for details
- Your life: your work, home, family, money or plans → Friendly chat from a stranger

- Yes: you started it through a way you already had, and it asks for nothing more → Real request for details

### 2. Real request for details

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-realdetails`]

First, the ordinary one: a real company asking for facts about you.

*The savings account*

> Chen wants to open a savings account. He ⟦types the Oakfield Credit Union's web address into his browser himself⟧, and the page says: 'Open an account'. The form asks for his full name, his date of birth and his home address. A second page says: '⟦We are required to check who our customers are⟧. Please upload a photo of your passport or driver’s license.' He uploads one.

Chen started this himself, by typing the credit union’s address into his browser. The form asks for a lot: his date of birth, his address and a photo of his passport. But the credit union says why, and the reason fits: a bank has to know who owns an account.

A long list does not make a request suspicious. What matters is that you started it, and that the list matches the job.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check that you started it: Chen decided to open an account.** Nobody had to talk him into it.
2. **Check how you got there: he typed the credit union’s web address himself.** That is *a way you already had*, not a link or a number someone sent you.
3. **Check the list matches the job: a new account needs his name, date of birth, address and a photo of his passport.** A request that goes further than the job is the warning sign.
4. **Check they say why: “We are required to check who our customers are.”** A real company explains its request, and does not mind being asked.

This is **Real request for details**. Of the three names in this unit, it is the only real one.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Give what the job needs and nothing more.** The extra is the only part that can be misused.
2. **If a form asks for something the job does not seem to need, ask why before you fill it in.** A real company can answer in one sentence.
3. **Give the facts on the other side’s own site, at their own desk, or on a number you already had.** Then you know who is getting them.

### 4. Identity theft

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-identitytheft`]

Second, the copy: the same facts, asked for in a way that does not fit.

*The energy grant*

> ⟦An email reaches Kayode⟧ from the 'Energy Support Office': 'You are eligible for a $400 energy grant. To receive it, reply with a photo of your passport, your date of birth and your home address by Friday.' ⟦Kayode has never applied for any grant⟧.

The facts are the same as at Chen’s credit union. The difference is that Kayode started nothing: an email arrived out of nowhere, offered him something nice and asked for his passport.

A passport photo, a date of birth and an address are all someone needs to pose as you: to open a bank account, take out a loan or sign a phone plan in your name. The grant is only the bait. And you will not notice: months later, a letter arrives about a loan you never took out.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check who started it: Kayode never applied for any grant, and the email came to him.** If you started nothing, the facts cannot be for anything you began.
2. **Look at what it asks for: a photo of his passport, his date of birth and his address.** These are what someone needs to pose as you.
3. **Look at the reason it gives: a $400 grant.** A nice offer is how they get you to hand the facts over.
4. **Look for hurry: “by Friday”.** A deadline stops you from checking.

This is **Identity theft**. The facts are the ones **Real request for details** asks for, so what tells them apart is who started it, not the facts.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Do not send, read out or type the papers or the numbers.** Once they are out, you cannot take them back.
2. **Say “I will contact you through your official number”, then end the call or leave the message unanswered.** A real company will not mind.
3. **Run *the check*: look the organization up yourself, through *a way you already had*, and ask whether it sent this.** Only the real company can tell you.
4. **If you have already sent something, tell your bank at once, on the number on your card.** The sooner they know, the less anyone can do in your name.

You may also hear this called “identity fraud” or “ID theft”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Identity theft**.

### 6. Identity theft or Real request for details: telling them apart

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 6 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-identitytheft-realdetails`]

These two ask for the very same papers, which is why people mix them up.

**Story A**

> Dina applied for a warehouse job on the Brackley Logistics website, and was offered it after an interview. She signs in to the applicant account she made on that site, where a page says: 'Offer accepted. So that we can confirm you are eligible to work before you start on November 3, please upload your passport and your Social Security number.'

**Story B**

> Dina has never applied to Brackley Logistics. An email reaches her: 'You have been selected for a warehouse job. Before any contract is sent, please send a photo of your passport, a photo of you holding it, your Social Security number and your bank account details. This is our standard check.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about Dina and a warehouse job at Brackley Logistics, and in both she is asked for her passport and Social Security number. Compare one thing: did she start it, and does the company need what it asks for yet?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“No: it came to you, or it asks for more than you set out to do”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A, Dina applied on the company’s own website and was offered the job. Now a page there asks for her passport and Social Security number, to confirm she may work. She started it, and the papers are what this stage needs. That is **Real request for details**.

In Story B, Dina never applied. An email arrives, and before any contract it asks for her passport, a photo of her holding it, her Social Security number and her bank account details. She started nothing, and a company with no contract to send does not need any of it. That is **Identity theft**.

Same company, same person, same papers. What differs is who started it, and whether the company needs the papers yet.

**How to tell them apart**

Did I start this, through a way I already had? And does what they ask for match what the job needs, or does the list go further?


### 7. A call that sounds real, and still is not

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `exception`, id `exc-bankcall`]

Most copies have something that sounds wrong. This one has nothing.

*The call from the bank*

> Gabriela's phone rings. A man says that he is from Halbrook Bank's fraud team, and he uses her name. In a calm voice he says: 'We have seen a strange payment on your account. First I must be sure that I am speaking to you. Please confirm your date of birth and your home address.'

The caller knows Gabriela’s name, speaks calmly and asks only for what a real bank asks for: a date of birth and an address. It sounds like **Real request for details**, but it is **Identity theft**.

**You are asked:** This looks like **Real request for details**. Before you read why it is **Identity theft**, tap the words in the story that settle it.

The pieces you can tap:
1. “Gabriela's phone rings”
2. “A man says that he is from Halbrook Bank's fraud team, and he uses her name”
3. “We have seen a strange payment on your account. First I must be sure that I am speaking to you”
4. “Please confirm your date of birth and your home address”

**Shown as soon as you tap.** The words are “Gabriela's phone rings”.
- If you tapped “A man says that he is from Halbrook Bank's fraud team, and he uses her name”: The caller says who he is, but anyone can say that on a call. It does not show who started it.
- If you tapped “We have seen a strange payment on your account. First I must be sure that I am speaking to you”: A good reason is why the call sounds real, but it does not show who started it.
- If you tapped “Please confirm your date of birth and your home address”: A real bank and a copy ask for the same two things, so the request cannot settle it.

**Why this is Identity theft and not Real request for details**

What decides it is how the call began: her phone rings. She did not start it, so she cannot tell whether he really works for the bank. Anyone can say so, and the number on her screen can be faked.

Knowing her name proves nothing either. Names and addresses are on lists that companies lose and criminals buy.

The fix is *the check*: hang up and call the number on your card. A real bank does not mind, and it costs a minute. The other way can cost months of someone else using your name.

**How to tell them apart**

Did I start this, through a way I already had? And does what they ask for match what the job needs, or does the list go further?

## Part 2 of 2: A friendly stranger, the two questions, then the drill

### 8. Friendly chat from a stranger

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 8 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-friendlychat`]

Third, the one that looks harmless: a stranger who is friendly, and wants to know about you.

*The wrong number*

> A text arrives on Sam's phone ⟦from a number he does not know⟧: 'Hi Sam! Sorry, I think I have the wrong number. I was trying to reach my friend Lou.' Sam answers kindly. Over the next two weeks the stranger, who says her name is Mei, writes to him every day. ⟦She asks what he does for work, whether he lives alone, and where he is going on vacation⟧. ⟦She has not asked him for anything⟧.

Mei asks for nothing that could cost Sam anything today, so it is easy to wave off. But every small answer tells a stranger something: where he works, that he lives alone, when his home will be empty. And after two weeks he trusts her, so a later request for money or papers comes from a friend, which is much harder to refuse.

A real wrong number ends after one polite reply. This one carries on, with questions about Sam.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check how it began: a text from a number Sam does not know.** A stranger started it, and you started nothing.
2. **Check whether it carries on: Mei writes every day for two weeks.** A real wrong number is over after one reply.
3. **Look at what she asks about: his work, whether he lives alone, his vacation.** That is your life, and nothing you started needs the answers.
4. **Check what she has not asked for yet: papers, numbers, money.** That is why it is not **Identity theft**, yet.

This is **Friendly chat from a stranger**. The chat is friendly, and the request has not come yet.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Do not keep replying to a stranger. Answer “wrong number” and stop, or block the number.** You do not owe a stranger a reply.
2. **If you have already been chatting, stop sharing where you work, where you live and who lives with you.** Each detail helps them plan what to ask for.
3. **Show the messages to someone you trust.** It is much easier to see from outside where a chat is going.
4. **If it turns to money, papers or a favor, end the chat.** That is the request the chat was building toward.

You may also hear this called “wrong-number scam” or “grooming” or “building rapport” or “reconnaissance”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Friendly chat from a stranger**.

### 10. Identity theft or Friendly chat from a stranger: telling them apart

**Story A**

> Imani has put her bicycle up for sale on a selling site. A stranger writes to her: 'I am not after the bike, I just liked your profile photo! What do you do for work? Do you live near the station?' She has never heard of him.

**Story B**

**How to tell them apart**

Do they ask for papers, a number that proves who I am, my date of birth or my address? Or only for chat about my job, my home, my family or my plans?

Is the person asking me to tell them about myself, or to send them money?


### 12. The question about what they want to know

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 12 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-f1`]

You have seen this question at the foot of the cards. Here it is in one place.

**The question:** **“What do they want to know about you?”**

**Its answers**

- **“Your ID details: papers, ID or card numbers, date of birth, address”**
  - Give this answer when you are asked for a document or a photo of one, an ID, tax or card number, your date of birth or your address, or a photo of yourself holding your ID.
  - Keeps **Identity theft** and **Real request for details**. Rules out **Friendly chat from a stranger**.
- **“Your life: your work, home, family, money or plans”**
  - Give this answer when someone you know only through messages, who reached you out of nowhere, keeps up a friendly chat about your work, family, money or plans, and asks for no numbers yet.
  - Keeps **Friendly chat from a stranger**. Rules out **Real request for details** and **Identity theft**.

**Why it matters**

Your ID details can be used right away to pose as you, though a real company may need some of them for something you started. Chat about your life is collected for a request that comes later, from someone who has become your friend.

It tells **Real request for details** from **Friendly chat from a stranger**. A clinic receptionist asks for your date of birth and address, because setting up your record needs them. A stranger asks what you do and whether you live alone, because getting to know you needs that, and nothing you started needs it. Both ask about you, but they want to know different things.

**How to answer it**

1. **Check whether they ask for papers or numbers: a passport, an ID or card number, your date of birth, your address.** That is **“Your ID details: papers, ID or card numbers, date of birth, address”**.
2. **Check whether they ask about your life: what you do for work, who you live with, what you plan to do. This is a stranger you know only through messages.** That is **“Your life: your work, home, family, money or plans”**.
3. **If they ask for both, the papers decide.** A chat that reaches a request for a passport is **“Your ID details: papers, ID or card numbers, date of birth, address”**, however friendly it has been.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

- Identity theft or Friendly chat from a stranger: Do they ask for papers, a number that proves who I am, my date of birth or my address? Or only for chat about my job, my home, my family or my plans?
- Friendly chat from a stranger or Real request for details: Is there something I started that these questions belong to? Or did someone I know only through messages start them, with nothing I am doing that needs the answers?

**Why it matters**

A real company and a scam can ask for the same facts about you. What tells them apart is whether you started it yourself, and whether the reason given really needs what they ask for.

Two things tell a real request from its copy, and you can see both in the request itself: whether you started it, and whether what they ask for is what the job needs. If either is no, the facts are not going where you meant to send them, however convincing it sounds.

It is the same question you ask of a sign-in page: **“Did you start this, and is that all it asks?”**

**How to answer it**

1. **Ask first: did I start this? You did if you called the number on your card or bill, typed an address in yourself, or walked into their office.** If a call, text, email or stranger reached you first, you did not.
2. **Check where the number, link or app came from.** One that came with a message is never one you already had, even if you are the one who dials or taps it.
3. **Then ask: does what they ask for match what I came to do? A new account needs proof of who you are. A room held until a showing needs only a name and a way to reach you.** If the list goes further than the job, the answer is no.
4. **Answer from the request itself, before you give anything.** You cannot tell from the request whether they are honest: only *the check* settles that.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

You may have started it and still be asked for far more than the job needs: the answer is no, because both must be true. Or it may have reached you and sound exactly right, like the bank call that used Gabriela’s name: the answer is no too.

### 17. What to carry away

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 17 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap`]

That is the whole unit in a few lines.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What do they want to know about you?
- Your ID details: papers, ID or card numbers, date of birth, address → Identity theft · Real request for details
- Your life: your work, home, family, money or plans → Friendly chat from a stranger

Did you start this, and is that all it asks?
- Yes: you started it through a way you already had, and it asks for nothing more → Real request for details
- No: it came to you, or it asks for more than you set out to do → Identity theft · Friendly chat from a stranger

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Real request for details**: facts about you, asked for something you started yourself through a way you already had, and nothing more than that needs.
- **Identity theft**: a request for your ID details, like your ID number or date of birth, that came to you, or asks for more than its reason needs.
- **Friendly chat from a stranger**: someone you know only through messages who reached you out of nowhere, friendly questions about your work, family, money or plans, and nothing yet to pay or send.

**To carry away**

- A request for facts about you is **“Tell them about yourself”**, unless it also asks you to install something, sign in or pay: then the earlier answer wins. Then ask **“What do they want to know about you?”** Papers and numbers that identify you, or your life?
- Then ask whether it fits: did you start it, through *a way you already had*, and is what they ask for what you came to do? The facts never decide it: **Real request for details** and **Identity theft** ask for the same passport.
- A request that came to you does not fit, however real it sounds, and knowing your name, address or date of birth proves nothing. Stop, and use *the check*: contact them yourself, through *a way you already had*.
- A chat that asks for nothing is the stage before the request, not a sign of safety. When the request comes, it will be for money or papers.
- Most requests for facts are real. A doctor’s form, a new account or a job you accepted all fit.

A plan is one line: if I see this, then I will do that. The worst moment to decide what to do is when the request arrives, so decide now. The lines below are examples: use one, change it, or write your own.

- a call, a message or a stranger that I did not start asks for my date of birth, my address or my card number, say that I will call back, end the call or leave the message, and contact them on a number I already had, such as the one on my card or my bill
