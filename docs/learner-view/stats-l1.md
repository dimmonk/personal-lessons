# Statistical Claims: Find the claim and its number

Revision 1, draft (the screen carries the draft line). Part: Find the claim and its number. Where the subject ends up: When you meet a number on your phone (a headline and its article, a chart, an ad, a post or a report) and are about to believe, share or act on it, you can tell whether it shows what it is used to claim, point to what is wrong or missing when it does not, and say how big it is in plain terms (so many in 1,000).

## The why

A number on your phone comes with a claim: a headline, a post or an ad says what the number means. The text underneath often says something smaller.
Here you find the claim, find the number it rests on, and check whether the text shows what the headline says.

## Step 1: What to find

Every headline, post, ad or report has three things to find. What it claims. The number it rests on. Whether the text shows what it claims.
A claim is one of five kinds. A cause says one thing makes another happen (“cuts”, “boosts”, “leads to”). A comparison says one thing is more, less or better than another, or changed between two times. A size says how big or how common something is. A forecast says what will happen. A “most people” claim says what most people do or think.
Then there are three answers. It holds up: the text shows what the headline says. It does not show it: the text says less than the headline does. You cannot tell from this: the text gives no number, or too little to check.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “A headline with a number in it has been checked.”
The right line: A headline is the writer’s short version. The facts are in the text under it, and the text sometimes says less. Where the two differ, go with the text.

## Step 2: A worked example

A news story, from "Harbor Ledger · Health":

> # Coffee cuts heart attack risk by 12%, study finds
>
> People who drank three cups of coffee a day had 12% fewer heart attacks than people who drank none, researchers reported Tuesday.
> The study followed 8,400 adults for six years. Among those who drank no coffee, 25 in 1,000 had a heart attack. Among those who drank three cups a day, 22 in 1,000 did.
> The coffee drinkers also exercised more and smoked less, the team noted. “This is a link, not proof that coffee protects the heart,” said lead researcher Dr. Alan Pruitt.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**What is this claiming?**
- One thing makes another happen
- One thing is more, less or better than another, or it changed
- How big or how common something is
- What will happen
- What most people do or think

The working, one step at a time:
1. Find what the headline claims. The headline says coffee “cuts” heart attack risk. A word like “cuts” says one thing makes another happen.
1. Find the number it rests on. The first sentence has it: 12% fewer heart attacks among people who drank three cups a day.
1. Check the headline against the text. The text calls it “a link, not proof”, and says the coffee drinkers also exercised more and smoked less. So the text does not show that the coffee did it. The headline says more than the text.

Then the answer: One thing makes another happen / People who drank three cups of coffee a day had 12% fewer heart attacks than people who drank none, researchers reported Tuesday. / Does not show it. The headline says coffee “cuts” the risk, but the study only compared people who already drank different amounts of coffee. The lead researcher calls it “a link, not proof”, and the coffee drinkers also exercised more and smoked less. In plain terms, the numbers are 25 heart attacks in every 1,000 people who drank no coffee and 22 in every 1,000 who drank three cups: 3 fewer in 1,000.
What you would need to see: You would need people split by chance into coffee and no coffee, or two groups alike in exercise and smoking, and the difference still showing.
