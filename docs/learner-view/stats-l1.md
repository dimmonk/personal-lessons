# Statistical Claims: What it claims, and the number it rests on

Revision 2, live. Part: What it claims, and the number it rests on. Where the subject ends up: When a headline and its article, a chart, an ad or a post on your phone is about to make you believe, share or act on a number, you can say whether the number shows what it is used to claim, point to the line that decides it (or say what is missing), and say how big it really is in plain counts, such as from 3 in 1,000 to 2 in 1,000 over ten years.

## The why

A number on your phone comes with a claim: the headline, post or ad says what the number means. The text underneath often says less.
People who check claims for a living first pin down exactly what is being said, and only then look at the number. Here you do the same: find the claim, find the number it rests on, and see whether the text shows it.

## Step 1: What to find

Every headline, post, ad or report has three things to find.
What it claims: what a reader takes away from it, in plain words. Does it say one thing makes another happen (“cuts”, “boosts”, “causes”)? Or only that two things went together (“linked to”, “people who … had …”)? A headline that puts a change right after something (“fell after the new lights”) invites you to hear a cause: take it as claiming one. And who is it about?
The number it rests on: the sentence with the count, the share or the change the claim stands on. If no sentence gives one, tap Not given.
Then the verdict. It holds up: the text shows what it claims. It does not show it: the text itself shows the claim goes too far, because its numbers say less, it is about other people, or it names something else that could explain it. You cannot tell from this: something you would need is not in the text at all.
So a missing number does not always mean you cannot tell: if the text says in words that the claim goes too far, it does not show it. And a number can be there while the one thing you need is missing.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “A headline with a number in it has been checked.”
The right line: A headline is the writer’s short version. The facts are in the text under it, and the text sometimes says less. Where the two differ, go with the text.

## Step 2: A worked example

A news story, from "Harbor Ledger · Health":

> # Coffee cuts heart attack risk by 12%, study finds
>
> People who drank three cups of coffee a day had 12% fewer heart attacks than people who drank none, researchers reported Tuesday.
> The study followed 8,400 adults for six years. Among those who drank no coffee, 25 in 1,000 had a heart attack. Among those who drank three cups a day, 22 in 1,000 did.
> The coffee drinkers also exercised more and smoked less, the team noted. “This is a link, not proof that coffee protects the heart,” said lead researcher Dr. Alan Pruitt.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**What is this claiming?**
(the options come in an order that depends on the seed of the question)
- Coffee makes heart attacks less likely
- People who drink coffee had fewer heart attacks
- Coffee cuts heart attack risk by 12% in people with heart disease

The working, one step at a time:
1. Find what the headline claims.: The headline says coffee “cuts” heart attack risk. “Cuts” says coffee makes heart attacks less likely, not only that the two went together. It says it about people in general.
1. Find the number it rests on.: The first sentence has it: 12% fewer heart attacks among people who drank three cups a day.
1. Check the headline against the text.: The text calls it “a link, not proof”, and says the coffee drinkers also exercised more and smoked less. So the text does not show that the coffee did it. The headline says more than the text.

Then the answer: Coffee makes heart attacks less likely / People who drank three cups of coffee a day had 12% fewer heart attacks than people who drank none, researchers reported Tuesday. / Does not show it. The headline says coffee “cuts” the risk, but the study only compared people who already drank different amounts of coffee. The lead researcher calls it “a link, not proof”, and the coffee drinkers also exercised more and smoked less. In plain terms, the numbers are 25 heart attacks in every 1,000 people who drank no coffee and 22 in every 1,000 who drank three cups: 3 fewer in 1,000.
What you would need to see: You would need people split by chance into coffee and no coffee, or two groups alike in exercise and smoking, and the difference still showing.
