## Part 1 of 3: A good breath, and two ways the breath goes wrong

### 1. Most “I have no breath” problems are one of four habits

*Unit Three · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 1 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

When the air gives out in a line, you can tell which of five things is happening before you change anything: four habits, each with a fix you can try the same day, and one where nothing is wrong. A hand on your belly, a mirror and your own ears are enough.

You grab a breath at the end of a long line and the air is gone halfway through the next. Or you hear a hiss in the soft parts. Or you shout the chorus and your throat is tired for the rest of the night. Or you find yourself gasping in the middle of a word. All of these feel like “I have no breath”, and each one has a different fix.

Most of the time it is one of four habits. Sometimes the breath was fine all along, and the fix is to leave it alone.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“What bothers you about it?”** Its answers:

- **“How the top notes come out”**: give this answer when the trouble starts where the notes go up: at the top of the line the voice shouts, flips, clamps, cannot reach the note, or goes light in a way you are not sure of.
- **“How the air holds up”**: give this answer when the trouble is with the air: it is gone before the line ends, grabbed in the middle of a word, heard leaking out with the sound, or driven out hard, or you suspect the way you breathe. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“Whether you are on the note”**: give this answer when what you doubt is the note itself: whether the note you sing is the song’s note, because it sounds a shade off, you slide into it, you hunt for it, or you simply are not sure.
- **“How the words sound when they come out”**: give this answer when the notes and the air are fine, and what bothers you is the sound: pinched and nasal, dull and muffled, words that run together, or a voice that only sounds strange recorded.
- **“Only that it is not the record’s voice”**: give this answer when the notes are right, nothing is tight, sore or short of air, the words are clear, and the only complaint is that it does not sound like the singer on the record.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. A breath that works

*Unit Three · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 2 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-breathfine`]

Start with the one that needs no fix, so you know what the other four are measured against.

*The quiet breath*

> Rosa is driving and singing the second verse of a slow song, and she is sure she has no breath for it. At a red light she puts a hand on her belly and looks in the mirror. ⟦Her belly moves out when she breathes in, her shoulders do not move, and the breath is too quiet to hear⟧. She sings the verse again and ⟦still has air left at the end of the line⟧.

Rosa was sure she had no breath, so she checked instead of guessing. Her belly moved out, her shoulders stayed still, the breath made no sound, and there was air left when the line ended. Nothing is wrong with a breath like that.

Feeling short of air in a line is the usual complaint, and it does not always mean the breath is wrong. Rosa could have spent a week changing a breath that already worked. The check takes a few seconds.

**How to spot it**

1. **Put a hand on your belly and breathe in: Rosa’s belly moved out.** A belly that moves out means the breath went low.
2. **Watch your shoulders in a mirror: Rosa’s did not move.** Shoulders that stay still mean the breath is not high.
3. **Listen to the breath: Rosa’s was too quiet to hear.** A quiet breath is not a gasp.
4. **Check the end of the line: Rosa still had air left.** Air left over means the breath was enough for the line.

This is **A breath that works**. Nothing is wrong here. It has a name so that you do not try to fix a breath that already works.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Change nothing about how you breathe.** Nothing here needs a fix.
2. **When the doubt comes back, run the check again: a hand on your belly, your shoulders in a mirror, and a listen to the breath.** It tells you whether the breath is the problem.
3. **If the line still sounds wrong, ask again: “What bothers you about it?”** Then the trouble is somewhere other than the air.

You may also hear this called “breathing low” or “breathing from the belly”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **A breath that works**.

### 4. A shallow breath

*Unit Three · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 4 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-shallowbreath`]

Now the opposite of **A breath that works**: a breath that is quick and stays high.

*The gasp*

> At karaoke, Marcus ⟦takes a quick, noisy gasp⟧ before the long chorus line. ⟦His shoulders jump up as he does it⟧, and ⟦halfway through the line the air is gone⟧. The last words come out squeezed.

Marcus took a quick, noisy gasp, and his shoulders jumped. A breath that lifts the shoulders is a high one, and a high one gives out sooner than a low one. Halfway through the line his air was gone, and the last words came out squeezed.

**How to spot it**

1. **Watch your shoulders as you breathe in: Marcus’s jumped up.** Shoulders or a chest that lift mean the breath is high.
2. **Listen to the breath: Marcus’s was a quick, noisy gasp.** A low breath is quiet.
3. **Check the middle of the line: Marcus’s air was gone halfway through.** A low breath lasts the whole line.
4. **Listen to the end of the line: Marcus’s last words were squeezed.** A squeezed ending comes when the air is gone.

This is **A shallow breath**. The breath is the problem: it is taken quickly and high, so the air is gone early.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Breathe low, the way you breathe lying down, with a hand on your belly.** Your belly should move out and your shoulders should stay still.
2. **Breathe in through your mouth, quietly, as if you were surprised.** That makes the breath quiet instead of a gasp.
3. **Practice a slow hiss: breathe low, then hiss “sss” as long as you can while you count.** Your count will grow over the days, which shows the breath is lasting longer.
4. **In the song, start your breath a beat early.** That leaves time for a low breath instead of a gasp.

You may also hear this called “chest breathing” or “breathing high”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **A shallow breath**.

### 7. Unplanned breaths

*Unit Three · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 7 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-unplanned`]

Next, a breath that is low and full, but taken in the wrong place.

*The chopped verse*

> In choir, Beth ⟦breathes in low and full, belly out and shoulders still⟧. But she waits until she runs out, so ⟦she grabs her next breath in the middle of the word 'tomorrow'⟧, and again halfway through the next phrase. ⟦She has not marked a single breath⟧ in her copy of the words, and every line comes out in chunks.

Beth’s breaths are fine. They are low and full, with her belly out and her shoulders still. The trouble is where she takes them: she waits until she runs out, so a breath lands in the middle of a word and the line comes out in chunks.

Nobody planned those breaths, and that is the whole problem. A breath at a comma goes unnoticed. A breath forced into the middle of a word breaks the word in two.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check the breath itself: Beth’s was low and full.** If it were a quick gasp with the shoulders up, it would be **A shallow breath**.
2. **Find where she breathed in: in the middle of “tomorrow”.** A breath in the middle of a word was taken when she ran out, not when she chose.
3. **Listen to the line: it came out in chunks.** Every breath taken wherever the air gave out chops the line there.
4. **Look in your copy of the words for marks that say where to breathe: Beth had none.** With no marks, running out is the only signal to breathe.

This is **Unplanned breaths**. The breath is fine. What is missing is a plan for where to take it.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Read the words and mark where to breathe with a tick: at commas, at the ends of lines, and before a long phrase.** Then each breath has a place before you need it.
2. **Take a breath early at a marked spot, even before you need it.** That way you do not run out in the middle of a word.
3. **If a line is too long, sneak a quick breath at a comma instead of in the middle of a word.** A comma is a natural place to pause, and the middle of a word is not.
4. **Speak the words in rhythm with your marked breaths before you sing them.** The places are in your head by the time you sing.

You may also hear this called “breathing mid-word” or “gasping between words”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Unplanned breaths**.

## Part 2 of 3: Air that leaks, and air that is shoved

### 10. An airy tone

*Unit Three · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 10 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-airytone`]

Now a different way to run short: the breath is fine, and the air leaks.

*The whispery ballad*

> In the shower, Pablo sings a soft ballad and does not like what he hears. His breath is low and quiet, but ⟦the sound is soft and whispery, and he can hear air hissing out along with the notes⟧. ⟦After two short lines the air is gone⟧. He did not mean it to sound like a whisper.

Pablo’s breath was low and quiet, so he did everything right on the way in. The air still ran out in two short lines, because it was leaking past his voice without turning into sound. That is the hiss he heard, and it is why the sound was soft and whispery.

More air does not fix a leak. A cleaner start to the sound does. An airy sound sung on purpose for one soft line is a choice, but Pablo did not choose it.

**How to spot it**

1. **Listen for the air itself: Pablo could hear it hissing out along with the notes.** A clean sound has no hiss in it.
2. **Listen to the sound: it was soft and whispery.** Leaking air is what makes it whispery.
3. **Check how fast the air ran out: after two short lines it was gone.** Leaking air runs out fast, however the breath was taken.
4. **Check the breath going in: Pablo’s was low and quiet.** That rules out the breath as the problem.
5. **Ask whether you meant it: Pablo did not.** An airy sound chosen for one soft line is a choice, not a fault.

This is **An airy tone**. The breath is fine. The air is escaping instead of turning into sound.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Hum the line first.** A hum cannot be airy, so you feel what a clean sound is like.
2. **Open the hum into “ah” and keep the same feeling.** The clean feeling carries over into the open sound.
3. **Start each note with a tiny, gentle “uh”, as in “uh-oh”.** That starts the sound clean.
4. **Use less air, not more.** The trouble is a leak, not too little air.

You may also hear this called “a breathy voice” or “whispery singing”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **An airy tone**.

### 12. Forcing the air

*Unit Three · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 12 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-forcing`]

The opposite of a leak: here the air is not leaking, it is being shoved.

*The loud chorus*

> At a party, Dario sings the big chorus as loud as he can. ⟦He shoves the air out hard on every word⟧, the sound comes out ⟦loud and harsh⟧, and by the second chorus ⟦his throat is tired⟧.

Dario’s trouble was not a leak. He drove the air out hard, and the louder he wanted to be, the harder he shoved. The sound came out loud and harsh, and his throat paid for it by the second chorus. The notes can drift up as well.

The song does not need him to be that loud. Loudness comes from an open mouth and a bright vowel, not from more air.

**How to spot it**

1. **Feel what you do with the air: Dario shoved it out hard on every word.** A shove is the opposite of a small, steady stream.
2. **Listen to the sound: it was loud and harsh.** Loud and harsh together is what air pushed too hard sounds like.
3. **Notice your throat afterward: Dario’s was tired by the second chorus.** A tired throat is what pushing costs.

This is **Forcing the air**. The trouble is not too little air. It is too much, pushed too hard.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Sing the line at talking volume.** The song does not need you to be loud.
2. **Keep the stream of air small and steady, like the slow hiss.** A small, steady stream is the opposite of a shove.
3. **Sing the line on a lip trill: blow through loose, fluttering lips while you sing.** A lip trill stops the moment you push too much air.
4. **For more loudness, open your mouth wider and use a bright vowel.** Loudness comes from the mouth, not from more air.

You may also hear this called “oversinging” or “pushing air”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Forcing the air**.

### 15. When a shove comes after a quick, high breath

### 16. The question to ask about the air

*Unit Three · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 16 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-air`]

Here is the question and its five answers in one place.

**The question:** **“What is the air doing?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other four.

- **“It is low and quiet, and lasts the line”**
  - Give this answer when you breathe in low and quietly, with the belly moving out and the shoulders still, the sound is clear, and there is air left at the end of the line.
  - It leads to **A breath that works**.
- **“It is gone before the line ends, after a quick, high breath”**
  - Give this answer when you breathe in quickly, with the shoulders or the chest lifting, and the air is gone before the line ends.
  - It leads to **A shallow breath**.
- **“It leaks out with the sound, which is soft and whispery”**
  - Give this answer when you can hear air escaping along with the sound, the sound is soft and whispery, and the air runs out fast however you breathed in.
  - It leads to **An airy tone**.
- **“It is driven out hard, and the sound is loud and harsh”**
  - Give this answer when you drive the air out hard, the sound is loud and harsh, the throat tires, and the note tends to drift up.
  - It leads to **Forcing the air**.
- **“It is grabbed wherever you run out, mid-word”**
  - Give this answer when you breathe in low and get a full breath, but you take the breaths wherever you happen to run out, in the middle of a word or a phrase, because none were planned.
  - It leads to **Unplanned breaths**.

**Why it matters**

Each has its own fix: a lower breath, a cleaner start to the sound, a gentler stream of air, a plan for where to breathe, or nothing at all. How long the line is and how loud the song is do not change the answer.

“I ran out of air” is the same complaint in four of the five. What tells them apart is what you can check: the breath going in, the sound coming out, and where you took the breath.

**How to answer it**

1. **Check the breath going in: a hand on your belly, your shoulders in a mirror, and a listen.** A quick, noisy gasp that lifts the shoulders is **A shallow breath**.
2. **If the breath was low and quiet, listen to the sound.** A soft, whispery sound with air hissing out is **An airy tone**, and a loud, harsh one with the air shoved is **Forcing the air**.
3. **If the sound is clean, look at where you took your breaths.** Breaths taken wherever you ran out, even mid-word, are **Unplanned breaths**, and a breath with air left at the end is **A breath that works**.
4. **Find the exact words that show it: the breath, the sound, the place.** If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Sometimes two answers both seem to fit. Each pair below has one question that separates it. If a story shows two at once, use the answer named under the pair.

- A shallow breath or A breath that works: Where does the breath go: does your belly move out while your shoulders stay still, or do your shoulders or chest lift?
- A shallow breath or Unplanned breaths: Was the breath itself a quick gasp with the shoulders up, or was it full and low and taken at a bad moment?
- An airy tone or Forcing the air: Is the sound soft and whispery with air hissing through it, or loud and harsh with the air shoved out?
- Forcing the air or A shallow breath: Was the breath going in a quick, high one, or was the only trouble how hard the air went out?
- An airy tone or A breath that works: Is there a hiss of air in the sound, or is the sound clean with air to spare at the end of the line?
- An airy tone or A shallow breath: Was the breath a quick one that lifted the shoulders, or was it low and quiet with a hiss in the sound?

### 19. What to carry away

*Unit Three · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 19 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap`]

You can now tell all five apart on your own.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What is the air doing?
- It is low and quiet, and lasts the line → A breath that works
- It is gone before the line ends, after a quick, high breath → A shallow breath
- It leaks out with the sound, which is soft and whispery → An airy tone
- It is driven out hard, and the sound is loud and harsh → Forcing the air
- It is grabbed wherever you run out, mid-word → Unplanned breaths

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **A breath that works**: a low, quiet breath with the belly moving out and the shoulders still, a clear sound, and air left at the end of the line.
- **A shallow breath**: a breath taken quickly with the shoulders or the chest lifting, and air that is gone before the line ends.
- **An airy tone**: air you can hear escaping with the sound, a soft, whispery sound, and air that runs out fast whatever the breath was.
- **Forcing the air**: air driven out hard, a loud, harsh sound, a throat that tires, and a note that drifts up.
- **Unplanned breaths**: a full, low breath, and breaths taken wherever you run out, in the middle of a word or a phrase, with none planned.

**To carry away**

- When the air gives out, check what it is doing before you change anything: a hand on your belly, your shoulders in a mirror, and a listen to the breath and the sound.
- A quick, noisy gasp with the shoulders up is **A shallow breath**. A low, full breath taken wherever you run out is **Unplanned breaths**. A soft, whispery sound with air hissing out is **An airy tone**. Air shoved out hard is **Forcing the air**.
- When a quick, high breath and a shove show up together, fix the breath first: a low breath fixes both.
- **A breath that works** is one of the five. It needs no fix, and trying to fix it is a mistake too.

### 20. A plan, if you want one

*Unit Three · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 20 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `plan`, id `plan`]
