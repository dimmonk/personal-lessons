# Strategy: Better, or different?

Revision 1, draft (the screen carries the draft line). Part: Better, or different?. Where the subject ends up: After this you can look at a business decision (expand, add a product, chase a big customer, answer a rival, invest) and tell whether it is a strategy, say where the business makes its money and why, and pick the move that keeps it different in a way rivals cannot copy, when you face that decision in your own business.

## The why

Most of an owner’s week goes on running the business better: faster, cheaper, fewer mistakes. That work matters, but it is not strategy, and it cannot fix every problem.
Strategy teachers first settle which kind of problem they are looking at. Here you learn to tell, from the facts of a story, whether a business needs to run better or to choose a different position.

## Step 1: Running better, or a different position

Running it better means doing the same things your rivals do, only better: on time, with less waste, with the tools they already use.
A different position means doing different things from your rivals, or the same things in a different way, for customers who want that.
To tell which a business needs, put one question to it: would doing the same things the rivals do, only better, fix it?
Yes, when the business is behind its rivals at the same work, and the rivals show it can be done: late where they are on time, wasteful where they are not. Then run it better.
No, when the business already runs as well as its rivals and loses customers or profit anyway, when every gain is copied within months, or when a rival’s price is below any cost the business could reach by running better. Then choose a different position.
Facts can pull both ways: a business can be late at something and also face a rival it cannot match on price. Then find which fact explains the loss, and let that one decide.
A different position looks like this: a cleaning firm that bids against three alike could clean only medical offices, to their stricter rules, which the other three do not do. Choosing one well comes later; here you only decide whether one is needed.
Why it matters: what one business does better, the others can buy or copy, so the gain ends up with customers as lower prices. Only being different keeps something for you.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “Our strategy is to be the best-run shop in town: new equipment, the methods the top shops use, no waste.”
The right line: That is running the business well. It is needed, but rivals can copy it, so it is not a strategy. A strategy is a choice to be different in a way that rivals cannot simply buy.

## Step 2: Two airlines

| A | B |
|---|---|
| In the 1990s, Southwest Airlines did not try to be a full-service airline run more cheaply. It chose to do different things from the full-service airlines, and gave up some of what they offer. | Picture an airline that copies the best practice of the full-service airlines: the same service, run with fewer staff and newer software. |

Compare: Which would a rival find hard to match? Anyone can match the second by buying the same software. To match the first, a full-service airline would have to give up what makes it full-service.

## Step 3: Two plans of the same shape

| A | B |
|---|---|
| “Food comes out cold because our kitchen has no fixed order for tickets, while the bistros nearby serve the same dishes hot. So we fix the order: a ticket rail, and the cook sends dishes out by table.” | “Online printers undercut us on cards and flyers. So we become the shop for rush and tricky jobs: same-day printing, a check of every file, no cut-price card deal.” |

Compare: Both name a challenge, one approach and steps that serve it. Only the second is a strategy: it chooses to be different from its rivals. The first catches up with rivals at the same work, so it is a good plan to run the business better.

## Step 4: A worked example

A written note: "Notes on our office-cleaning business", Written in November.

> Three other cleaning firms in town bid for the same office contracts. All four of us use the same products, schedules and checklists.
> This year we bought faster floor machines and cut our time per office by 15%.
> Within months the other three bought the same machines, and every firm’s bids dropped by about 15%.
> Our profit is lower now than before we bought the machines.

The working, one step at a time:
1. Would doing the same things as the rivals, only better, fix it? Find the line that answers.: The third line: the firm did run better, and the rivals copied the machines within months, so every bid fell.
1. Decide.: Running better was copied at once, so more of it would leave the firm where it is. It needs to be different from the other three, not faster than them.

Then the answer: Within months the other three bought the same machines, and every firm’s bids dropped by about 15%. / Choose a different position. The firm did run better, and the rivals copied the machines within months, so the gain went to customers as lower bids. Running better cannot fix that; it needs a different position.
