# Strategy: Do the pieces fit?

Revision 1, draft (the screen carries the draft line). Part: Do the pieces fit?. Where the subject ends up: After this you can look at a business decision (expand, add a product, chase a big customer, answer a rival, invest) and tell whether it is a strategy, say where the business makes its money and why, and pick the move that keeps it different in a way rivals cannot copy, when you face that decision in your own business.

## The why

A position is not one clever idea. It is the way the whole business runs: who you hire, how you sell, what you stock, when you open.
Strategists check that every piece serves the position and helps the others, because a rival can copy one piece but rarely all of them. Here you learn to find the piece that pulls the other way.

## Step 1: Every piece serves the position

First read the position: who the business serves, and what it gives them that others do not.
Then hold each activity against it. Does it serve that position? Does it help the other activities do their part?
An activity that serves another kind of customer does not fit. Nor does the trade’s usual way of working when it pulls against this position, however sensible it looks on its own.
An activity can look costly or odd and still fit: a gym that pays for coaches all day, a cafe that lets one pass keep a seat all day. Judge it by the position, not by what it costs.
To replace a piece that does not fit, pick the activity that serves the position and helps the others.
Why it matters: a rival can copy one piece in a month. To copy pieces that fit together, it has to copy them all, and give up whatever its own pieces serve.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “Each part of the business should follow the trade’s best practice: the cheapest supplier, the usual hours, the standard pay.”
The right line: The trade’s usual way serves the trade’s usual customers. Check each piece against your position, and choose the one that does, even when the usual way is cheaper.

## Step 2: One fund company

Vanguard’s position: funds that cost investors very little, for people who hold them for years.
Its funds buy and sell shares rarely, which keeps trading costs down. It does not pay for star fund managers. It sells straight to investors, not through brokers. It spends little on advertising. Staff bonuses are tied to saving costs.
Each piece serves low cost, and each helps the others: investors who hold for years do not need star managers or constant trading, and selling direct needs less advertising. A rival would have to change all of them at once to match it.

## Step 3: A worked example

Hal’s moving company: pianos and other heavy, fragile things. The position: nothing damaged, for owners who pay more than double the house-move rate for that.

Your own note, written after an argument: "How Hal’s moving company runs it", Written this year.

> Each crew has moved pianos together for years.
> Ramps and padded dollies are made for each kind of piano.
> We win jobs by quoting the lowest price in town.
> Every job is insured for the piano’s full value.
> Each crew does one job a day, so nobody rushes.

The working, one step at a time:
1. Read the position: who it serves and what it gives them.: Piano owners who pay double for nothing being damaged.
1. Hold each activity against it.: A crew that has worked together for years, gear made for each piano, full insurance and one job a day all serve care, and each makes the others work. The lowest quote in town serves owners who shop on price, and pulls crews toward rushing to make it pay.
1. Find what would serve the position and help the other activities.: Jobs from piano teachers and stores bring the owners who pay for care, which pays for the crews, the gear and the insurance.

Then the answer: We win jobs by quoting the lowest price in town. / Get jobs through piano teachers and piano stores, whose customers care most about the piano. Owners pay double for care, and the crews, the gear, the insurance and one job a day all serve that. The lowest quote serves price shoppers and pulls against the rest; jobs sent by piano teachers and stores bring the owners who pay for care.
