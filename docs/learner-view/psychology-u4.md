## Part 1 of 2: Superior, overlooked, clinging, or just ordinary

### 1. Before you call someone “a narcissist” or “borderline”, check the years

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient-pat`]

Before you tell a friend that your boss is “a narcissist”, your ex is “borderline” or your neighbor is “a psychopath”, check what you have actually seen. A real pattern takes years, many places and many people, and most loud, shy or dramatic people are not one.

Almost always, the person using the label has seen one bad week, one hard relationship, or one loud person at one party. This unit gives you what to look for instead: the years, what the person does when something goes against them, and whether it keeps costing someone.

There are six answers. Five are ways of being that keep hurting the person or the people around them. The sixth, **Just how they are**, is a person who is loud, shy, dramatic, blunt or touchy the same way for years and costs no one anything. It is the right answer most of the time. None of this is a diagnosis: only a professional can give one, after a long assessment.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“What are you looking at?”** Its answers:

- **“A choice and its reasons”**: give this answer when one person explains a choice, a view or an act of their own, and nobody else is the target.
- **“Something done to someone”**: give this answer when one person says or does something to another, about that person or about what happened between them.
- **“A lifelong pattern”**: give this answer when the same behavior shows up for years, in different places and with different people. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“Just a one-off”**: give this answer when one person acts a certain way on one occasion or for a short stretch, often after something happened to them, and there is nothing more to it.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. Years, many places, and a cost

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-pd`]

First, one word the whole unit leans on. Here is a man it fits.

*Twenty years and many jobs*

> Dale is fifty-four and has had five jobs in twenty years. In every one he has ended up in the same row: he tells his manager how the department should be run, calls his colleagues 'not in my league', and is furious when anyone else is thanked. He has left or been pushed out of all five. His first wife and his two sons say it was the same at home. His eldest son has not spoken to him for six years, and Dale says that is his son's loss.

Three things stand out about Dale. He has been like this for twenty years. He has been like it everywhere: five workplaces, a marriage and two sons. And it keeps costing: he has left or been pushed out of every job, and one son has not spoken to him in six years.

Those three together are what the word below stands for. A bad week, one hard boss or one rough marriage would not be enough. A “cost” is anything lost or harmed because of how the person is: a job, a friendship, money, trust, health.

**The word for this.** *Personality disorder*: the same behavior for years, in different places and with different people, that keeps hurting the person or the people around them. Only a professional can diagnose one, after a long evaluation. Here the names describe what a story shows, not a person.

Being shy, or loud, for years in every place is not this on its own. It has to keep costing someone.


### 3. Grandiose narcissism

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 3 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-narcgrand`]

Start with the best-known label, the one people mean by “a narcissist”.

*The law partner*

> Dennis is fifty-three and a partner at his third law firm. In his twenties he told the other trainees that they were there to carry his files, and ⟦he still opens every meeting by describing how the firm managed before he arrived⟧. He takes the corner room, sits at the head of every table and talks over the juniors. When a trainee was praised at last year's dinner for winning a case, he told her in front of the team that she was '⟦a nobody who got lucky⟧'. His wife says that in thirty years he has never asked how her day was. ⟦Two juniors have resigned this year with the same complaint⟧, and his son, who is twenty-four, no longer visits.

Dennis is not just confident. Confidence is believing you can do something. Dennis needs the people around him to treat him as special, over and over, and when someone else is praised it feels like a threat. So he runs that person down.

The cost is what makes it more than a loud, sure man: two juniors resigned, and his son no longer visits.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check the years and places: three law firms and thirty years of marriage.** One bad workplace or one rough month is not enough.
2. **Watch how he treats people: the corner room, talking over the juniors, never asking his wife about her day.** He acts as if he is better and owed more, with little interest in anyone else.
3. **Watch what he does when someone else is praised: she is “a nobody who got lucky”.** Anger or scorn when he is not the special one is the sign.
4. **Look for the cost: two juniors resigned, and his son no longer visits.** Without a cost, it is just a loud person.

This is **Grandiose narcissism**. “Narcissism” means a sense of worth that depends on being treated as special, and “grandiose” means acting as if you are better than others.

You may also hear this called “a narcissist” or “overt narcissism” or “narcissistic personality disorder”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Grandiose narcissism**.

### 5. Just how they are

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 5 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-ordpersonality`]

Most people you will ever describe are not like Dennis, and there is a name for them.

*The loud baker*

> Rosa is fifty-eight and has run her bakery for thirty years. She ⟦has been the loudest and surest person in every room since she was a girl⟧: she ran her school's basketball team, her union branch and the church flower guild the same way, by telling everyone what to do and being right about half the time. Her family teases her about it. When she gets a recipe wrong she laughs and says 'wrong again'. When her sister's husband won the town's business award, she organized the party. ⟦Her staff have stayed an average of fifteen years, and she has the same three friends she made at school⟧.

Rosa is bossy and loud, and has been since she was a girl, so you might think of **Grandiose narcissism**. But she laughs at her own mistakes, and when her sister’s husband won an award she organized the party. Nothing is being lost: her staff stay, and so do her friends.

Everyone has a way of being: shy, loud, dramatic, blunt, touchy. A strong one is still ordinary. It turns into something else only when it keeps costing someone, year after year.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check the years and places: thirty years at the bakery, and before that a school team, a union and a church guild.** It is the same Rosa everywhere, so it is a way of being and not a mood.
2. **See what she is like: the loudest and surest person in every room.** A strong way of being is still an ordinary one.
3. **Watch what she does when someone else is thanked: she organizes the party.** No scorn, no sulking and no scene.
4. **Look for the cost, and find none: her staff stay fifteen years, and she still has the three friends from school.** No lasting damage means there is nothing more to name.

This is **Just how they are**, and it is the answer you will need most often. Rosa is loud and bossy, but nothing she does keeps hurting anyone.

### 7. Grandiose narcissism or Just how they are: telling them apart

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-narcgrand-ordpersonality`]

Both can be loud and sure of themselves, and this is the pair people mix up most. Here are two head chefs who each say they are the best in the city.

**Story A**

> Paolo is fifty-six and has been head chef in four restaurants. He tells every new cook that he is the best chef in the city and that they are lucky to be near him. When a young cook was written up in the paper, Paolo told the kitchen that she was 'a pretty face with a borrowed recipe' and stopped giving her shifts. In each kitchen the best cooks have left within a year, and his two daughters say they stopped bringing friends to the restaurant years ago.

**Story B**

> Sunil is fifty-five and has been head chef in four restaurants. He tells every new cook that he is the best chef in the city and that they will have to work hard to keep up. When a young cook was written up in the paper, Sunil framed the article and put it by the kitchen door. In each kitchen the cooks have stayed for years, several have opened places of their own and still call him, and his two daughters work in the restaurant on Saturdays.

**What to compare.** Both chefs tell every new cook that he is the best in the city. Compare two things: what each does when a young cook is written up in the paper, and what the years have cost.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Acts superior, and gets angry when not treated as special”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story A.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A, Paolo runs the young cook down: “a pretty face with a borrowed recipe”. The best cooks leave within a year, and his daughters stopped bringing friends years ago. That is **Grandiose narcissism**.

In Story B, Sunil says the same thing about himself, and then frames the article. His cooks stay for years, and some still call him. That is **Just how they are**.

The boast is the same. What makes it **Grandiose narcissism** is the scorn when someone else is praised, and the cost that keeps coming back.

**How to tell them apart**

When someone else is praised or chosen, does this person turn on them, and have people been lost over the years because of it? Or do people stay?


### 8. Vulnerable narcissism

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 8 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-narcvuln`]

The next one is built the same way underneath, but you can miss it because the person is quiet.

*The clerk*

> Ellis is forty-seven and works in a city government office. Since school he has said that the teachers liked the other boys better. In three offices ⟦he has stopped speaking to anyone who was promoted over him⟧, and he says, quietly, '⟦Some people just get handed things⟧.' When his younger sister got engaged he said 'lovely' and left before the cake, and ⟦he has not called her in two years⟧. His wife says he keeps a count of who has been thanked and who has not, and that she is always on the list of those who never thank him. He has turned down two promotions because 'they would only have given it to me when it no longer mattered'.

Put Ellis next to Dennis. Dennis is loud and scornful. Ellis is quiet, polite and says he has been overlooked. Underneath they work the same way: how much they think they are worth depends on being treated as special.

The difference is what they do when it is not given. Dennis hits out. Ellis goes quiet, keeps a count of what he is owed, and lets the resentment grow. Both care little how other people feel, and both keep costing.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check the years and places: school, three offices, a marriage and a sister.** The same thing, over years, with different people.
2. **Listen for “I am overlooked”: “Some people just get handed things.”** He says he is owed more than he gets.
3. **Watch what he does when someone else is chosen: he stops speaking to the colleagues who were promoted, and to his sister.** He goes cold and hurt, where **Grandiose narcissism** would run them down.
4. **Look for the cost: his sister has not heard from him in two years, and he has turned down two promotions.** The silence keeps costing him and the people close to him.

This is **Vulnerable narcissism**. “Vulnerable” means easily hurt: the same need to be treated as special, shown as hurt silence instead of anger.

You may also hear this called “covert narcissism”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Vulnerable narcissism**.

Most quiet people are just quiet. Here are two colleagues who both keep to themselves.

**Story A**

> Hugh is forty-one and keeps to himself at work, as he has done in four offices. **Story B**

> Amara is forty-one and keeps to herself at work, as she has done in four offices. She eats lunch at her desk, does not go to the bar and says she is happiest with a quiet week. When a colleague was thanked at a team meeting, Amara sent her a note saying well done. Over the years her managers have written that she can be relied on, and the two friends she has made in each office are still her friends.

**What to compare.** Both keep to themselves at work, in four offices. Compare two things: what each does when a colleague is thanked, and what it has cost.

In Story B, Amara is just as quiet. When a colleague is thanked she sends a note saying well done, and her friends from each office are still her friends. That is **Just how they are**.

The quietness is the same. What differs is the count of what others owe, the cold silence when someone else is thanked, and the cost.

**How to tell them apart**

Is the silence a count of what is owed, going cold on someone who was thanked, with people lost to it? Or is the person simply quiet, and still on good terms with the people around them?


### 11. Borderline personality

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 11 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-borderline`]

The first two were about needing to be treated as special. This one is about not being able to bear being left.

*The friend who was going abroad*

> Nadia is thirty-one. Since she was fifteen she has not been able to bear a friend or partner pulling away. When a friend said she would be abroad for a month, Nadia ⟦sent forty messages in two days, offered to pay for her flights home early and begged her to stay⟧. When the friend replied a day late, Nadia wrote, '⟦You are a fake and I never want to see you again⟧,' and blocked her. The next morning she sent twelve apologies: 'You are the only person who has ever understood me.' It has gone the same way with every close friend since school and with all four of her partners; she called one of them 'the love of my life' in March and 'a monster' in April. ⟦She has lost three friends and a job⟧, after she called her manager at midnight to ask whether he was going to let her go.

Nadia looks like two different people. One adores a friend and cannot do without her. The other attacks her. What switches her from one to the other is a friend who seems about to leave: a reply a day late, or a month abroad.

She holds on hard, with messages, gifts and begging. When that does not seem to work, she attacks the one who is going, and then holds on again. It is not an act: people like this are often as frightened by the swings as everyone around them.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check the years and the people: from fifteen to thirty-one, with friends, partners and an employer.** It has to repeat with every close person, not just one.
2. **Find what sets it off: a friend who seems about to pull away.** It is not criticism, and it is not someone else being praised.
3. **Watch the swing: forty messages in two days, then “You are a fake and I never want to see you again”, then twelve apologies.** Clinging, attacking and clinging again is the whole sign.
4. **Look for the cost: three friends, a job and all four partners.** The swings keep costing her the people she is trying to keep.

This is **Borderline personality**. The word “borderline” is old and tells you nothing, so go by what the story shows.

You may also hear this called “borderline” or “BPD” or “borderline personality disorder”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Borderline personality**.

## Part 2 of 2: The center of attention, broken rules, then the drill

### 13. Histrionic personality

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 13 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-histrionic`]

The names so far were about needing to be special, or needing someone to stay. This one is about attention itself.

*The guest who is always at the center*

> Marguerite is forty-four and is the first to arrive at any gathering and the last to leave. ⟦She tells every story as if on a stage⟧, hugs people she met ten minutes ago, and has told three different friends that each of them is her very best friend. When a colleague was applauded after a presentation, she ⟦began a long story about her own terrible week, her voice shaking and her eyes filling, until the room turned back to her⟧. She has done this in every job she has had, at her sister's wedding and at her father's funeral. Her sister says ⟦she has stopped inviting her to small gatherings⟧.

Dennis needed to be above others. Marguerite does not need anyone below her. She needs to be looked at, and any attention will do as long as it is on her.

You see it best when the attention leaves her. A colleague is applauded, and Marguerite says nothing unkind. She puts on a bigger show until the room turns back. The feelings are usually real: big, and quick to change.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check the years and places: every job she has had, her sister’s wedding, her father’s funeral.** It happens even at events that are not about her.
2. **See how she acts: every story told as if on a stage, first to arrive and last to leave.** She puts herself at the center.
3. **Watch what she does when attention goes elsewhere: a colleague is applauded, and she tells the story of her terrible week until the room turns back.** Her show gets bigger, and she does not run anyone down.
4. **Look for the cost: her sister has stopped inviting her to small gatherings.** The show keeps wearing people out.

This is **Histrionic personality**. “Histrionic” comes from an old word for an actor, so it means theatrical.

You may also hear this called “histrionic personality disorder” or “attention-seeking”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Histrionic personality**.

### 14. A question about a new story

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 14 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `check`, id `check-histrionic`]

> Tilly is thirty-eight and sings in a community choir. She arrives in a different costume each week and tells everyone about her week at a volume that stops the rehearsal. When the choir applauded a soloist, Tilly clutched her chest and said she felt faint with emotion, and kept it up until the conductor stopped to ask if she was all right. ### 15. Borderline personality or Histrionic personality: telling them apart

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 15 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-borderline-histrionic`]

Both have big feelings, and big feelings are what people notice first. Here is what separates them.

**Story A**

> Mira is twenty-nine. At her roommate's leaving party she cried and clung to her, saying she would be nothing without her and that she must not go. When the roommate said she would visit on weekends, Mira said she knew she would forget her, and the next week told the other roommates that she had always been selfish. Mira has been through the same thing with every close friend who has gone away since she was sixteen, and has lost three of them for good.

**Story B**

> Orla is twenty-nine. At her roommate's leaving party she stood on a chair and gave a speech about how much she would miss her, and cried so that the whole room turned to watch. When another guest was given a round of applause for a song, Orla sang louder over the end of it. Orla has made herself the center of every leaving party, wedding and birthday since she was sixteen, and two friends have stopped inviting her to parties.

**What to compare.** In both stories a roommate is leaving. Compare one thing: who the display is for. Is it for the one who is leaving, or for the whole room?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Clings to people, and turns on them when they seem to be leaving”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story A.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A, Mira clings to her roommate, says she would be nothing without her, and the next week tells the others that she was selfish. All of it is aimed at one person who seems to be leaving, and it swings from adoring her to attacking her. That is **Borderline personality**.

In Story B, Orla stands on a chair and speaks to the whole room, and when another guest is applauded she sings louder over the end of it. The room is the audience, and she attacks no one. That is **Histrionic personality**.

The tears are the same. What differs is who the display is aimed at, and whether it turns into an attack on the person who seems to be leaving.

**How to tell them apart**

Who is the display for: one particular person who seems to be leaving, or whoever is in the room? And does it turn into an attack on that one person?

### 17. Antisocial personality

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 17 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-antisocial`]

The last one is the label people reach for fastest, as “psychopath”. It needs more than the everyday word does.

*The dealership owner*

> Callum is thirty-nine and owns a car dealership. He has ⟦sold three customers cars with the odometer rolled back⟧, and told each of them it was 'the best car on the lot'. He borrowed four thousand dollars from his brother-in-law and has never repaid it, saying 'he can afford it'. At his last dealership he forged his boss's signature on a lease, and at school he was expelled for forging notes from his mother. When a friend lost his savings in a scheme Callum had pushed him into, Callum shrugged: '⟦He should have read the paperwork⟧.' He has been fired twice, and ⟦his brother-in-law no longer speaks to him⟧.

The earlier names were about what a person needs from other people. Callum just wants something for himself, and he gets it by lying, using people and breaking whatever rule is in the way.

Look at what is missing: regret. A customer is cheated, a friend loses his savings, and Callum shrugs: “He should have read the paperwork.” And it has to repeat. One lie that someone felt terrible about is not this.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check the years and places: school, two garages and a family.** One lie, once, is not enough.
2. **Find the lies and broken rules: a rolled-back odometer, a forged signature, a loan never repaid.** He gets what he wants by using people.
3. **Listen for regret, and find none: “He should have read the paperwork.”** No regret for the harm is what separates this from an ordinary rule-bender.
4. **Look for who got hurt: customers, a friend’s savings, a brother-in-law who no longer speaks to him.** Someone always pays for it.

This is **Antisocial personality**. Here “antisocial” does not mean shy: it means against other people and the rules they live by together.

You may also hear this called “psychopath” or “sociopath” or “psychopathy” or “antisocial personality disorder”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Antisocial personality**.

### 21. The one question that sorts all six

**The question:** **“What do they keep doing, year after year?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other five.

- **“Acts superior, and gets angry when not treated as special”**
  - Give this answer when for years, at home and at work, the person acts better than others and owed special treatment, meets any slight with anger or scorn, and it keeps hurting someone.
  - It leads to **Grandiose narcissism**.
- **“Feels overlooked and owed more, and sulks when not treated as special”**

  - It leads to **Vulnerable narcissism**.
- **“Clings to people, and turns on them when they seem to be leaving”**
  - Give this answer when for years, at home and at work, the person clings to people and turns on them when they seem about to leave, and it keeps hurting someone.
  - It leads to **Borderline personality**.
- **“Needs to be the center of attention, and makes a bigger scene when they are not”**

Look at what they do again and again, wherever they are and whoever they are with, and whether it keeps hurting someone. One bad week, one relationship or one label someone gives them is not enough.

Two people can both be loud, both be quiet, or both be furious when crossed, and still get different names. How loud, how likeable or how much you dislike them tells you nothing. What they keep doing, and what it keeps costing, does.

2. **Find what they keep doing: how they act with others, what they do when something goes against them, what they do when someone seems about to leave.** That is where the six answers differ.

### 24. What to carry away

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 24 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap-pat`]

Here is the whole unit in one place.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What do they keep doing, year after year?
- Acts superior, and gets angry when not treated as special → Grandiose narcissism
- Feels overlooked and owed more, and sulks when not treated as special → Vulnerable narcissism
- Clings to people, and turns on them when they seem to be leaving → Borderline personality
- Needs to be the center of attention, and makes a bigger scene when they are not → Histrionic personality
- Breaks rules and uses people, with no regret → Antisocial personality
- Nothing that keeps hurting anyone → Just how they are

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Grandiose narcissism**: years of it, at home and at work: acting better than others and owed special treatment, anger or scorn when not treated as special, and harm to them or others.
- **Vulnerable narcissism**: years of it, at home and at work: feeling overlooked and owed more, hurt silence or quiet resentment when not treated as special, and harm to them or others.
- **Borderline personality**: years of it, at home and at work: clinging to people, swinging from adoring someone to attacking them when they seem about to leave, and harm to them or others.
- **Histrionic personality**: years of it, at home and at work: needing to be the center of attention, a bigger scene when attention goes to someone else, and harm to them or others.
- **Antisocial personality**: years of it, at home and at work: rules broken, people lied to or used for the person’s own gain, no regret, and people hurt by it.
- **Just how they are**: years of being the same way at home and at work, shy or blunt for example, and no repeated harm to them or the people around them.

**To carry away**

- Before any name, count: years, more than one place, more than one relationship. A week, or one other person, is not enough.
- Then ask what the person keeps doing, and find the words. For the first five names, find the cost too. No cost means **Just how they are**, the right answer for most people anyone describes.
- The two narcissisms both need to be treated as special. Outward, it is anger and scorn. Inward, it is hurt silence and resentment.
- When a story shows the scorn of **Grandiose narcissism** and also everything **Antisocial personality** needs, the answer is **Antisocial personality**.
- Five of the six names describe a *personality disorder*, as the word is used here. They describe what a story shows, not a person: only a professional can diagnose, after a long assessment.
