# Strategy: Goal, wish, or strategy?

Revision 1, draft (the screen carries the draft line). Part: Goal, wish, or strategy?. Where the subject ends up: After this you can look at a business decision (expand, add a product, chase a big customer, answer a rival, invest) and tell whether it is a strategy, say where the business makes its money and why, and pick the move that keeps it different in a way rivals cannot copy, when you face that decision in your own business.

## The why

Have an owner tell you their strategy and you often hear a number: “grow 20% this year.” That is where they want to be, not how they will get there.
People who teach strategy test a plan with one question first: does it say what is in the way? Here you learn to read any plan, your own included, for that line, and to tell a strategy from a goal or a to-do list.

## Step 1: The three parts of a strategy

A strategy has three parts, and you can find each one on the page.
The challenge: what is in the way, and why. “Since the supermarket opened its own bakery, our bread sales fell by a third: it sells a loaf for half our price.”
One approach to it: the one way past that challenge. “We stop selling bread on price and sell what the supermarket cannot make: cakes to order.”
Steps that follow from the approach: each one serves it. “Move a baker to cakes. Show the cakes in the window.”
Read a plan in that order. First look for the challenge.
No challenge named? Then a plan that is only a target or a wish (“$1 million in sales”, “the best gym in town”) is a goal. A plan that lists steps is a to-do list, even with a target on top.
A challenge named, but the steps go off in different directions and none of them gets past it? That is a to-do list too.
A challenge, one approach, and steps that serve it: that is a strategy. Why it matters: a target tells your team where to end up, but only the challenge tells them what to work on.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “My strategy is to grow sales 20% this year.”
The right line: That is a goal. It says where you want to be. A strategy starts from what stands in the way of the 20%, picks one way past it, and lists the steps that serve that way.

## Step 2: A number that fell is not yet a challenge

| A | B |
|---|---|
| “Sales fell 15% last year.” | “Sales fell 15% last year because our two biggest office customers now order lunch through a delivery app.” |

Compare: Which line says what is in the way? Only the second. The first is a number that fell; until a plan says what made it fall, there is nothing to work on.

## Step 3: A worked example

Your own note, written after an argument: "Dana’s plan for her bakery", Written in January.

> Since the supermarket opened its own bakery, our bread sales fell by a third: it sells a loaf for half our price.
> We cannot beat that price, so we stop selling everyday bread on price and sell what the supermarket cannot make: whole cakes to order, for birthdays and weddings.
> Move our morning bread baker over to cakes.
> Put cake photos and an order form on our website and in the window.
> Keep only the three loaves our regulars buy with their coffee.

The working, one step at a time:
1. Find what is in the way, and why.: The first line: a supermarket sells bread at half Dana’s price, and that is why her bread sales fell.
1. Find the one approach to it, then check the steps against it.: The second line: stop competing on price and sell what the supermarket cannot make. Each step serves it: a baker moved to cakes, cakes shown and ordered, the bread cut back. So it is a strategy.

Then the answer: Since the supermarket opened its own bakery, our bread sales fell by a third: it sells a loaf for half our price. / A strategy. The first line says what is in the way and why: a rival selling bread at half the price. The second gives one way past it, and every step after it serves that way, so it is a strategy.
