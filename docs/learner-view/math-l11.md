# Basic Math: What a loan costs

Revision 1, live. Part: What a loan costs. Where the subject ends up: Work out the number a situation needs (a total, a price per one, a scaled amount, a percent or the price before one, how much paint or soil to buy, how high a ladder reaches, what savings grow to or a loan costs) and tell whether it is sensible, when a receipt, a recipe, a sale tag, a room or an offer hands you some numbers and a question and nobody names the method.

## The why

An ad or a quote for a car loan or a “pay later” plan shows a payment and a rate. What the loan costs is in the papers you sign, in small print.
Here you work it out from the payment, to check the papers and to compare offers before you sign, and you read a yearly rate as a yearly rate.

## Step 1: What you pay in all, less what you borrowed

A loan costs what you pay in all, less what you borrowed. Borrow $20,000 for a car and pay $396.02 a month for 60 months: 396.02 × 60 − 20000 = $3,761.20 is what the loan costs.
The loan papers must print this: the total of payments ($23,761.20) and the finance charge ($3,761.20), the dollars the loan costs you. A quote or an ad does not, so work it out and compare.
Rough: the cost is a small difference of two big numbers, so round the payment only a little, to three figures: $396 × 60 = $23,760, less $20,000: about $3,760. Rounded to $400, it gives $4,000; a $473.65 payment rounded to $500 is $1,600 too much over 60 months.
A rate is for a year (the APR) unless it says otherwise. Charged each month, a month’s rate is about the APR ÷ 12: 24% a year is 2% a month. On $1,500 owed, a month’s interest is 2% of $1,500, $30.
“Buy now, pay later” in monthly payments is a loan too: the payments in all, less the price you could pay today.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “24% a year on $1,500 is $360 for the month.”
The right line: $360 is a whole year’s interest. A month is a twelfth of it: $30.

1. Say what is asked and what is given. Asked: what the loan costs beyond the $20,000 borrowed. Given: 60 payments of $396.02. The APR is already in the payment, and the first payment’s day does not matter.

1. Work it out exactly on the calculator, then look back. On the calculator: 396.02 × 60 − 20000 = shows $3,761.20. Look back: near the rough $3,760, and far less than the amount borrowed.

Then the answer: $3,760 / $3,761.20. 60 × $396.02 = $23,761.20 paid in all, less the $20,000 borrowed: $3,761.20. On the loan papers this is the finance charge.
