# Statistical Claims: Before you start

Revision 1, live. Part: none. Where the subject ends up: When a headline and its article, a chart, an ad or a post on your phone is about to make you believe, share or act on a number, you can say whether the number shows what it is used to claim, point to the line that decides it (or say what is missing), and say how big it really is in plain counts, such as from 3 in 1,000 to 2 in 1,000 over ten years.

## The why

Six claims, the way they arrive on your phone. Answer each as you would if you met it today.

## Step 1: How this goes

Six short claims: a headline, a post, an ad or a report. About six minutes.
For each, you say what it claims, tap the sentence with the number it rests on (or Not given), and say whether the text shows it: it holds up, it does not show it (the text itself shows the claim goes too far), or you cannot tell from this (something you would need is not in the text).
Nothing is marked as you go, and you will see your result at the end. Go with your first judgment; this only shows where you start.

## The baseline

Six things, the way they arrive on a phone. Do what you would really do. Nothing here is graded, and you see no answers until the end.
It is not passed or failed: no rule is shown, nothing is shown per question, and the questions come in mixed order. It is stored as tries in the context "baseline", never counted in the results by lesson or in the schedule of a topic, and taken once.

- "Researchers said students who stay up late may also work more hours or have less time to study." — if tapped: That is not the sentence.

**Does the text show what it claims?**

After the answer: the deciding words are marked. Reason: The headline says late nights “drag down” grades. The survey only compared two groups of students as they already were, and the researchers say the late sleepers may also work more hours or have less time to study, which could explain the gap.
What you would need to see: You would need students whose bedtime changed and nothing else, or two groups that were alike except for bedtime.
