## Part 1 of 2: An office running a law, and an order with no law behind it

### 1. Six things the President or a federal office can do

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient-pres`]

When the news says the President or a federal office “did something”, you can tell what it actually did. It is one of six things, and only one of them is the President reaching for a power that belongs to Congress.

You hear this almost every day. “The office published new rules.” “The President signed an order.” “The President sent the army to help.” “The President won’t sign the bill.” “The President pardoned him.” Each one is a different thing, and the word “government” in a headline hides which.

Unit One taught you to ask: **“Who makes the final call?”** When the answer is the President or a federal office, one question is left: **“What is the President or the agency doing?”** Five of the six answers are things the President or an office is allowed to do. The sixth is reaching for something only Congress can do: a new tax, a new crime, a ban.

A federal *agency* is also called a federal office, and the two words mean the same thing: a government office that runs certain laws day to day, such as the immigration service or the tax office.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“Who makes the final call?”** Its answers:

- **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**: give this answer when the final call is a vote in the House or Senate, or someone asks Congress for one, such as on a law or money. A law stays Congress’s call even once the President signs it.
- **“The President or a federal agency”**: give this answer when the final call is the President’s or a federal agency’s, or someone is asking them for one, such as an agency enforcing a law or the President refusing to sign one. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“A judge, in any court”**: give this answer when the final call is a judge’s, in any court, federal or state, or someone is asking a judge to decide.
- **“A state, city or county government”**: give this answer when the final call is made by a state’s lawmakers, governor or agencies, or a city, town or county, or someone asks them for it. A state court judge counts as a judge.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. Carrying out the law

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-execute`]

Start with the one you will meet most in the news: Congress passed a law, and an office has to make it work.

*The insulation credit*

> Last year Congress passed a law that gives a tax credit to anyone who adds insulation to their home. The law says the work must be real and must be done by a licensed builder. On Monday ⟦the federal tax agency published the form people must fill in to claim the credit, and the receipts they must keep⟧. Its staff will start checking the forms in the spring.

The law says what people get: a tax credit for real insulation work done by a licensed builder. It does not say which form to fill in or which receipts to keep, so the tax office decides that and publishes the form.

This is how most laws reach ordinary people. Congress writes the law in general words, and the federal offices do the daily work: the forms, the receipts, the checking. An office has no power of its own to give out tax credits. It gets that power from the law, so it can go only as far as the law allows.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the law Congress passed: the tax credit for insulation.** The office is working from it, not making things up.
2. **Find what the office does: it publishes the form and says which receipts to keep.** Forms, permits, inspections and collecting money are the daily work of a law.
3. **Check it stays inside the law: the form asks for receipts that show the work is real, as the law says.** An office may fill in details, but it may not add demands the law does not have.

This is **Carrying out the law**. The law already exists, and an office is making it work in daily life.

You may also hear this called “enforcing the law” or “implementing a law” or “executing the law”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Carrying out the law**.

### 4. A written instruction from the President to the offices

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-order`]

You will hear this word in headlines, and the next card depends on it.

*The one-page instruction*

> On Monday the President signed a one-page paper that tells every federal office to answer letters from the public within thirty days. No one voted on the paper. It asks nothing of anybody outside the government: it is addressed to the offices themselves.

The paper only tells the government’s own offices how to do their work. Nobody outside the government has to do anything because of it.

**The word for this.** *Executive order*: a written instruction from the President to the federal agencies about how to do their work. It is not a law.

Nobody in Congress voted on it, and the next President can cancel it by signing another. An *executive order* tells the offices how to carry out laws that already exist.

So when you read “the President signed an order”, ask what it demands, and of whom.


### 5. Beyond the President’s power

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 5 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-beyondpres`]

The last name was an office staying inside a law. This is what it looks like when there is no law to stay inside.

*The straw ban*

> On Monday the President signed an executive order that bans every company in the country from selling plastic straws. ⟦No law passed by Congress gives the President the power to ban a product⟧, and Congress has not voted on any such ban. Shops were told they must stop selling straws by March.

Shops must stop selling plastic straws by March, and no law Congress passed gives the President that power. A ban on selling a product is a new rule for everyone who sells it, and a new rule for everyone is what a law is. Only Congress makes laws.

The same goes for a new tax or fee, a new crime, or a new duty for people or businesses. An *executive order* cannot demand any of them on its own. It is weaker than a law: the next President can cancel it by signing another, and a judge can be asked to strike it down. With no law behind the order, the President has nothing to carry out.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the demand on people outside the government: stop selling plastic straws by March.** An order that only tells the offices what to do is a different thing.
2. **Check what it demands: a ban, which is a new rule for every company that sells straws.** A new tax, fee, crime, duty or ban is something only a law can create.
3. **Look for a law Congress passed that allows it: the story says there is none.** With no law behind it, there is nothing for the President to carry out.

This is **Beyond the President’s power**. The demand goes past what the President can do alone.

You may also hear this called “exceeding the President’s powers”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Beyond the President’s power**.

### 7. An order that only carries out a law

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `exception`, id `exc-order`]

An order from the President can look like **Beyond the President’s power**: it has the President’s signature, and nobody voted on it. This story shows an order that is the opposite.

*The sixty-day order*

> Congress passed a law last year that lets families apply for a federal grant to repair storm damage. On Monday the President signed an executive order telling the federal housing agency to answer every grant application within sixty days, and to publish each month how many it has approved. The order asks nothing of families. It only tells the office how to run the law.

The President signed an *executive order* that nobody voted on, and that is often what **Beyond the President’s power** looks like. Yet this story is **Carrying out the law**.

**You are asked:** This looks like **Beyond the President’s power**. Before you read why it is **Carrying out the law**, tap the words in the story that settle it.

The pieces you can tap:
1. “Congress passed a law last year that lets families apply for a federal grant to repair storm damage”
2. “the President signed an executive order telling the federal housing agency to answer every grant application within sixty days”
3. “The order asks nothing of families. It only tells the office how to run the law”

**Shown as soon as you tap.** The words are “The order asks nothing of families. It only tells the office how to run the law”.
- If you tapped “Congress passed a law last year that lets families apply for a federal grant to repair storm damage”: That is the law, and it is why the order exists. The words that decide it are in the order itself.
- If you tapped “the President signed an executive order telling the federal housing agency to answer every grant application within sixty days”: That is the part that looks suspicious: an order from the President that nobody voted on. What settles it is what the order asks of people.

**Why this is Carrying out the law and not Beyond the President’s power**

Ask what the order demands, and of whom. It tells a federal office to answer grant applications within sixty days and to publish how many it approved. The only people it commands are the office’s own staff.

A law is behind it too: the one Congress passed that lets families apply for a grant. The order adds nothing to what that law gives or demands. It just tells the office how to run it.

**How to tell them apart**

Can you name a law Congress passed that allows what the rule or order demands, and does it stay inside that law?

In the news both kinds are called an *executive order*. The word does not settle it. What the order demands, and of whom, does.


### 8. Carrying out the law or Beyond the President’s power: telling them apart

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 8 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-execute-beyondpres`]

Both can be a rule from a federal office, with a law somewhere in the story. The test is whether the law allows what the office demands.

**Story A**

> Congress passed a law that says every packaged snack must show how much salt it holds. On Thursday the federal food agency published how large the salt line on the package must be, and the date from which every package must carry it.

**Story B**

> Congress has passed no law about how much salt a snack may hold. On Thursday the federal food agency published a rule that no packaged snack may hold more than a set amount of salt, and that any firm selling one will be fined.

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the same federal food office and the salt in packaged snacks. Compare one thing: is there a law Congress passed behind what the office does, and does the office stay inside it?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Demands something of people that no law allows”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A a law exists: Congress passed one that says every snack must show how much salt it holds. The office only decides the details of the label. It adds no demand of its own. That is **Carrying out the law**.

In Story B no law exists: Congress has passed none on how much salt a snack may hold. The office sets a limit and a fine for every firm that sells snacks, with nothing behind them. That is **Beyond the President’s power**.

A law can be in both stories. Say a law sets a camping permit at $15 a night and an office announces $40: the law is only the line the office crossed, and that is **Beyond the President’s power**. Finding a law is not enough. Check that what the office demands is inside it.

**How to tell them apart**

Can you name a law Congress passed that allows what the rule or order demands, and does it stay inside that law?

## Part 2 of 2: What only the President can do, and the question to ask

### 10. Commander in chief

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 10 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-commander`]

The first two were about a law. The next four are things the President does with no law behind them. The first is an order to the army, the navy or the air force.

*The flood relief*

> A flood has cut three mountain towns off from the rest of the country. On Tuesday ⟦the President ordered the army to send twelve helicopters and two thousand soldiers to the towns⟧ to carry in food and clear the roads. The first helicopters left within the hour.

The President is the head of the armed forces. One order sent twelve helicopters and two thousand soldiers to the flooded towns within the hour, with no vote and no law needed first. The President decides where the forces go and what they do, and picks who leads them.

There is one limit, and stories about armies are often about it. Only Congress can declare war, and Congress votes the money that pays for the forces. So when you hear that the President “declared war”, check whose decision the story ends on.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the order the President gives: send twelve helicopters and two thousand soldiers to the towns.** An order is an instruction that must be obeyed, not a request.
2. **Find who receives it: the army.** The name fits only when the order goes to the armed forces.
3. **Check nobody else has to agree first: the helicopters left within the hour.** No vote and no law has to come before the order.

This is **Commander in chief**. The President gives the armed forces their orders, and they obey.

You may also hear this called “command of the armed forces”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Commander in chief**.

### 12. Dealing with other countries

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 12 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-diplomacy`]

The next one is not about the armed forces at all. It is about speaking for the country to another country.

*The coast talks*

> The President flew to the capital of a neighboring country on Monday and ⟦spent two days talking with its leader about fishing rules along the shared coast⟧. By Wednesday the two leaders had agreed on a set of rules and had signed a paper saying so.

The President flew to a neighboring country, talked with its leader for two days about fishing rules on the shared coast, and signed a paper with the rules they agreed on. A country needs one voice at the table, or the other side cannot know who has agreed to what. That voice is the President’s.

The President does not have to go in person. Someone sent to speak for the President, such as the Secretary of State, does the same job. A *treaty* takes effect only when two-thirds of the senators present vote to approve it. That vote is a different decision, by different people, and this story stops before it.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the other country’s government: the neighboring country’s leader.** Dealing with another country needs the other country at the table.
2. **Find who speaks for our country: the President, who flew there.** Someone the President sends, such as the Secretary of State, counts too.
3. **Find what they do together: talk for two days and sign a paper on fishing rules.** Meeting, bargaining and signing are the whole job.

This is **Dealing with other countries**. The President speaks for the country to another country.

You may also hear this called “foreign affairs” or “diplomacy” or “negotiating a treaty”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Dealing with other countries**.

Both can involve ships, soldiers and another country. The test is what the President does.

**Story A**

> The President ordered the navy to send three ships to the port of Istrene, in a friendly country, and to keep them there for a month. The first ship sailed on Tuesday morning.

**Story B**

> The President traveled to Istrene and spent two days with its leader. On Thursday the two of them signed an agreement that navy ships of each country may use the other’s ports.

Ships and a port appear in both. What differs is what the President does: tell the forces what to do, or settle something with another country.

**How to tell them apart**

### 15. Veto

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 15 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-veto`]

The last two are what the President can do when others have already acted. First: Congress passed a bill, and the President does not want it.

*The park pay rise*

> Congress passed a bill that raises the pay of everyone who works for the national parks by four percent. The bill reached the President’s desk on Monday. On Wednesday the President wrote that the country cannot afford the rise, ⟦would not sign the bill, and sent it back to Congress with a letter of objections⟧.

A bill that both the House and the Senate passed goes to the President. If the President signs it, it becomes a law. This President did not sign: a letter says why, and the bill goes back to Congress. That refusal is the whole of this name.

It does not kill the bill for good. Congress can pass it anyway if two-thirds of the House and two-thirds of the Senate vote for it again, but that is a new decision by lawmakers. And doing nothing is not a refusal: if the President neither signs nor returns the bill within ten days, Sundays not counted, while Congress is in session, it becomes a law without a signature.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the bill Congress passed: the pay rise for park staff.** This acts on a bill, not on a law already in force.
2. **Find the President refusing to sign it: “would not sign the bill”.** Saying nothing for ten days lets the bill become a law.
3. **Check the bill goes back to Congress: with a letter of objections.** Sending it back is what makes the refusal real.

This is **Veto**. The President refuses to sign a bill and sends it back to Congress.

You may also hear this called “vetoing a bill”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Veto**.

### 17. Pardon

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 17 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-pardon`]

The last one is also something the President can do alone, and it is about a person, not a bill.

*The false tax forms*

> A man was found guilty in a federal court of mailing false tax forms, and the judge sentenced him to two years in prison. After six months ⟦the President signed a pardon for him⟧. The man left the prison that evening.

The President can forgive a person for a federal crime: a crime against a law of the whole country, tried in a federal court. The punishment is lifted, or never comes if the person has not been tried yet. The President decides alone, and no judge is involved. It does not say the man was innocent: he stays a person who was found guilty.

It covers federal crimes only. A person convicted under a state’s own law cannot ask the President, because only that state’s governor can forgive it. So when a story says someone was pardoned, first ask which law they broke.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the crime, and check it is federal: false tax forms, found guilty in a federal court.** Only the governor can forgive a crime against a state’s own law.
2. **Find the person who broke the law: the man in prison.** This acts on a person, not on a bill.
3. **Find the President forgiving it: the President signs a paper, and he leaves prison that evening.** The punishment is lifted, and no judge is asked anything.

This is **Pardon**. The President forgives a federal crime.

You may also hear this called “a presidential pardon”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Pardon**.

### 19. The one question to ask about the President or an office

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 19 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-pres`]

Here is the question and its six answers in one place.

**The question:** **“What is the President or the agency doing?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other five.

- **“Carries out a law Congress passed”**
  - Give this answer when a federal agency or the President writes the detailed rules for a law Congress passed, or inspects or enforces it, and stays inside what that law allows.
  - It leads to **Carrying out the law**.
- **“Demands something of people that no law allows”**
  - Give this answer when the President or a federal agency, by an order or a rule, demands something of people, like a new tax or a ban, that no law Congress passed allows.
  - It leads to **Beyond the President’s power**.
- **“Gives orders to the armed forces”**
  - Give this answer when the President tells the armed forces where to go or what to do, or chooses who leads them.
  - It leads to **Commander in chief**.
- **“Deals with another country”**
  - Give this answer when the President, or an official sent by the President, meets, negotiates with or signs an agreement with another country.
  - It leads to **Dealing with other countries**.
- **“Refuses to sign a law Congress passed”**
  - Give this answer when Congress has passed a law and the President refuses to sign it and sends it back. Two-thirds of the House and of the Senate can still make it law.
  - It leads to **Veto**.
- **“Forgives a federal crime”**
  - Give this answer when the President forgives someone for a federal crime, so the punishment is lifted. Only a state can forgive a crime against its own law.
  - It leads to **Pardon**.

**Why it matters**

Each of the six names is one thing the President or an agency does, and for an order or a rule, whether a law Congress passed allows it. The President carries out the laws but does not make them, so an order that demands something no law allows goes beyond the President’s power, however it is worded.

Each name has a different limit, so the right name tells you what to check. An office carrying out a law may go only as far as the law allows. The other four are the President’s own powers, and no law has to stand behind them. Mix them up and you check the wrong limit.

**How to answer it**

1. **Read to the end, then find the sentence where the President or the office does something: an order, a rule, a meeting, a refusal, a signed paper.** It is often the last sentence.
2. **Ask if a law Congress passed stands behind it, and the office stays inside it: Carrying out the law.** The office is only making the law work.
3. **Ask if a demand on people outside the government has no law behind it: Beyond the President’s power.** Only a law can create a new tax, fee, crime, duty or ban.
4. **Look for orders to soldiers, ships or planes: Commander in chief.** The forces are told where to go or what to do.
5. **Look for the President, or someone the President sent, talking with another country’s government: Dealing with other countries.** They sit across the table from another country.
6. **Look for a bill Congress passed that the President will not sign: Veto.** The bill goes back before it can become a law.
7. **Look for a person who broke a federal law and is forgiven: Pardon.** The punishment is lifted, or never comes.
8. **If the story ends on a vote in Congress, it is from Unit Three: the vote that passes a law is Within Congress’s power, and senators voting on the President’s pick or a *treaty* is Senate approval.** In this unit the last decision belongs to the President or an office.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Sometimes two answers both seem to fit. Each pair below has one question that separates it.

- Carrying out the law or Beyond the President’s power: Can you name a law Congress passed that allows what the rule or order demands, and does it stay inside that law?
- Commander in chief or Dealing with other countries: Who is on the other side: the armed forces, who are told what to do, or another country’s leaders, who are met and bargained with?
- Veto or Pardon: What is the President acting on: a bill Congress passed, or a person who broke a federal law?
- Veto or Carrying out the law: Is the President deciding whether a bill takes effect, or is an office already putting a law into practice?
- Dealing with other countries or Carrying out the law: Is the official dealing with another country’s government, or with people under a law Congress passed?

### 22. What to carry away

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 22 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap-pres`]

You can now sort what the President or an office does on your own.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What is the President or the agency doing?
- Carries out a law Congress passed → Carrying out the law
- Demands something of people that no law allows → Beyond the President’s power
- Gives orders to the armed forces → Commander in chief
- Deals with another country → Dealing with other countries
- Refuses to sign a law Congress passed → Veto
- Forgives a federal crime → Pardon

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Carrying out the law**: a law Congress passed, and a federal agency or the President doing the work it sets out, like writing the detailed rules or enforcing them, within what the law allows.
- **Beyond the President’s power**: an executive order or a rule from the President or a federal agency, something it demands of people or businesses, and no law Congress passed that allows it.
- **Commander in chief**: an order from the President to the armed forces: where to go, what to do, or who is in charge.
- **Dealing with other countries**: the President, or an official sent by the President, meeting, negotiating or signing an agreement with another country.
- **Veto**: a law Congress has passed, and the President refusing to sign it and sending it back with objections.
- **Pardon**: a federal crime someone was charged with or convicted of, and the President forgiving it, so the punishment is lifted or never comes.

**To carry away**

- Say what the President or the office did, and find the words that show it. If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.
- A rule or an order that demands something of people outside the government needs a law Congress passed behind it. With one, and staying inside it, it is **Carrying out the law**. Without one, it is **Beyond the President’s power**. An *executive order* that only tells the offices how to do their work is **Carrying out the law**.
- The other four are the President’s own powers, and no law has to stand behind them: **Commander in chief**, **Dealing with other countries**, **Veto** and **Pardon**. A visit from another country’s leader does not make a story **Dealing with other countries**, and soldiers in a story do not make it **Commander in chief**. What the President does decides.
- Only Congress can declare war, and the President cannot forgive a crime against a state’s own law.
