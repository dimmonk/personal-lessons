## Part 1 of 2: The President in charge of the laws, and what it looks like when no law is behind a demand

### 1. Six things the President or a federal office can do

*Unit Four · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient-pres`]

After this unit you can read a short news item or an everyday story in which the President or a federal office makes the last decision, and say which of six things it is: an agency putting a law into practice; an order that demands what no law allows; the President giving the armed forces orders; the President dealing with another country; the President sending a law back unsigned; or the President forgiving a federal crime.

You already hear about this part of government almost every day. 'The office has published new rules.' 'The President signed an order.' 'The President sent the army to help.' 'The President will not sign the bill.' 'The President has pardoned him.' Each of these is a different thing, and the word 'government' in a headline hides which one.

Unit One taught you to ask whose decision a story ends on. When the answer is the President or a federal office, one question is left: what does the President or the office do? Five of the six answers are things the President or an office is allowed to do. The sixth is reaching for something that only Congress can do: a new tax, a new crime, a ban.

One word is used in two ways in this unit, so here it is once. A federal *agency* is also called a federal office, and the two words mean the same thing: an office of a government that carries out particular laws day to day, such as the immigration service or the tax office.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“Who makes the last decision in the case, or is asked to make it?”** Its answers:

- **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**: give this answer when the last decision in the case is a vote in the House, the Senate or both, or the case ends by asking Congress for one: on a law, on money, on a person the President chose or an agreement the President signed with another country, or on charging or trying an official. A law Congress passed is still Congress’s decision when the case adds that the President signed it.
- **“The President or a federal agency”**: give this answer when the last decision in the case is made by the President or a federal agency, or the case ends by asking them for one: an agency writes rules, inspects or enforces, the President gives an order, commands the armed forces, deals with another country, refuses to sign a law or forgives a federal crime. **This unit is about these cases.**
- **“A judge, in any court”**: give this answer when the last decision in the case is a judge’s, in any court, federal or state, or the case ends with someone asking a judge to decide.
- **“A state, city or county government”**: give this answer when the last decision in the case is made by a state’s lawmakers, its governor or its agencies, or by a city, town or county, or the case ends by asking one of them for it. A judge in a state’s court counts as a judge, not as the state.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a case, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. An agency putting a law into practice

*Unit Four · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-execute`]

Start with the plainest of the six, and the one you meet most often in the news: a law that Congress has already passed, and an office that has to make it work.

*The insulation credit*

> Last year Congress passed a law that gives a tax credit to anyone who adds insulation to their home. The law says the work must be real and must be done by a licensed builder. On Monday ⟦the federal tax agency published the form people must fill in to claim the credit, and the receipts they must keep⟧. Its staff will start checking the forms in the spring.

- Congress passed a law that gives a tax credit for adding insulation to a home. It says the work must be real and must be done by a licensed builder.
- A federal office then decides what the law leaves open: which form people fill in, and which receipts they keep. It adds nothing the law does not allow.
- Nobody votes in the case, and no judge appears. The last thing in it is the office publishing the form.

This is how most laws reach ordinary people. Congress writes a law in general words. It does not write the form, collect the receipts or check each claim for millions of people, so the federal offices do that daily work, and the President leads them.

An office has no power of its own to give out tax credits or to ask for receipts. It borrows both from the law Congress passed, which is why it can go only as far as that law allows. Here it stays inside: the law said the work must be real, and the form asks for receipts that show it is.

**What you must be able to point to.** A law Congress passed, and a federal agency or the President putting it into practice (writing its detailed rules, processing, inspecting, collecting, enforcing) without going past what it allows.

The name for this is **Carrying out the law**. It means what it says: the law already exists, and someone is making it work in daily life.

You may also hear this called “enforcing the law” or “implementing a law” or “executing the law”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Carrying out the law**.

### 4. A written instruction from the President to the offices

*Unit Four · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-order`]

The next name leans on a word that is easy to pass over.

*The one-page instruction*

> On Monday the President signed a one-page paper that tells every federal office to answer letters from the public within thirty days. No one voted on the paper. It asks nothing of anybody outside the government: it is addressed to the offices themselves.

The paper is a set of instructions from the President to the federal offices, and nobody outside the government has to do anything because of it.

**The word for this.** *Executive order*: a written instruction from the President to the federal agencies about how to do their work. It is not a law.

It is not a law, because nobody in Congress voted on it, and the next President can undo it by signing another. An *executive order* tells the offices how to carry out the laws that already exist.


### 5. An order that demands what no law allows

*Unit Four · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 5 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-beyondpres`]

The last name was an office staying inside a law. The next is what it looks like when there is no law to stay inside. It begins with the kind of paper you have just met.

*The straw ban*

> On Monday the President signed an executive order that bans every company in the country from selling plastic straws. ⟦No law passed by Congress gives the President the power to ban a product⟧, and Congress has not voted on any such ban. Shops were told they must stop selling straws by March.

- The President signed an *executive order*, a written instruction.
- It does not only tell the offices how to work. It demands something of companies outside the government: stop selling a product, by March. The case says that no law of Congress gives the President that power.
- Nobody votes in the case. The last thing in it is the order itself.

Congress makes the laws. The President leads the offices that carry them out, and gives them orders. A ban on selling a product is a new rule for everyone who sells it, and a new rule for everyone is what a law is. So a ban of that kind has to come from a law that Congress passed.

The same is true of a new tax or fee, a new crime, or a new duty for people or businesses. An *executive order* cannot ask any of them on its own, and it is not as strong as a law: the next President can undo it by signing another, and a judge can be asked to strike it down. Here there is no law behind the order, so there is nothing for the President to carry out.

**What you must be able to point to.** An executive order or a rule from the President or a federal agency, something it demands of people or businesses, and no law Congress passed that allows it.

The name for this is **Beyond the President’s power**. "Beyond" because the demand goes past what the President can do alone, and "power" because power is the word for what a part of government is allowed to do.

You may also hear this called “exceeding the President’s powers”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Beyond the President’s power**.

### 7. An order that only carries out a law

*Unit Four · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `exception`, id `exc-order`]

A written order from the President can look like **Beyond the President’s power**: it has the President’s signature, and nobody voted on it. This card shows a case in which an order is exactly the opposite.

*The sixty-day order*

> Congress passed a law last year that lets families apply for a federal grant to repair storm damage. On Monday the President signed an executive order telling the federal housing agency to answer every grant application within sixty days, and to publish each month how many it has approved. The order asks nothing of families. It only tells the office how to run the law.

The President has signed an *executive order* that nobody voted on, and that is what **Beyond the President’s power** often looks like. Yet this case is **Carrying out the law**.

**You are asked:** This looks like **Beyond the President’s power**. Before you read why it is **Carrying out the law**, tap the words in the case that settle it.

1. “Congress passed a law last year that lets families apply for a federal grant to repair storm damage”
2. “the President signed an executive order telling the federal housing agency to answer every grant application within sixty days”

- If you tapped “the President signed an executive order telling the federal housing agency to answer every grant application within sixty days”: That is the part that looks like the second name: an order from the President, with no vote. What settles it is what the order asks of people.

**Why this is Carrying out the law and not Beyond the President’s power**

Ask what the order demands, and of whom. It tells a federal office to answer applications within sixty days and to publish how many it has approved. It asks nothing of families or companies: the only people it commands are the office’s own staff.

A law is behind it, too: the one Congress passed that lets families apply for a grant. The order adds nothing to what that law gives or demands. It tells the office how to run it.

### 8. Carrying out the law or Beyond the President’s power: telling them apart

*Unit Four · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 8 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-execute-beyondpres`]

You have met both names on their own. They are easy to mix up, because both can be a rule from a federal office, and both can have a law somewhere in the story. This card puts them side by side.

**Case A**

> Congress passed a law that says every packaged snack must show how much salt it holds. On Thursday the federal food agency published how large the salt line on the package must be, and the date from which every package must carry it.

**Case B**

> Congress has passed no law about how much salt a snack may hold. On Thursday the federal food agency published a rule that no packaged snack may hold more than a set amount of salt, and that any firm selling one will be fined.

**What to compare.** Both cases are about the same federal food office and the salt in packaged snacks. Compare one thing: is there a law Congress passed behind what the office does, and does the office stay inside it?

**You are asked:** Which case gives the answer **“Demands something of people that no law allows”**? (Case A / Case B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Case B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Case A a law exists: Congress passed one that says every snack must show how much salt it holds. The office decides the details of the label. It adds no new demand, and only fills in how the law is followed. The answer is **“Puts a law Congress passed into practice”**, and the case is **Carrying out the law**.

In Case B no law exists: the case says Congress has passed none on how much salt a snack may hold. The office sets a limit and a fine, which are demands on every firm that sells snacks, with nothing behind them. The answer is **“Demands something of people that no law allows”**, and the case is **Beyond the President’s power**.

A law can be in both cases. If an office goes past what the law allows, such as a law that sets a camping permit at $15 a night and an office that announces $40, the law is only the line the office has crossed, and the case is **Beyond the President’s power**. Do not stop at the law: check whether what the office demands is inside it.

**How to tell them apart**

Can you name a law Congress passed that allows what the rule or the order demands, and does the rule or order stay inside it?

## Part 2 of 2: The President’s own powers, and the question

### 10. The President giving the armed forces orders

*Unit Four · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 10 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-commander`]

The first two names were about a law, and whether one stands behind a rule. The next four are things the President does that need no law behind them. The first is an order to the army, the navy or the air force.

*The flood relief*

> A flood has cut three mountain towns off from the rest of the country. On Tuesday ⟦the President ordered the army to send twelve helicopters and two thousand soldiers to the towns⟧ to carry in food and clear the roads. The first helicopters left within the hour.

- A flood has cut three towns off.
- The President gives an order: send helicopters and two thousand soldiers to carry in food and clear the roads. The army carries it out within the hour.
- No law is named, no company is told to do anything, and nobody votes.

The President is the head of the armed forces: the army, the navy, the air force and the others. One order from the President can send soldiers and helicopters at once, with no vote beforehand. The President decides where the forces go and what they do, and picks who leads them. Unlike an office carrying out a law, the President needs no law behind the order.

There is one limit, and stories about armies are often about it. Only Congress can declare war, and Congress votes the money that pays for the forces. So the President commands the forces, and does not start a war or pay for one. When you hear that the President "declared war", check whose decision the story ends on.

**What you must be able to point to.** The President giving orders to the armed forces: where they go, what they do, or who leads them.

The name for this is **Commander in chief**. A "commander" is someone who gives orders, and "in chief" means the highest: the President is the highest commander of the armed forces.

You may also hear this called “command of the armed forces”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Commander in chief**.

### 12. The President dealing with another country

*Unit Four · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 12 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-diplomacy`]

The second of the President’s own powers is not about the armed forces at all. It is about speaking for the country to another country.

*The coast talks*

> The President flew to the capital of a neighboring country on Monday and ⟦spent two days talking with its leader about fishing rules along the shared coast⟧. By Wednesday the two leaders had agreed on a set of rules and had signed a paper saying so.

- The President traveled to another country and met its leader.
- They talked for two days about fishing along a shared coast, reached an agreement and signed a paper that says so.
- No law is named, nobody at home is ordered anywhere, and nobody outside the two governments is asked to do anything.

The President speaks for the country when it deals with other countries: receives other countries’ leaders, chooses the country’s ambassadors and negotiates agreements. A country needs one voice at the table, or the other side cannot know who has agreed to what.

The President does not have to do it in person. Someone sent to speak for the President, such as the Secretary of State, deals with the other country in the same way. And a *treaty* does not bind the country until two-thirds of the senators present vote to approve it. That vote is a different decision, by different people. This case stops before it.

**What you must be able to point to.** The President, or an official acting for the President, dealing with another country: meeting, negotiating or signing an agreement.

The name for this is **Foreign affairs**. "Foreign" means belonging to another country, and "affairs" means the things a government has to see to. So the name means the business of dealing with other countries.

You may also hear this called “diplomacy” or “negotiating a treaty”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Foreign affairs**.

### 14. Commander in chief or Foreign affairs: telling them apart

*Unit Four · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 14 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-commander-diplomacy`]

These two are easy to mix up, because both can be about ships, soldiers and another country. This card puts them side by side.

**Case A**

> The President ordered the navy to send three ships to the port of Istrene, in a friendly country, and to keep them there for a month. The first ship sailed on Tuesday morning.

**Case B**

> The President traveled to Istrene and spent two days with its leader. On Thursday the two of them signed an agreement that navy ships of each country may use the other’s ports.

**What to compare.** Both cases are about navy ships and the port of Istrene. Compare one thing: does the President tell the navy what to do, or settle something with Istrene’s leader?

**You are asked:** Which case gives the answer **“Deals with another country”**? (Case A / Case B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Case B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Case B the President goes to Istrene and spends two days with its leader, and the two of them sign an agreement about each country’s ships using the other’s ports. Ships, ports and another country appear in both. What differs is what the President does: tell the forces what to do, or settle something with another country.

**How to tell them apart**

Who is on the other side of what the President does: the armed forces, who are told where to go or what to do, or the leaders and officials of another country, who are met and negotiated with?

### 15. The President sending a law back unsigned

*Unit Four · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 15 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-veto`]

The last two names are about what the President can do when others have already acted. The first is what the President can do when Congress has passed a bill that the President does not want.

*The park pay rise*

> Congress passed a bill that raises the pay of everyone who works for the national parks by four percent. The bill reached the President’s desk on Monday. On Wednesday the President wrote that the country cannot afford the rise, ⟦would not sign the bill, and sent it back to Congress with a letter of objections⟧.

- Congress passed a bill that raises the pay of park staff. The House and the Senate both voted for it, and it reached the President’s desk.
- The President did not sign it. A letter says why, and the bill goes back to Congress.
- Nobody votes after that. The last thing in the case is what the President decided about the bill.

A bill that both the House and the Senate have passed goes to the President. If the President signs it, it becomes a law. But the President does not have to sign. The President may refuse, and send the bill back to Congress with a letter of objections. That is the whole of this name.

Refusing does not end the bill for ever. Congress can pass it anyway, if two-thirds of the House and two-thirds of the Senate vote for it again, but that vote would be a new decision by lawmakers, and a different case. And doing nothing is not a refusal: if the President neither signs the bill nor sends it back within ten days, Sundays not counted, while Congress is in session, it becomes a law without a signature.

**What you must be able to point to.** A law Congress has passed, and the President refusing to sign it and sending it back with objections.

The name for this is **Veto**: the President’s refusal to sign a bill, and the return of the bill to Congress.

You may also hear this called “vetoing a bill”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Veto**.

### 17. The President forgiving a federal crime

*Unit Four · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 17 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-pardon`]

The last of the six is another thing the President can do alone, and it is about a person, not a law.

*The false tax forms*

> A man was found guilty in a federal court of mailing false tax forms, and the judge sentenced him to two years in prison. After six months ⟦the President signed a pardon for him⟧. The man left the prison that evening.

- A man was found guilty in a federal court of mailing false tax forms, and the judge gave him a sentence.
- The President signed a paper that forgives him, and he left the prison that evening.
- No law is being put into practice, and no judge is asked anything after that. The last thing in the case is what the President did.

The President can forgive a person for a federal crime: a crime against a law of the whole country, tried in a federal court. The punishment is lifted, or it never comes, if the person has not yet been tried. It is the President’s own decision, and no judge is involved. It does not say the man was innocent: he stays a person who was found guilty.

It reaches federal crimes only. A person convicted under a state’s own law cannot ask the President. Only that state can forgive the crime, through its governor. So when a case says that someone was pardoned, ask first which law the person broke.

**What you must be able to point to.** A federal crime someone was charged with or convicted of, and the President forgiving it so that the punishment is lifted or never comes.

The name for this is **Pardon**: the President’s forgiveness of a federal crime.

You may also hear this called “a presidential pardon”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Pardon**.

### 19. The question you have been answering all along

*Unit Four · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 19 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-pres`]

Since the insulation credit you have seen the question at the foot of each new name, with one answer under it. This card puts the question and its six answers in one place, and says why it is asked.

**The question:** **“What does the President or the agency do?”**

**What it is for.** Tells apart six things the President and the federal agencies do: carry out a law, demand what no law allows, command the armed forces, deal with other countries, send a law back unsigned, and forgive a federal crime.

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other five.

- **“Puts a law Congress passed into practice”**
  - Give this answer when a federal agency, or the President, writes the detailed rules for a law Congress passed, processes applications under it, inspects, collects or enforces, and stays inside what that law allows.
  - It leads to **Carrying out the law**.
- **“Demands something of people that no law allows”**
  - Give this answer when the President or a federal agency, by an executive order or a rule, demands something of people or businesses (a new tax or fee, a new crime, a new duty, a ban) that no law Congress passed allows.
  - It leads to **Beyond the President’s power**.
- **“Gives orders to the armed forces”**
  - Give this answer when the President tells the armed forces where to go or what to do, or chooses who leads them.
  - It leads to **Commander in chief**.
- **“Deals with another country”**
  - Give this answer when the President, or an official acting for the President, meets, negotiates with or signs an agreement with another country.
  - It leads to **Foreign affairs**.
- **“Refuses to sign a law Congress passed”**
  - Give this answer when Congress has passed a law and sent it to the President, who refuses to sign it and sends it back with objections. It can still take effect if two-thirds of the House and two-thirds of the Senate vote for it again.
  - It leads to **Veto**.
- **“Forgives a federal crime”**
  - Give this answer when the President forgives someone for a federal crime, so that the punishment is lifted or never comes. A crime against a state’s own law is outside this, and only that state can forgive it.
  - It leads to **Pardon**.

**Why it decides**

The six names are defined by the act itself, and for an order or a rule by whether a law Congress passed allows it. The President carries out the laws but does not make them, so an order that demands something no law allows is beyond the President, however it is worded.

Each of the six is held back by a different limit, so naming the right one tells you what to look for. An office putting a law into practice may go only as far as the law allows. A rule or an order that demands something of people outside the government needs a law Congress passed behind it, and without one it is beyond what the President can do. The other four are the President’s own powers, and no law has to stand behind them. Mistaking one for another sends you to the wrong limit.

**How to answer it from a case**

Read the whole case. Then find the sentence in which the President or the office does something: an order, a rule, a meeting, a refusal, a signed paper. It is often the last sentence. Compare it with the six answers: **“Puts a law Congress passed into practice”**, **“Demands something of people that no law allows”**, **“Gives orders to the armed forces”**, **“Deals with another country”**, **“Refuses to sign a law Congress passed”** and **“Forgives a federal crime”**.

Two of the answers turn on a law, so look for the law in the words of the case. **Carrying out the law** has a law Congress passed behind it, and the office stays inside it. **Beyond the President’s power** has none behind what it demands of people outside the government.

The other four need no law behind them, and a few are easy to mix up. **Veto** acts on a bill that Congress passed, and **Pardon** on a person who broke a federal law. **Veto** comes before a law takes effect, where **Carrying out the law** works on a law already in force. **Foreign affairs** sits across the table from another country’s government, where **Carrying out the law** works on people under a law.

Two names from Unit Three sit nearest to these. **Enumerated power** ends on the lawmakers’ vote that passes a law, where **Carrying out the law** comes after the law has passed. **Advice and consent** ends on the senators’ vote, where **Foreign affairs** ends on the President’s side.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Sometimes two answers both seem to fit. Each pair below has one question that separates it.

- Carrying out the law or Beyond the President’s power: Can you name a law Congress passed that allows what the rule or the order demands, and does the rule or order stay inside it?
- Commander in chief or Foreign affairs: Who is on the other side of what the President does: the armed forces, who are told where to go or what to do, or the leaders and officials of another country, who are met and negotiated with?
- Veto or Pardon: What is the President acting on: a bill that Congress passed, or a person who broke a federal law?
- Veto or Carrying out the law: Is the President deciding whether the bill will take effect, or is an office already putting a law into practice?
- Foreign affairs or Carrying out the law: Is the official dealing with another country’s government, or dealing with people under a law Congress passed?

### 22. What to carry away

*Unit Four · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 22 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap-pres`]

You have now run the questions on your own. This card puts the unit in one place.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What does the President or the agency do?
- Puts a law Congress passed into practice → Carrying out the law
- Demands something of people that no law allows → Beyond the President’s power
- Gives orders to the armed forces → Commander in chief
- Deals with another country → Foreign affairs
- Refuses to sign a law Congress passed → Veto
- Forgives a federal crime → Pardon

**For each name: what you must be able to point to, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Carrying out the law**: a law Congress passed, and a federal agency or the President putting it into practice (writing its detailed rules, processing, inspecting, collecting, enforcing) without going past what it allows.
- **Beyond the President’s power**: an executive order or a rule from the President or a federal agency, something it demands of people or businesses, and no law Congress passed that allows it.
- **Commander in chief**: the President giving orders to the armed forces: where they go, what they do, or who leads them.
- **Foreign affairs**: the President, or an official acting for the President, dealing with another country: meeting, negotiating or signing an agreement.
- **Veto**: a law Congress has passed, and the President refusing to sign it and sending it back with objections.
- **Pardon**: a federal crime someone was charged with or convicted of, and the President forgiving it so that the punishment is lifted or never comes.

**To carry away**

- Say what the President or the office did, and point to the words that show it. If you cannot point, you do not have an answer yet.
- A rule or an order that demands something of people outside the government needs a law Congress passed behind it. With one, and staying inside it, the case is **Carrying out the law**. Without one, it is **Beyond the President’s power**. An *executive order* that only tells the offices how to do their work is **Carrying out the law**.
- The other four are the President’s own powers, and no law has to stand behind them: **Commander in chief**, **Foreign affairs**, **Veto** and **Pardon**. A visit by another country’s leader does not make a case **Foreign affairs**, and soldiers in a case do not make it **Commander in chief**. What the President does decides.
- Only Congress can declare war, and the President cannot forgive a crime against a state’s own law.

The first return is about two days after the drill, the next about a week after that, the next about three and a half weeks later.
