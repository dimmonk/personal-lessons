## Part 1 of 2: One group, a rise or fall, and two things side by side

### 1. A claim with numbers that holds up: how far does it go?

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient-holds`]

When a number you read or hear holds up, work out exactly what it shows, and repeat that much and no more. It is always one of the four things below.

Most claims with numbers that reach you are not wrong. A headline says, "Of 1,000 adults called, 31% smoke." A report says, "The reservoir fell from 82% full to 61% full over the summer." A health department says, "Patients at one clinic wait more than an hour more often than patients at another: 40 in 100 against 30." A school says, "The reading program raised scores." Any of these can hold up. When one does, there is nothing to correct, and the useful thing to know is how far it goes.

A claim that holds up has earned only what it says. The first gives one figure about one group, and shows nothing about change. The second follows a figure through time, and shows a rise or a fall but not why. The third sets two things side by side, and shows which is bigger but not why. Only the fourth may say that one thing made the other happen, and only because of how its groups were formed.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“Where does this claim first go wrong?”** Its answers:

- **“Who was counted”**: give this answer when the people or things the figure came from do not match the group the claim is about, or are too few to trust.
- **“What the number counts”**: give this answer when the figure could rise, fall or differ without the real thing it is taken to show doing the same.
- **“What it is compared with”**: give this answer when the figure is a percentage change, a test’s accuracy, or a total beside another total, and the claim leaves out what you need beside it to read it fairly.
- **“What caused what”**: give this answer when the claim says one thing made another happen, and the same result could have come about another way.
- **“Nothing goes wrong”**: give this answer when every part the claim uses checks out in order, and none of them goes wrong. **This unit is about these stories.**

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

*(One tap on any of these lines opens the card in Unit One that taught it.)*

What does the claim tell you?
- A figure for one group → A fair count
- A rise or fall in one figure → A real change
- A difference between two things → A fair comparison
- One thing causing another → A fair test

- A fair count: a figure for one group, from a fair picture of it
- A real change: a figure that moved because the real thing moved
- A fair comparison: two like things, counted the same way and set side by side
- A fair test: groups split by chance, one given the thing and one not

### 2. Asking some of them

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-sample`]

The first claim that holds up is built on a figure from some of a group, and the word for those few people comes first.

*The town council and its 800*

> A town council wants to know how many of the town's 40,000 residents walk to work. It cannot ask all 40,000, so it asks 800 of them. Of the 800, 248 say they walk to work, which is 31 in 100. The town council announces: 'About 31% of residents walk to work.'

Asking everyone is usually too slow, too costly or impossible. A town council cannot stop 40,000 people in the street, so it asks 800 of them and works out the figure from those 800. The claim is about all 40,000, and the figure comes from the 800.

**The word for this.** *Sample*: the people or things actually counted, standing in for a bigger group.

Every figure from a *sample* comes with one question: how did these 800 come to be the 800? If the council had asked the first 800 people it met outside the stadium on game day, the figure would still be 31 in 100, and it would mean something else.


### 3. Letting a lottery choose

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 3 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-atrandom`]

A *sample* can stand for the whole group only if the way it was chosen does not lean. One way of choosing is built not to lean, and it has a name.

*The town council’s lottery*

> The town council has a list of all 40,000 addresses in town. To choose the 800, a clerk asks a computer to draw 800 of the 40,000 numbers, like pulling tickets from a drum. Every address has the same chance of being drawn: 800 out of 40,000, which is 1 in 50. Nobody on the staff chooses which houses.

Compare two ways the council could choose its 800. A clerk could pick the streets she knows, or the people who are home when she calls. Even if she means no harm, her choice leans toward people like the ones she knows, or who are easy to reach. Or a computer could draw 800 of the 40,000 addresses, like tickets from a drum, so that nobody decides which.

With the draw, every address has the same chance: 800 ÷ 40,000 is 1 in 50. A house on the richest street and a house on the poorest each have a 1 in 50 chance, so the draw cannot favor either. Luck can still make the 800 a little different from the town, but nothing pushes it in one direction.

**The word for this.** *At random*: picked by chance, like a lottery or a coin toss, so that each one had the same chance and nobody chose.

Two things to notice. First, *at random* says how the people were picked, not how many there are. Second, it is not the same as picking carelessly. Picking anyhow is what the clerk does when she asks whoever is nearby, and the lottery is the careful way, because the choice is taken out of everyone’s hands.


### 4. A fair count

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-sampok`]

Unit One ended with the answer **“Nothing goes wrong”**. Here is what it looks like, simplest first: one figure about one group.

*The library card holders*

> A library system has 90,000 card holders and wants to know how many borrowed a book last month. A computer drew 1,500 card numbers by lottery from the full list. Staff emailed all 1,500, then phoned and finally visited the ones who had not replied, until 1,380 had answered. Of those 1,380, 566 had borrowed a book last month, which is 41 in 100. The library announces: '⟦About 41% of our card holders borrowed a book last month⟧.'

The library could not ask 90,000 people, so it asked a *sample* of 1,500, and a lottery chose them: that is *at random*. Every card number had the same chance, 1 in 60, so the library could not have picked the people it expected to say yes.

Nearly everyone chosen is in the figure: 1,380 of the 1,500 answered, which is 92 in 100. Even if all 120 who did not answer had borrowed a book, the figure would be 46 in 100, and if none had, 38. So 41 cannot be far off. How the people were chosen matters more than how many there are: 100,000 people who chose to answer a website poll are a fair picture of nobody but themselves.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find how the people were chosen: a computer drew card numbers by lottery from the full list.** If you cannot find this, you do not have an answer yet.
2. **Find how many of those chosen answered: 1,380 of the 1,500, and the rest were chased.** If most answered, the figure stands for the whole group; if few did, it does not.
3. **Check what the claim says: only that 41% borrowed a book last month.** Nothing about a rise, another library or a reason.

This is **A fair count**. The figure is a fair stand-in for all the card holders, give or take the luck of the draw. It does not mean the figure is exact.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Repeat the figure for the group it names: 41 in 100 of the library’s card holders borrowed a book last month.** That is the whole claim.
2. **Leave out "more", "less", "rising" and "because".** The claim has not earned any of them.

You may also hear this called “a representative sample” or “a random sample”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **A fair count**.

### 6. How far luck can move a figure

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 6 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-margin`]

A *sample* drawn *at random* does not lean, but luck can still move the figure a little. Honest claims say how far, and that has a name.

*The two polling firms*

> A newspaper hires two polling firms. Each draws 1,000 adults by lottery from a list of every adult in the city, which has about 3 million, and each hears from nearly all 1,000. The first firm finds that 520 of its 1,000 approve of the mayor, which is 52 in 100. The second firm finds that 490 of its 1,000 do, which is 49 in 100.

Both polling firms did everything right, and they still got different figures: 52 in 100 and 49 in 100. Each drew a different 1,000 adults by lottery, and luck made each group a little different from the city, and from the other group. Neither firm made a mistake.

So any figure from a few people is a little off from the figure for everyone, and the reason is luck, not carelessness. Only asking more people makes it smaller.

You can estimate the size of the luck. As a rough guide, for shares between about 20 and 80 in 100, divide 1 by the square root of the number of people. The square root of 1,000 is about 32, and 1 ÷ 32 is about 0.03, which is 3 points. So a firm with 1,000 people says "52 in 100, give or take 3 points". That means the figure for the whole city is very likely between 49 and 55.

**The word for this.** *Margin of error*: how far a figure from a sample could be off from the true figure for the whole group, just by luck.

Two things to carry away. First, the *margin of error* covers only the luck of a fair draw. If the people were chosen badly, it does not warn you: a badly chosen group of 1,000 has the same margin, and its figure can be far off. Second, the size of the city does not appear in the sum. 1,000 people give the same margin whether the city has 300,000 adults or 3 million, which is why a thousand people can speak for millions.


### 7. A real change

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-measok`]

The first claim gave one figure at one time. This one follows a figure through time. Take a town’s own records.

*The town’s births*

> The town clerk's office records every birth to a mother who lives in the town, checking the register against hospital notices in the same way every year. The office has used the same form since 2015, and nobody's budget or pay depends on the number. It recorded 1,210 births in 2019 and 1,090 in 2023. The town newsletter says: '⟦Births in town fell from 1,210 in 2019 to 1,090 in 2023⟧.'

When a figure changes, one of two things happened: the thing it counts changed, or the way of counting did. If the clerk’s office had switched its form in 2021 to include births at a hospital across the river, the figure could rise without one more baby being born. If the office were paid for each birth it recorded, the figure could rise because the office tried harder.

Here none of that happened. The same office wrote down every birth on the same form, and nobody was paid or judged on the number. So the fall of 1,210 − 1,090 = 120 births is a real fall in births.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find how the figure was counted each time: the same office, the same form, the same checks.** A new form or tool can move a figure all by itself.
2. **Check that nobody is paid, ranked or judged on it: nobody’s pay or budget depends on the number.** If someone is, stop: people can push a figure without changing the real thing.
3. **Check that the claim only says it fell, with the numbers: 1,210 in 2019, 1,090 in 2023.** It shows that the figure moved, not why.

This is **A real change**. “Change” means a rise or a fall, and “real” means the thing itself changed, not only the figure.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Say that it rose or fell, and by how much: births fell by 120.** That is all the claim has shown.
2. **Leave out "because".** The claim has not earned a reason.

### 9. A fair count or A real change: telling them apart

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 9 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-samp-meas`]

Two claims can look alike when they give the same figure about the same thing. Here is the first pair: a claim about one time, and a claim about two.

**Story A**

> A clinic saw 12,000 patients last year. To find out how long patients wait, a computer drew 400 of last year's visits by lottery from the booking system, and a clerk looked up the check-in time and the time the patient saw a doctor for every one of the 400. The average wait was 24 minutes. The clinic says: 'Last year the average wait for a visit was about 24 minutes.'

**Story B**

> A clinic saw about 12,000 patients in each of 2022 and 2023. In both years the booking system recorded the check-in time and the time the patient saw a doctor for every visit, and nobody's pay depends on the number. The average wait was 31 minutes in 2022 and 24 minutes in 2023. The clinic says: 'The average wait fell from 31 minutes in 2022 to 24 minutes in 2023.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the same clinic and the same figure, an average wait. Compare one thing: does the claim give the figure once, or follow it through time?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“A rise or fall in one figure”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the clinic drew 400 of last year’s 12,000 visits by lottery and timed each one. The claim gives one figure, 24 minutes, for one year, and says nothing about earlier or later years. The answer is **“A figure for one group”**, so it is **A fair count**.

In Story B the clinic timed every visit in two years, the same way, and nobody’s pay depends on the number. The claim gives the figure twice, 31 minutes and 24 minutes, and says it fell by 7 minutes. The answer is **“A rise or fall in one figure”**, so it is **A real change**.

The 24 minutes is the same in both. What separates them is whether the claim gives it once or follows it through time.

**How to tell them apart**

Does the claim give the figure once, or give it at two or more times and say it rose or fell?


### 10. A fair comparison

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 10 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-compok`]

The first two claims had one thing in them. This one has two, set side by side, and says which is bigger.

*The two bus lines*

> A city transit agency compared two bus lines. Both are 12 miles long, run on weekdays in the same hours, and serve neighborhoods of similar size. The agency checked every trip in March with the same GPS tracker. On Line 5, 12 of 200 trips arrived more than ten minutes late. On Line 9, 31 of 205 did. The agency says: '⟦Line 9 buses are late more often than Line 5 buses: 15 trips in 100 against 6⟧.'

The agency compares two bus lines that are alike, counted the same way, and shown with the trips behind each figure. When all three hold, the gap between them is real.

A line through a quiet suburb at midday beside a line through downtown at rush hour would tell you very little, however carefully it was counted.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check the two are alike: both lines are 12 miles long, run in the same hours and serve similar neighborhoods.** Otherwise the gap may come from an easier or harder stretch of road, not from the lines.
2. **Check both were counted the same way over the same time: every trip in March, with the same tracker.** A different tool or a different month can make the gap by itself.
3. **Find the totals behind each figure: 200 trips and 205 trips.** Dividing each count by its own total puts the two on the same scale.
4. **Check the claim only says which is bigger: Line 9 is late more often than Line 5.** A gap shows which is ahead, not why.

This is **A fair comparison**. It is a comparison because the claim sets one thing beside another, and it is fair because the two are alike, counted alike, and shown with their totals.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Say which is bigger and by how much: 15 late trips in 100 on Line 9, against 6 in 100 on Line 5.** That is all the comparison has shown.
2. **Leave out any reason.** The comparison has not earned one.

You may also hear this called “like for like”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **A fair comparison**.

### 12. A real change or A fair comparison: telling them apart

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 12 of 21*

The second pair: both put two figures in front of you, and both can sound like “this is lower than that”.

**Story A**

> A water district reads the same gauge at the same dam at 8 a.m. on the first of each month, and has done so since 2005. Nobody's pay depends on the reading. On 1 June the reservoir stood at 82% full, and on 1 September at 61% full. The district says: 'The reservoir fell from 82% full on 1 June to 61% full on 1 September.'

**Story B**

> A water district reads the same gauge at the same dam at 8 a.m. on 1 September every year, and has done so for twenty-one years. Nobody's pay depends on the reading. This year the reservoir stood at 61% full. The average of the twenty earlier readings for that date is 74% full. The district says: 'On 1 September the reservoir stood at 61% full, 13 points below the usual 74% full for the date.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the same reservoir and the same gauge. Compare one thing: does the claim follow the reservoir through time, or set it beside something else?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“A difference between two things”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the district read the gauge on 1 June and again on 1 September, and the claim follows the one reservoir through the summer: from 82% full to 61% full, a fall of 21 points. The answer is **“A rise or fall in one figure”**, so it is **A real change**.

In Story B the district looks on one date, 1 September, and sets this year’s 61% beside the average of twenty readings for that date, 74%. The claim says the level is 13 points below the usual. Nothing is followed through time: one thing is set beside its own usual figure. The answer is **“A difference between two things”**, so it is **A fair comparison**.

The 61% is the same in both. In one claim the reservoir moves while the gauge is watched. In the other it stands still beside the usual.

**How to tell them apart**

Does the claim follow one thing as time passes, or set one thing beside another (or beside its own usual figure) and say which is bigger?

## Part 2 of 2: What made the difference, then one whole claim and the drill

### 13. A dummy tablet

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 13 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-placebo`]

The first three claims stopped short of saying why. The fourth may say why, and the tests behind it often use a word you have not met.

*The cold remedy test*

> A researcher wants to know whether a cold remedy shortens colds. She gives one group the remedy and a second group a tablet that looks, tastes and weighs the same but has nothing in it. Nobody, including the staff who hand out the tablets and the staff who ask about colds, knows who got which until the end.

The cold remedy test gave the second group a dummy tablet instead of nothing. Why not give them nothing? Because people who know they were given a remedy often feel better, or say they do. A dummy tablet puts both groups through the same thing, so the only difference left between them is the medicine.

Not every test has a dummy. A bank that texts a reminder to some customers has no dummy text to send, so the other customers simply get the usual. What matters in every such test is that the two groups differ in one thing only: one gets the thing, and the other does not.

**The word for this.** *Placebo*: a fake treatment that looks like the real one, so nobody in a test can tell which group they are in.


### 14. A fair test

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 14 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-causeok`]

The first three claims stop at what the figures show. The fourth goes a step further and says why. That step is safe in only one situation.

*The migraine test*

> A clinic enrolled 400 adults who had migraines on at least eight days a month. A computer split them into two groups of 200 by lottery. One group took a new tablet every day for eight weeks. The other group took a placebo that looked and tasted the same. Nobody knew who had which until the end. At the end of the eight weeks the tablet group averaged 4 migraine days a month and the placebo group averaged 7. The clinic reports: '⟦The new tablet reduced migraine days: 4 a month against 7⟧.'

The migraine claim goes further than the others: it says the tablet is why the groups differ. That is safe only when nothing but the tablet differs between the groups, and the way to be sure of that is that nobody chose who went in which group.

Suppose people had chosen for themselves whether to take the tablet. They might be the ones who take their health most seriously, or the ones with the worst migraines. Then the gap of 3 days a month might come from who they are, and the tablet might have done nothing.

Here a lottery formed the groups. Each of the 400 people had an equal chance of landing in either group, whatever their age, however bad their migraines and however much they care about their health. So before the tablet the two groups were alike. After eight weeks they differ by 7 − 4 = 3 migraine days a month, and the tablet is the one thing left that was different. The *placebo* does a second job: neither group knew which it had, so everyone hoped the same.

The claim stays inside what the test shows: on average, for people like the ones enrolled, the group given the tablet had fewer migraine days.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find who decided which group each person went into: a computer split them by lottery.** A lottery, a coin toss or a computer draw works; "the ones who signed up" does not.
2. **Check both groups were treated and counted alike: a *placebo* for one, and the same eight weeks for both.** Then the tablet is the one thing left that was different.
3. **Find what the claim says the tablet did: reduced migraine days, 4 a month against 7.** Only a lottery lets a claim say what made the gap.

This is **A fair test**. It is a test because the researchers gave the thing to one group and not the other, and it is fair because a lottery, not anyone’s choice, formed the groups.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Repeat the cause the claim states: the tablet cut migraine days, 4 a month against 7.** The lottery lets it say that.
2. **Limit it to people like the 400 enrolled: adults with migraines on at least eight days a month.** The test shows nothing about anyone else.

You may also hear this called “a randomized controlled trial” or “a randomized trial”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **A fair test**.

### 16. A fair comparison or A fair test: telling them apart

The last pair: both show two groups with a gap, and the numbers can be exactly the same. Only what the claim says differs.

**Story A**

> A school drew names from a hat to choose 60 of its 120 students for a new reading program, and the other 60 kept their usual lessons. All 120 took the same test at the end of the term. > A school drew names from a hat to choose 60 of its 120 students for a new reading program, and the other 60 kept their usual lessons. All 120 took the same test at the end of the term. The program group averaged 74 points and the other group averaged 62. The school says: 'The reading program raised test scores by 12 points: 74 against 62.'

Does the claim stop at which group is ahead, or say what made the gap? If it says what made the gap, who decided which group each person or thing went into?


### 17. The question you have been answering all along

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 17 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-holds`]

You have seen this question under each new name. Here it is with its four answers in one place.

**The question:** **“What does the claim tell you?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other three.

- **“A figure for one group”**
  - Give this answer when the claim gives a figure for one group at one time, like a share or an average, and says nothing of a rise or fall, a difference, or a cause.
  - It leads to **A fair count**.
- **“A rise or fall in one figure”**
  - Give this answer when the claim gives one figure for one thing at two or more times and says it rose or fell, sets it beside nothing else, and does not say what caused it.
  - It leads to **A real change**.
- **“A difference between two things”**
  - Give this answer when the claim sets two groups, places or things side by side and says which is bigger, likelier or riskier, without saying that one thing caused it.
  - It leads to **A fair comparison**.
- **“One thing causing another”**
  - Give this answer when the claim says one thing made another happen.
  - It leads to **A fair test**.

**Why it matters**

A sound claim has earned only what it says. Each sort of claim needs different parts to check out, so knowing which one it is tells you what you can rely on and what it has not shown: a fair count says nothing about change, and a fair comparison says nothing about cause.

Each answer is a size of claim, and the size sets what you can repeat to someone else. One group at one time shows nothing about change. A rise or fall shows nothing about why. A bigger and a smaller shows nothing about why either. Only a claim of cause may say why, and only because a lottery formed its groups.

Go by what the claim says, not by what the story hints at. A program in the story does not turn a difference into a cause, and a list of years does not turn one figure into a change unless the claim says it rose or fell.

**How to answer it**

1. **Find the claim: the sentence that says what the figures show.** Go by what it says, not by what the story around it hints at.
2. **Count the groups and the times in it.** That sorts it into one of four sizes.
3. **One group at one time? It is “A figure for one group”.** The figure is all it gives.
4. **One thing at two or more times, said to have risen or fallen? It is “A rise or fall in one figure”.** It follows the figure through time.
5. **Two groups or things, or one thing beside its usual level, with the claim saying which is bigger? It is “A difference between two things”.** It sets one beside the other.
6. **Does it credit one thing with causing the result? It is “One thing causing another”.** Only a lottery lets it say that.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Sometimes two answers both seem to fit. Each pair below was set side by side earlier in this unit, and one question tells it apart.

- A fair count or A real change: Does the claim give the figure once, or give it at two or more times and say it rose or fell?
- A real change or A fair comparison: Does the claim follow one thing as time passes, or set one thing beside another (or beside its own usual figure) and say which is bigger?
- A fair comparison or A fair test: Does the claim stop at which group is ahead, or say what made the gap? If it says what made the gap, who decided which group each person or thing went into?

### 20. What to carry away

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 20 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap-holds`]

You have now run the questions on claims that hold up. Here is the unit in one place.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What does the claim tell you?
- A figure for one group → A fair count
- A rise or fall in one figure → A real change
- A difference between two things → A fair comparison
- One thing causing another → A fair test

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **A fair count**: a figure for one group, taken from all of it or a random pick, most of them answering, enough that one or two more would not move it, and a claim that goes no further.
- **A real change**: one figure that rose or fell, counted the same way and with the same effort throughout, nothing else pushing it, and a claim that says only that it rose or fell.
- **A fair comparison**: two things of the same sort counted the same way over the same time, with the actual counts, no hidden mix of easy and hard ones, and a claim only about which is bigger.
- **A fair test**: people or things split into two groups at random, only one given the thing, both counted the same way afterward, and a difference the claim says the thing caused.

**To carry away**

- When the first question gives **“Nothing goes wrong”**, one question is left: **“What does the claim tell you?”** Find the words that answer it, then give the name.
- A claim that holds up has earned only what it says. One figure about one group is not a trend, a fall is not a reason, and a gap is not a cause. Repeat what it says and no more.
- A figure from a few people is a little off by luck. The *margin of error* says how far.
- **A fair test** is the only name that may say what made the difference. Look for the words that say a lottery formed the groups.
- The story can hint at more than the claim says. Go by what the claim says.
- A claim that looks like these may still go wrong. Run the first question first, and give this unit’s answer only if it gives **“Nothing goes wrong”**.
