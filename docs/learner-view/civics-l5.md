# US Civics & History: Theirs to do?

Revision 1, live. Part: Theirs to do?. Where the subject ends up: Answer the citizenship interview’s civics questions aloud from memory, and, reading a news story about a US law, order or ruling, say who acted, whether it was theirs to do, who can stop it, and whether to take the story as it stands or check who is behind it first.

## The why

Once you know who acted, put the next question to yourself: was it theirs to do? Each level has only some powers. Find the line that decides it: a law the story names, or the act itself when the table settles it.

## Step 1: Where each power comes from

A plain table, "What lets them do it":

> Who  |  Where their power comes from  (the column heads)
> Congress  |  The powers the Constitution lists: taxes and spending, the army, war, coining money, trade between states and with other countries
> A state  |  Everything not given to the federal government (the 10th Amendment). Never a power only for the federal government, such as printing money or making treaties.
> A city, county or school board  |  Only what its state hands down: the story has to show the state law. Carrying out a city rule already made under that power is theirs too.
> The president or an agency  |  The president’s own powers (leading the military, vetoing bills, appointing judges) and running the executive branch’s own offices: theirs. Reaching outside the branch, to companies, people or states, needs a law of Congress: a law that allows it, theirs; Congress silent, not settled; a law against it, not theirs. A power of the states, such as schools: the president cannot order it, with or without Congress. Congress can only attach conditions to money it gives the states, and an agency enforcing those conditions acts on a law that allows it.
> When two levels clash  |  The higher level’s law wins: federal over state and local, state over local.

Put one question to the story: what lets them? A line may name the law, or only the act is told and the table decides.

## Step 2: Four answers

A plain table, "What to say":

> Say  |  When  (the column heads)
> Theirs to do  |  The power is theirs by the table, and nothing stands against it
> Not theirs to do  |  A law stands against it, or the power belongs to another level
> Not settled  |  Only for the president or an agency: reaching outside the executive branch, into something Congress could allow, where Congress has said nothing
> In court  |  A court has the lawsuit now and has not ruled

## Step 3: A worked example

A news story, from "The Capital Sun":

> # President releases emergency oil
>
> A pipeline broke last week, and gasoline supplies on the East Coast are running low. The president ordered the government to release oil from the national emergency stockpile. A law Congress passed years ago lets the president release oil from the stockpile when supplies run short. Gas stations said deliveries should improve next week.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**Was it theirs to do?**
- Theirs to do
- Not theirs to do
- Not settled: the president or an agency reaching outside its branch where Congress has said nothing
- In court: a court is deciding it now

The working, one step at a time:
1. Find who acted: The story says the president ordered the government to release oil from the national emergency stockpile.
1. Find what lets them: Look for what Congress said about the stockpile: the third line names a law that allows it.

Then the answer: Theirs to do / A law Congress passed years ago lets the president release oil from the stockpile when supplies run short. A law of Congress lets the president do this. That is the strongest ground a president can have.

## Step 4: A wrong idea

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “A judge will sort it out.” A court only steps in when someone has brought it a lawsuit.
The right line: Say “in court” only when a court has the lawsuit now. Someone saying they might sue has not brought one.

## Step 5: Same act, different power

| A | B |
|---|---|
| The governor closed a state park during a fire, as state law allows. | The governor closed a national park, which is federal land. |

Compare: Both are a governor closing a park. A state park is the state’s; a national park is federal land, and no state law can hand the governor power over it.

| A | B |
|---|---|
| The president ordered federal offices to close at 5 p.m. | The president ordered every private store to close at 5 p.m., and Congress has said nothing. |

Compare: Federal offices are the president’s own branch to run: theirs. Private stores are outside it, and with Congress silent the order is not settled.

| A | B |
|---|---|
| The president ordered every public school to start at 9 a.m. | A law of Congress gives schools lunch money if they serve fresh fruit, and the Education Department checks that they do. |

Compare: No one in Washington can order a state’s schools. Congress can attach conditions to money it gives, so the first is not theirs and the second is.

#### Item 1

> # President orders 9 a.m. school start
>
> The president signed an order on Monday saying every public high school in the country must start classes at 9 a.m. School boards said they were surprised. Congress has passed no law about when schools start.

- Not theirs to do (right)
- Not settled: the president or an agency reaching outside its branch where Congress has said nothing — if chosen: Not settled is for an order Congress could allow. No law can let the president order a state’s schools, so there is nothing to settle.
- In court: a court is deciding it now — if chosen: No lawsuit about it is before a court now.

- "School boards said they were surprised." — if tapped: That is not the sentence.
- "Congress has passed no law about when schools start." — if tapped: That is not the sentence.

After the answer: the deciding words are marked. Reason: Running schools is a power of the states, and the president cannot order a state’s schools to do anything. (Congress can only attach conditions to money it gives.)
The interview question this rests on: Name one power that is only for the states. Accepted: Provide schooling and education; Provide protection (police); Provide safety (fire departments); Give a driver’s license; Approve zoning and land use.
