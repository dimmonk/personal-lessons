# US Civics & History: Federal, state, local, and your answers

Revision 1, live. Part: Federal, state, local, and your answers. Where the subject ends up: Answer the citizenship interview’s civics questions aloud from memory, and, reading a news story about a US law, order or ruling, say who acted, whether it was theirs to do, who can stop it, and whether to take the story as it stands or check who is behind it first.

## The why

Three levels of government share the work: the whole country, your state, and your city or county. Each level has three parts. Knowing which level does what answers three questions on the list, and it is how you will read the news later in this course.
Eight answers depend on where you live and who holds office now, so you look them up and enter them here.

## Step 1: Three levels, three parts each

A plain table, "Who does what, at each level":

> Level  |  Writes the laws  |  Carries them out  |  Judges disputes  (the column heads)
> Federal: the whole country  |  Congress  |  The president, the Cabinet and federal agencies  |  The Supreme Court and the other federal courts
> State: one state  |  The state legislature  |  The governor and state agencies  |  State courts
> Local: a city, town or county  |  The city council or county board  |  The mayor or county head, the police and the fire department  |  Local courts, under state law

Whenever you read about the government, put two questions to yourself first. Which level? Which part? The president is not behind every decision: a school board, a governor and a judge each answer to their own level.

## Step 2: Who handles what

A plain table, "What each level handles":

> Level  |  What it handles  (the column heads)
> Federal  |  Only the federal government may: print paper money, mint coins, declare war, create an army, make treaties, set foreign policy
> State  |  Only the states: provide schooling and education, provide protection (police), provide safety (fire departments), give a driver’s license, approve zoning and land use
> Local  |  What the state hands down: a city or county often runs the schools, the police and the fire department, and decides how land is used

The 10th Amendment says it in one sentence: powers not given to the federal government belong to the states or to the people.
The table uses the official list’s own words, so the words you learn are the words it accepts.
