# Strategy: Will it last?

Revision 1, draft (the screen carries the draft line). Part: Will it last?. Where the subject ends up: After this you can look at a business decision (expand, add a product, chase a big customer, answer a rival, invest) and tell whether it is a strategy, say where the business makes its money and why, and pick the move that keeps it different in a way rivals cannot copy, when you face that decision in your own business.

## The why

Most things that make a business different this year are copied by next year: a new machine, a new dish, a new look.
Strategists test an advantage with two questions: what does it give you, and what stops a rival getting it too? Here you learn to put both to any difference, your own included.

## Step 1: What it gives, and what stops a copy

First: what does the difference give the business? Either customers pay more here than at rivals for the same work, or it costs less here than at rivals to make it. If customers pay the same and nothing costs less, it gives nothing, however different it looks.
Then: what stops a rival getting the same? One of three answers.
Only this business has it, and a rival cannot get or make one: the last plot of land, a deal no one else can sign, a dock the rules allow no one else to build.
It rests on many activities that fit together, so a rival would have to copy all of them at once, and give up how it runs now.
Or nothing: a rival can buy it, copy it, or get the same result another way.
It lasts only with both: something it gives, and something that stops a copy. Why it matters: a difference a rival can buy is worth having, but it is a race, not a position. Plan as if it will be gone.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “We were the first in town to get one, so we are ahead.”
The right line: Being first stops no one. If a rival can buy the same thing from the same maker, the lead lasts only until it does. Look for what a rival cannot get.

## Step 2: Two differences

| A | B |
|---|---|
| A bakery buys an oven that cuts the cost of each cake by $2. The maker sells it to anyone. | A dog daycare owns the last open land in town, and the town allows no new dog businesses elsewhere. Owners pay $15 a day more for the grass. |

Compare: Both give something: a lower cost, a higher price. Only the second has a barrier. The oven’s gain lasts until the other bakeries order one.

## Step 3: A worked example

Your own note, written after an argument: "Eve’s notes on her dog daycare", Written in March.

> We are the only dog daycare in town with a fenced acre of grass; the others are indoor rooms.
> Owners pay us $45 a day; the indoor places charge $30.
> Two indoor places have said they want to add outdoor space.
> We own the last open land at the edge of town, and the town will allow no new dog businesses anywhere else.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**What does the difference give the business?**
- A higher price than rivals get
- A lower cost than rivals have
- Neither: it gives nothing a rival lacks

The working, one step at a time:
1. What does the difference give? Compare the price or the cost with rivals’.: Owners pay $45 here against $30 at the indoor places: a higher price.
1. What stops a rival getting the same?: The rivals want outdoor space, but Eve owns the last open land and the town allows no new dog businesses elsewhere. Only she has it, and they cannot get it.
1. Both a benefit and a barrier?: Yes, so the advantage should last.

Then the answer: A higher price than rivals get / Only we have it, and a rival cannot get or make one. Owners pay $15 a day more for the grass, and the rivals who want to copy it cannot: Eve owns the last open land and the town allows no more. A benefit and a barrier, so it lasts.
