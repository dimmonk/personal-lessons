## Part 1 of 3: Software you chose, and a file that was sent to you

### 1. Before you install, open or share anything, check how it started

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 1 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

Before you install something, open a file or press Share, check how the request reached you. It is always one of four things: three scams, and one real thing. You can tell which before you press anything.

A red page fills your laptop with a siren and a phone number. An email from a firm you have never heard of has a file attached. A caller says you are owed a refund and needs a minute to see your computer. And on an ordinary day, you download a program from its maker’s website.

Three of these are scams. One is fine, and treating it as a scam would stop you installing the updates that keep your device safe. The box that appears and the name of the program tell you nothing. How the request reached you does, and you already know that before you press anything.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“What is it asking you to do right now?”** Its answers:

- **“Install something, open a file, or share your screen”**: give this answer when it asks you to install something, open or run a file, or let someone see or control your screen, or it warns of a device problem and gives you someone to call. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“Sign in, give a code, or allow an app”**: give this answer when it asks you to sign in, to give a one-time code by typing it, reading it out or passing it on, or to press Allow so that an app can use one of your accounts.
- **“Pay or send money”**: give this answer when it asks you to pay or send money, by any means, now or by a date.
- **“Tell them about yourself”**: give this answer when it asks for facts about you: a document, an ID or card number, your date of birth or address, or your work, home and family.
- **“Nothing: it only tells you something”**: give this answer when it tells you something has happened or will happen and asks you to do nothing, and anything it suggests uses only what you already had, like the number on your card.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. Real installation

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 2 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-realinstall`]

Start with the one that is not a scam, so you know what the scams copy.

*The video-calling program*

> Priya's team is switching to a video-calling program called Huddle. At her desk she ⟦types the maker's web address, huddle.com, into her browser herself⟧. She presses the Download button on the page and runs the file. Her computer shows a box: 'Do you want to allow this app to make changes to your device?' She presses Yes. ⟦Nobody has called or messaged her about any of this⟧.

Priya chose the program herself and typed the maker’s own web address. That is *a way you already had*: an address that was hers before anyone could message her. Your phone’s app store counts too. Because she started it, nobody was there to hurry her or tell her what to click.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find who started it: Priya did, because her team needed a program.** Anything you choose to get begins with you wanting it.
2. **Find where she went: the maker’s own address, which she typed herself.** The maker’s website, your app store and your device’s own update menu were yours before any message came.
3. **Check that nobody contacted her first: nobody called or messaged her.** A person who contacts you first is the one steering.
4. **Ignore the box asking whether to allow changes.** It appears for every installation, harmful ones too.

This is **Real installation**. Nothing is wrong here. It has a name so that you do not treat every installation as a threat.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Go ahead.** You did everything right.
2. **For the next update, use the same app store or the same saved address.** A link in an email or a pop-up is not the same thing.
3. **If a call you did not start comes in the middle, stop and ask again: “How did this start?”** Then someone else has started steering.

### 4. Malware

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 4 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-malware`]

Now the opposite: a file, or a link to one, that was sent to you without being asked for.

*The invoice file*

> ⟦An email reaches Sam at work from an address he does not know⟧: 'Hello, the invoice below is unpaid. ⟦Open the attached file to see the amount due⟧ and to avoid a late fee.' The attachment is called Invoice-4471.exe. Sam has not ordered anything from this firm, and ⟦nobody has called him⟧.

Sam asked for nothing. A file arrived with a reason to open it, and a late fee to hurry him. The reason is chosen to suit you: an unpaid invoice, a pay stub, a package note, a job test, a photo.

Once the file runs, it can copy your passwords, watch your screen, lock your files or let someone else control the device. Sam’s file ends in .exe, which means a program, and opening it runs it. You may notice nothing afterward, so you cannot wait to see what it does. His computer shows the same box as Priya’s, but her program came from the maker and his file came to him.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find what arrived: an email with Invoice-4471.exe attached.** A file or a link that came to you is not one you went to get.
2. **Find the reason to open it: an unpaid invoice and a late fee.** There is always a reason, and it is chosen to hurry you.
3. **Check whether anyone is on the phone with you: nobody called Sam.** With someone on the line, it is a different scam.

This is **Malware**, short for “malicious software”: software made to do harm.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Do not open the file or tap the link. Delete the message.** Nothing happens until you open it.
2. **If it might be real, use *the check*: ask the sender yourself, through *a way you already had*.** Never use the phone number or link in the message itself.
3. **If you already opened it, turn off wifi and call your bank at the number on your card.** That cuts the device off while you work out what was taken.

You may also hear this called “virus” or “ransomware” or “harmful attachment”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Malware**.

### 6. Malware or Real installation: telling them apart

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 6 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-malware-realinstall`]

In both, a program ends up on the device, and the same box appears.

**Story A**

> Lena has used a photo editor called Pixelbench for a year, and she wants the newest version. She types the maker's web address, pixelbench.com, into her browser herself and presses Download. She runs the installer, and her computer shows a box: 'Do you want to allow this app to make changes to your device?' She presses Yes.

**Story B**

> Lena has used a photo editor called Pixelbench for a year. An email arrives from an address she does not know: 'Your Pixelbench license needs updating. Run the attached installer to keep using it.' She runs the installer, and her computer shows a box: 'Do you want to allow this app to make changes to your device?' She presses Yes.

**What to compare.** Both stories are about Lena and the same photo editor, and both end in the same box. Compare one thing: how the installer reached her.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“A file or a link sent to you in a message”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A Lena typed the maker’s address herself, to get the newest version. Nobody had contacted her. That is **“You started it yourself, through a way you already had”**, so it is **Real installation**.

In Story B an email from an address she does not know says her license needs updating, with an installer attached. The installer came to her. That is **“A file or a link sent to you in a message”**, so it is **Malware**.

Same program, same company, same box. The only difference is who started it, and Lena can see that before she presses anything.

**How to tell them apart**

Did you set out to get this program yourself, from its maker’s website or your app store? Or did a file or link arrive in a message?

## Part 2 of 3: Someone who offers to fix something, or to pay you back

### 7. The first result on a search page may be an ad

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 7 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-searchad`]

The next scam depends on one thing that is easy to miss on a search page.

*The first result*

> Rosa's dryer stops working. She types 'Hartley appliance repair phone number' into a search page. The first result has a small label, 'Ad', beside it, and a phone number in bold. Underneath it, with no label, is the Hartley company's own website, which lists a different number. She calls the number in the ad, and a man answers: 'Hartley support, how can I help you?'

Rosa called the number in the result marked “Ad”. Anyone can pay for that spot, so it was not the company’s number: the company’s own website, lower down, listed a different one.

A scammer can buy the top spot, use the company’s name and put in their own phone number. A number from search results was not yours before you searched, so it is not *a way you already had*, even though you typed the search yourself.

**The word for this.** *Search ad*: a paid result at the top of a search page, marked “Sponsored” or “Ad”, which anyone can buy, a scammer included.

From now on, a result marked “Ad” or “Sponsored” does not count. Trust the numbers on your bill, your card or your contract, and addresses you typed or saved before.


### 8. Tech-support scam

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 8 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-techsupport`]

In the first two, nobody was talking to you. In the last two, a person is on the line. This one starts with a warning that gives you someone to call.

*The siren page*

> While Joan reads the news on her laptop, a red page fills the whole window and a loud alarm sounds: 'WARNING: your computer is infected. ⟦Do not switch it off. Call Support now at (800) 555-0188⟧.' The page does not close when she presses the X. She calls the number. A man says he is a technician and asks her to open a web page and type in a code he reads out, ⟦so that he can see her computer and remove the problem⟧.

The page is only a web page. It knows nothing about Joan’s computer, and the alarm is there to frighten her. It will not close because it was built to look as if it controls the machine. A real warning comes from your device’s own software, in that software’s own window, and does not send you to a stranger’s phone number.

Once she calls, it goes step by step. He has her share her screen (*screen-sharing*), shows her ordinary lists that every computer has as “proof”, and sells her a “protection plan” paid by gift card or wire transfer. Joan could refuse at any step until she lets him in. After that, everything happens on a screen someone else controls.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the warning: a red page and an alarm saying her computer is infected.** A web page cannot know anything about your computer.
2. **Find who it tells you to call: “Support” at (800) 555-0188.** A real warning does not send you to a stranger’s number.
3. **Notice that the page will not close when she presses the X.** It is built to look as if it controls the machine.
4. **Find what the man on the phone wants: to see her screen.** That is the way in, and it is what the page was for.

This is **Tech-support scam**. Real technical support exists, and the scam copies it so that you will let someone into your device. The problem was made up.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Do not call the number, and do not press anything on the page.** Every button is part of the trick.
2. **If it will not close, close the browser from your computer’s own menu, or hold the power button.** Nothing is lost.
3. **If you already called and were asked to install something or share your screen, say no and hang up.** Refusing now costs you nothing.
4. **To be sure, use *the check*: call the company at the number on your bill, never one from a page, a message or a *search ad*.** That number was yours before the page appeared.

You may also hear this called “fake virus alert” or “scareware”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Tech-support scam**.

### 12. Refund scam

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 12 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-refundscam`]

The siren page started with a warning about your device. This one starts with money: a caller who says you are owed some, or that your bank account needs attention.

*The energy refund*

> A man calls Harold: 'This is Brightwatt Energy. Our meter checks show that ⟦we have overcharged you $312⟧, and I want to put that right today. Please open a web page and type in the number I read out, ⟦so that I can see your screen and send the refund⟧.' Harold had not asked about a refund.

Harold never asked for a refund, and the caller started it. Once the caller is watching, he asks Harold to log in to his online bank “to see where to send it”. He moves money between Harold’s own accounts so that the balance jumps to $3,120, says he typed an extra zero and will lose his job, and asks Harold to send the difference back. What Harold sends is his own money.

Harold could refuse at the request to see his computer. After that, everything happens on a page someone else controls. A real refund goes back to the card or account that paid, with no call and nobody needing to see your computer.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the reason: Harold was overcharged $312.** The reason is money: a refund, or a danger to your bank account.
2. **Check whether you asked for it: Harold had not asked about a refund.** A refund you never asked for is the bait.
3. **Find what he wants while he sorts it out: to see Harold’s screen.** A real refund needs nobody watching.
4. **Check for a problem with the device: there is none.** A problem with your device would make it **Tech-support scam**.

This is **Refund scam**. The refund, or the danger to your bank account, is only the reason to get at your computer and your bank.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **End the call at the first mention of watching your device, installing something or pressing Share.** You do not have to be polite or explain.
2. **Never type in a code they read out, press Share or log in to your bank while they are on the line.** Everything you do then happens where they can see it.
3. **Never send money back because of what a balance seems to say.** They made the balance change, so what you send is your own money.
4. **Use *the check*: call the company or your bank at the number on your bill or the back of your card, on a different phone if you can.** That number was yours before the call.
5. **If you already let them in, take the device off the internet and call your bank.** That cuts them off while the bank protects your accounts.

You may also hear this called “remote access scam” or “overpayment refund scam”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Refund scam**.

### 14. Tech-support scam or Refund scam: telling them apart

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 14 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-techsupport-refundscam`]

These two are the closest pair: in both, a person on the phone asks to see your device and says it is to put something right.

**Story A**

> Hal's laptop starts beeping, and a page says: 'Your computer is locked. Call the helpline at (800) 555-0172.' He calls, and a man says: 'I can fix this today, but I need to see your screen. Press Share in the meeting app and I will tell you what to click.'

## Part 3 of 3: The question, one whole story, then the drill

### 16. The question to ask every time

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 16 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-how`]

Here is the question and its four answers in one place.

**The question:** **“How did this start?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other three.

- **“Someone offering to fix a problem with your device”**
  - Give this answer when a warning, call, message or search ad says your device has a problem or offers to fix it, and the person you reach wants to install something or see your screen.
  - It leads to **Tech-support scam**.
- **“Someone sorting out a refund or your bank account”**
  - Give this answer when someone says you are owed a refund or your bank account has a problem, and asks you to install something or let them see your screen to sort it out.
  - It leads to **Refund scam**.
- **“A file or a link sent to you in a message”**
  - Give this answer when a file or link arrives in an email, text or chat with a reason to open or install it, like an invoice or a package note, and nobody is on a call with you.
  - It leads to **Malware**.
- **“You started it yourself, through a way you already had”**
  - Give this answer when you started it yourself through a way you already had, like your app store or the company’s number you looked up, and nobody contacted you first to ask for it.
  - It leads to **Real installation**.

**Why it matters**

Once a program is on your device, or someone can see your screen, they can watch everything you do there. Each of these four starts in its own way, so how it started is all you need to know, and you can answer before you install, open or share anything.

The program, the box, the company named and the story do not decide it, because the same program can be fetched by you or sent to you. The signs that come later (a file that runs quietly, a technician’s “proof”, a balance on a page someone else controls) show up only after the harm.

**How to answer it**

1. **Ask who started it.** If you did, from the maker’s own website or your app store, it is **Real installation**.
2. **If it came to you, look for a person on the phone.** A file or a link in a message, with nobody on the phone, is **Malware**.
3. **If someone is on the phone, listen to the reason they give.** A problem with your device is **Tech-support scam**, and a refund or your bank account is **Refund scam**.
4. **Find the exact words that show it: where you went, what arrived, what was offered.** If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.
5. **If you did not fetch it yourself, stop and use *the check*.** The next step is the same whichever scam it turns out to be.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Sometimes two answers both seem to fit. Each pair below has one question that separates it. If a story shows two at once, use the answer named under the pair.

- Malware or Real installation: Did you set out to get this program yourself, from its maker’s website or your app store? Or did a file or link arrive in a message?
- Tech-support scam or Refund scam: Why does the person want to see your screen: a problem with your device, or money, such as a refund or your bank account?
- Tech-support scam or Real installation: Who started this, and where did the number or address come from: something you already had, or a page, a message, a call or a search?
- Tech-support scam or Malware: Is someone on the phone, or a number to call, who will talk you through it? Or is there only a file or link for you to open?
- Malware or Refund scam: Is a person with you on the phone or in a chat, talking money: a refund owed to you, or trouble with your bank? Or did the file just arrive?
- Real installation or Refund scam: Who started this: did you set out to get it yourself, or did someone contact you first and ask you to do it?

### 19. What to carry away

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 19 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap`]

You can now tell all four apart on your own.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

How did this start?
- Someone offering to fix a problem with your device → Tech-support scam
- Someone sorting out a refund or your bank account → Refund scam
- A file or a link sent to you in a message → Malware
- You started it yourself, through a way you already had → Real installation

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Real installation**: an install, update or screen share you started yourself through a way you already had, like your app store, and nobody contacting you first to ask for it.
- **Malware**: a file or a link that came in a message, a reason to open, run or install it, and nobody on a call with you.
- **Tech-support scam**: a warning, call, message or search ad about a problem with your device, and the person you then reach wanting to install something or see your screen.
- **Refund scam**: someone saying you are owed a refund or your bank account has a problem, and asking you to install something or let them see your screen while they sort it out.

**To carry away**

- Before you install, open or share anything, ask how it started and find the words that show it: where you went, what arrived, what was offered. Do not wait to see what a file does or what a technician shows you.
- The “allow changes” box, the company’s name and the program’s name do not change the answer. A number or an address from search results, or from a *search ad*, is not *a way you already had*.
- When a call shows a fault and a refund together, the answer is the refund. For anything that came to you, the next step is the same: stop, and use *the check*.
- **Real installation** is one of the four. Treating every installation as a scam is a mistake too.

### 20. A plan, if you want one

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 20 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `plan`, id `plan`]

This card is for what you will do about it. Fill it in, or leave it.

A plan is one line: if I see this, then I will do that. Decide it now, because the moment a pop-up fills your window or a caller asks to see your computer is the worst moment to think. These are examples to start from: use one, change it, or write your own.

- a warning on my device that gives me a number to call, not call it, close the page or the browser, and call the company at the number on my bill if I want to be sure
- a caller or a message that says I am owed a refund, or that my bank account needs attention, and wants to see my device, end the call, and call the company or my bank myself, at the number on my bill or my card
- a file or a link in a message that I did not ask for, with a reason to open it, not open it, and ask the sender myself, through a way I already had
- a phone number or a link at the top of a search page, marked “Ad”, not use it, and use the number on my bill or an address that I have saved
- software that I want, get it only from the maker’s website through an address that I type or have saved, or from my device’s app store

Or write your own: If …, then I will …
