## Part 1 of 3: One choice, and words aimed at someone

### 1. Before you call it manipulation, check what you are looking at

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 1 of 15*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient-kind`]

Before you call someone “manipulative”, “a narcissist” or “just moody”, check what you are actually looking at. It is always one of the four things below, each needs different evidence, and most snap judgments skip this step.

A friend says her boyfriend is “manipulative”. A coworker says the new manager is “a narcissist”. Your brother snaps at dinner, and someone says he has always been “difficult”.

Each label jumps past a simple question: what did you actually see? One conversation, one bad week, or twenty years? Get that wrong and every word you pick after it is wrong too.

What are you looking at?
- A choice and its reasons → one person explaining a choice or a view of their own
- Something done to someone → one person saying or doing something to another, about them
- A lifelong pattern → the same behavior for years, everywhere, with everyone
- Just a one-off → one occasion or one short stretch, and nothing more

The unit has three parts, and you can stop after any of them.

1. One choice, and words aimed at someone
2. Years of the same, and one bad day
3. One whole story, then the drill

Each starts from a real story. After each one you answer a quick question, and the reason is shown right away. Nothing here is graded. A miss only decides what comes back.

### 2. A choice and its reasons

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 2 of 15*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-reasoning`]

First: a person explaining a choice of their own.

*The job offer*

> Leila has been offered a better-paid job in another city. She tells her sister, '⟦The money is better, but I'd lose two hours a day to the train and I'd hardly see the children. I'm turning it down.⟧'

Leila is explaining her own decision. Her sister just listens, and nothing Leila says is about her sister.

This is the most common thing you will see, and it is not a problem in itself. Whether her reasons are good is a separate question.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the choice that is theirs: Leila turns the job down.** Everything else in the story hangs on it.
2. **Find their reasons: the train and the children.** Reasons are what you judge a choice by.
3. **Check who the words are about: only Leila.** Words aimed at the listener are a different thing, covered next.

This is **“A choice and its reasons”**. Her sister could leave the room and nothing would change.

### 4. Something done to someone

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 4 of 15*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-tactic`]

Second: one person saying or doing something to another, about them.

*The deadline*

> On Tuesday Ben asks Carla why the client report went out late. ⟦Carla says she never agreed to that date⟧, although her own email from last week says 'Thursday is fine'. Then ⟦she tells Ben that he is the one who is always disorganized⟧. Ben goes back to his desk and starts checking his own calendar.

Carla is not explaining a choice of her own. Her words are aimed at Ben: first that he got the date wrong, then that he is the disorganized one. And they work: Ben goes back to his desk doubting himself.

This is not a verdict. Praise or an apology counts too. Whether it was fair is the next question, not this one.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the target: Ben.** There is always a person on the receiving end.
2. **Check the words are about him: he is the disorganized one.** Words about the speaker’s own choice would be **“A choice and its reasons”**.
3. **Look at what it did to him: he checks his own calendar.** The effect on the target shows where the words landed.

This is **“Something done to someone”**. Take Ben out of the story and nothing is left.

### 6. A choice and its reasons or Something done to someone: telling them apart

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 6 of 15*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-reasoning-tactic`]

Both of these can sound like someone explaining themselves. The test is who the words are about.

**Story A**

> Dev forgot his wife's birthday. That evening he tells his brother on the phone, 'I've been working twelve-hour days for a month. Anyone would have lost track of the date.'

**Story B**

> Dev forgot his wife's birthday. When she says she is hurt, he tells her, 'You're too sensitive. You always make a drama out of nothing.' She ends up apologizing for bringing it up.

**What to compare.** Both stories are about Dev and the birthday he forgot. Compare one thing: who his words are about, and who hears them.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Something done to someone”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A, Dev explains what he did, and the explanation is about Dev: a month of long days. His brother just listens. That is **“A choice and its reasons”**.

In Story B, Dev tells his wife she is too sensitive. The words are about her, said to her, and she ends up apologizing. That is **“Something done to someone”**.

How bad it sounds does not decide it. Who the words are about does.

**How to tell them apart**

Who are the words about: the speaker’s own choice, or the person they are said to?

## Part 2 of 3: Years of the same, and one bad day

### 7. A lifelong pattern

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 7 of 15*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-pattern`]

Third: the same behavior for years, everywhere, with everyone. People claim this one often and can show it rarely.

*Twenty years of Moira*

> ⟦In twenty years⟧ Moira has never once said 'I was wrong'. ⟦At three different firms⟧, every project of hers that failed was somebody else's fault. ⟦Her two brothers and her oldest friends tell the same story⟧ about family vacations and shared apartments.

Nobody in Moira’s story is having one conversation. You are looking at twenty years, three firms and her whole family, and the same thing in all of them: nothing is ever her fault.

This is the biggest claim you can make about a person. Most of the time you have seen one evening or one job, and that is not enough.

**How to spot it**

1. **Count the years: twenty.** A bad stretch lasts weeks or months, not decades.
2. **Count the places: three firms, family trips, shared apartments.** One place could just be one bad workplace.
3. **Count the people: coworkers, brothers, old friends.** One person could just be one bad relationship.
4. **Call it a pattern only if all three repeat.** Then the only thing left to explain it is the person.

This is **“A lifelong pattern”**. It is not a diagnosis: only a trained professional can give one.

### 9. Something done to someone or A lifelong pattern: telling them apart

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 9 of 15*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-tactic-pattern`]

People mix these up in one direction: they see one thing done to someone and decide what the person is like for life.

**Story A**

> On Friday Gina's manager, Paul, presented her idea to the directors as his own. When she raised it with him, he told her she must be confused, and that he had mentioned the idea to her first. Gina went home wondering whether he had.

**Story B**

> Paul has taken the credit for other people's work in every job he has held. Two firms let him go over it in his thirties. His sister says he did the same with her school projects, and friends from his soccer club tell the same story about a tournament he says he organized.

**What to compare.** Both stories are about Paul taking credit for someone else’s work. Compare one thing: does the story stay between two people, or follow Paul for years?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“A lifelong pattern”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

Story A is one Friday between Paul and Gina: he takes her idea, then tells her she is confused. That is **“Something done to someone”**.

Story B never mentions Gina. It follows Paul through every job, his sister and his soccer club. That is **“A lifelong pattern”**.

Story A tells you what Paul did to Gina. It does not tell you what Paul is like.

**How to tell them apart**

Does the story stay between two people, or follow one person for years, in different places, with different people?


### 10. Just a one-off

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 10 of 15*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-none`]

Fourth: a one-off. Most of what you see in a single day is this.

*The week of the diagnosis*

> ⟦On Monday Amira learned that her father is seriously ill. All week she has been quiet at work and short with anyone who asks her a question⟧, and twice she has gone home early. On Friday a colleague says, 'What's wrong with her? She's so moody.'

Amira is not explaining a choice, and she is not targeting anyone: she is short with whoever asks her anything. And it is one week, with an obvious cause. She is having a hard week.

The coworker’s word, "moody", sounds like it describes Amira. It really describes five days. A word like "unstable" is worse: a hard week shows nothing a diagnosis needs.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find how long it lasts: one week.** One occasion or one short stretch can’t show a pattern.
2. **Look for a cause: her father’s illness.** A clear cause usually means it will pass.
3. **Check there is no target and no reasons.** Then there is nothing more to name.

This is **“Just a one-off”**. It is a real answer, and you will need it often.

### 13. The one question to ask first

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 13 of 15*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-kind`]

Here is the question and its four answers in one place.

**The question:** **“What are you looking at?”**

**Its answers**

- **“A choice and its reasons”**
  - Give this answer when one person explains a choice, a view or an act of their own, and nobody else is the target.
- **“Something done to someone”**
  - Give this answer when one person says or does something to another, about that person or about what happened between them.
- **“A lifelong pattern”**
  - Give this answer when the same behavior shows up for years, in different places and with different people.
- **“Just a one-off”**
  - Give this answer when one person acts a certain way on one occasion or for a short stretch, often after something happened to them, and there is nothing more to it.

**Why it matters**

Each needs different evidence before you can judge it. A choice is judged by its reasons, something done to someone by what it did to them, and a pattern only by many years. A one-off needs no label at all.

Get this wrong and everything after it goes wrong. Take one evening for a whole personality, and you go looking for years that are not there. Take words aimed at someone for the speaker’s own reasons, and you miss the person they hurt.

**How to answer it**

1. **Read to the end before you answer.** The years are often in the last sentence.
2. **First look for years, places and people: “A lifelong pattern”.** When a story shows the biggest claim, the biggest claim wins.
3. **Next look for a target: “Something done to someone”.** Words aimed at someone matter more than the speaker’s reasons.
4. **Next look for the person’s own reasons: “A choice and its reasons”.** That is what is left when nobody is targeted.
5. **None of these? It is “Just a one-off”.** Then there is nothing more to name.
6. **Find the exact words that show your answer.** If you can’t find them, you don’t have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Some stories show two at once: an excuse for your own lateness that blames the person you say it to, or one evening with twenty years behind it. Use the order above. The test for each pair is below.

- A choice and its reasons or Something done to someone: Who are the words about: the speaker’s own choice, or the person they are said to?
- Something done to someone or A lifelong pattern: Does the story stay between two people, or follow one person for years, in different places, with different people?
- A lifelong pattern or Just a one-off: How much of the person’s life does the story show: one occasion, or years, places and people?
- Something done to someone or Just a one-off: Is something said or done to one particular person, about them? Or is it just how someone was for a while, with everyone?
- A choice and its reasons or Just a one-off: Does the person give reasons for a choice or a view, or does the story only show how they felt and acted?
- A choice and its reasons or A lifelong pattern: Is it one choice on one occasion, or years of someone’s life?

## Part 3 of 3: One whole story, then the drill

### 14. One whole story, where the start points the wrong way

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 14 of 15*

[reviewers only: card kind `worked`, id `worked-rehearsal`]

Watch one story worked through. The first thing you notice is not what decides it, so read to the end.

*The wedding rehearsal*

> Petra missed her sister's wedding rehearsal. 'The traffic was impossible,' she said, 'and nobody told me the time had changed.' Her sister was not surprised. In the twenty years since they left home, Petra has missed birthdays, two graduations and her own farewell parties at three different jobs, and each time there has been a reason.

**Question 1 of 1: What are you looking at?**

> Petra missed her sister's wedding rehearsal. 'The traffic was impossible,' she said, 'and nobody told me the time had changed.' Her sister was not surprised. ⟦In the twenty years since they left home⟧, Petra has missed ⟦birthdays, two graduations and her own farewell parties at three different jobs⟧, and each time there has been a reason.

Answer: **“A lifelong pattern”**

It opens with one missed rehearsal and Petra’s reasons for it. If it stopped there, it would be **“A choice and its reasons”**.

It does not stop there: “In the twenty years since they left home” and “birthdays, two graduations and her own farewell parties at three different jobs”. That is twenty years, family events and three jobs, and the same thing every time.

**You are asked:** Petra gives reasons for missing the rehearsal, so this can look like **“A choice and its reasons”**. What decides it? All of these are true. Choose the one that settles it.

- (a) Petra gives reasons for missing the rehearsal: the traffic, and not being told the time.
- (b) The same thing has happened for twenty years, at family events and at three jobs, each time with a reason.
- (c) Her sister was not surprised.

**Shown as soon as you choose.** The one that settles it is (b): The same thing has happened for twenty years, at family events and at three jobs, each time with a reason.
- If you chose (a): True, and it is why this looks like **“A choice and its reasons”**. If the story ended there, that would be the answer.
- If you chose (c): True, but it is one person’s reaction on one day. The history itself is in the next sentence.

**Why this is A lifelong pattern and not A choice and its reasons**

The first half shows a person giving reasons. The second half shows the same thing for twenty years. When a story shows both, the bigger claim wins: **“A lifelong pattern”**.

Her excuse on the day may even be true. It does not change the answer.

**Does it look like a story you know?**

A second look: does this remind you of a story you know? A missed occasion and a ready excuse may bring back Dev and the forgotten birthday, which was **“A choice and its reasons”**.

When a likeness and the answer disagree, go back to the words that answer the question: “In the twenty years since they left home” and “birthdays, two graduations and her own farewell parties at three different jobs”. Dev’s story has nothing like them. Moira’s does, so the answer stands.

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **“A choice and its reasons”**: a choice, a view or an act that is the person’s own, and their reasons for it.
- **“Something done to someone”**: two people, and one of them saying or doing something to the other about that person or about what happened between them.
- **“A lifelong pattern”**: years, more than one place and more than one relationship, and the same behavior in all of them.
- **“Just a one-off”**: one occasion or one short stretch, how the person felt or acted in it, and nothing more: no reasons for a choice, nobody targeted, no years.

**To carry away**

- Before you use any label, ask what you are looking at, and find the words that show it.
- None of the four is a verdict or a diagnosis. Each says only what the story shows.
- One evening is never **“A lifelong pattern”**, however bad it was and whoever says "always". Count the years, the places and the people.
- A hard week after something real is **“Just a one-off”**. It does not need a bigger word.
- When a story shows two, use the order: years, places and people first; then a target; then the person’s own reasons.
