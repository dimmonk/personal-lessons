## Part 1 of 2: Stay out, or pay for a fair start

### 1. Everyone deserves a fair shot. But what should the government do about it?

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

When a speech, petition or letter says everyone deserves rights and fair treatment, you can tell what it is actually asking the government to do: stay out of the way, pay for a fair start, or change a rule. The three sound alike and ask for very different things.

You have probably heard all three in one week. One person says, "Just protect people’s rights and leave them alone." Another says, "Rights are no use to a child with no school, so the government should pay for one." A third says, "The rules are the same for everyone, and that is exactly the problem."

All three say every person has rights and should be treated fairly. They do not disagree about that. They disagree about what to do.

Each of the three leads to a name. The names describe what a text asks for. They are not insults or compliments, and a text can ask for any of them whether or not you agree.

Every text in this unit is invented, and the unit takes no side. It teaches you to read what a text asks for.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“Whose side is it on?”** Its answers:

- **“Workers against owners”**: give this answer when the text splits people into those who work for a wage and those who own the businesses (or got rich owning them), and takes the workers’ side.
- **“The nation, or its ordinary people”**: give this answer when the text speaks for one people, marked by its country, culture or blood, and puts it first: the whole nation as one, or its ordinary people against a few at the top.
- **“The old ways”**: give this answer when the text holds up ways handed down from the past (a faith, home life, old customs, or an old order of crown, church and rank) as what should guide the country.
- **“Everyone’s rights”**: give this answer when the text puts first what every person is owed: the freedom to speak, believe, own and trade, a fair start in life, or fair treatment whatever their group. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“No side at all”**: give this answer when the text only says who holds power and how they keep it, or how to handle one practical matter, and takes none of the other four sides.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. Classical liberalism

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-clib`]

Start with the answer that asks the least of the government.

*The street-music petition*

> From a petition by the Lowfield Street Musicians: 'Every person has the right to play, to speak and to sell what they make on a public street. ⟦The council has its proper jobs: the police who keep the peace, the courts that settle disputes and the fire service that answers a call⟧. Licensing who may sing is not one of them. ⟦Protect our rights, and then leave us alone⟧.'

The musicians want to play, speak and sell on the street, and they want the council to stay out of it. A council is a local government, and "the government" is the word this unit uses for any of them. They are happy for the council to keep a few jobs: the police, the courts and the fire service. They only ask that it stick to those and not decide who may sing.

A few jobs does not mean none. The musicians would be upset to lose the police, the courts or the fire service.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find what people are free to do: play, speak and sell on a public street.** This answer puts freedom first.
2. **List the government’s jobs: police, courts, fire service.** The list is short, and every job protects people.
3. **Check what it asks the government to give: no money, no service, no help.** A text that wants something given is the next answer.

This is **Classical liberalism**. "Liberal" comes from an old word for free, and "classical" means the older form of that view.

You may also hear this called “libertarianism” or “market liberalism”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Classical liberalism**.

### 4. Modern liberalism

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-modlib`]

The first answer asks the government to protect rights and then step back. This one asks it to do more.

*The fair-start leaflet*

> From a leaflet of the Ashgrove Fair Start Group: 'Each person has the right to speak, to believe and to keep what they earn, and ⟦the government must protect those rights⟧. But a right means little to a child who begins life with no school within reach and no doctor to call. ⟦We ask the government to give everyone a fair start: a school in every district, health care for anyone who is ill, help for anyone who loses work, and fair rules for the businesses that sell to us⟧. We will all pay for it together, through our taxes.'

The leaflet starts where the street musicians did: each person has rights, and the government should protect them. Then it goes further. A child with no school nearby and no doctor to call cannot really use those rights, since a child who cannot read cannot use the right to speak.

So the leaflet asks the government to pay for a school, health care and help for people who lose work, and everyone pays in through taxes.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the rights it wants protected: to speak, to believe and to keep what you earn.** This part matches the first answer.
2. **Find what it wants the government to give: a school in every district, health care, help when out of work.** Here something is given, not only protected.
3. **Find who pays: everyone, through taxes.** The cost is shared by all.
4. **Check that no rule is blamed for holding a group back.** The leaflet sets no one against anyone, and a text that blames a rule is the next answer.

This is **Modern liberalism**. "Liberal" still means each person’s freedom comes first, and "modern" means the newer form of that view, which also asks for a fair start. The everyday word "liberal" means different things in different places, so go by what the text asks for.

You may also hear this called “social liberalism” or “liberal, as Americans use the word”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Modern liberalism**.

### 6. Classical liberalism or Modern liberalism: telling them apart

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 6 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-clib-modlib`]

Both start from each person’s rights. Here are two speakers at the same meeting.

**Story A**

> At a meeting in Marrow about the new clinic, one speaker said: 'Every person is free to choose their own doctor and to pay for their own care. The government should keep the courts open and see that contracts are kept, and otherwise leave the clinic to those who run it.'

**Story B**

> At the same meeting in Marrow about the new clinic, another speaker said: 'Every person is free to choose their own doctor. But freedom to choose means little when there is no clinic to go to, so the government should pay for one in every district, and we should all pay for it through our taxes.'

**What to compare.** Both speakers are talking about the same new clinic in Marrow, and both say each person is free to choose their own doctor. Compare one thing: what the speaker wants the government to do about the clinic.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Protect their rights, and give everyone a fair start”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the speaker wants the government to keep the courts open and leave the clinic to those who run it. That is **“Protect their rights, and otherwise leave them alone”**, so it is **Classical liberalism**.

In Story B the speaker wants the government to pay for a clinic in every district, with everyone paying through taxes. A clinic is given. That is **“Protect their rights, and give everyone a fair start”**, so it is **Modern liberalism**.

The freedom to choose a doctor is the same in both. What differs is what is asked of the government after that: nothing more, or something given.

**How to tell them apart**

Does the text ask the government only to protect rights and then stay out? Or does it also ask the government to give people something, like a school, a doctor or help while out of work?

## Part 2 of 2: Changing a rule, when another answer wins, then the drill

### 7. When the same rules leave someone out

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 7 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-equity`]

Before the third answer, one word you will need. A story first.

*The town-hall steps*

> The Fennmoor town hall has a stone stair at its only entrance. The clerk says, 'Anyone may come in and apply for a permit, and the rules are exactly the same for each person.' Rafael uses a wheelchair and cannot get up the stair. The council builds a ramp at the side door, and only the people who need it will use it.

The clerk is telling the truth: the rules are exactly the same for each person. But Rafael cannot get up the stair, so the same rules do not get him in. The council fixes it by treating him differently, on purpose: a ramp at the side door.

**The word for this.** *Equity*: treating groups differently where needed so that results come out fair for each of them, instead of giving everyone the same rules.

A rule can treat everyone alike and still leave one person outside. Looking at the result, and treating people differently until it is fair, is *equity*. People argue about when that is the right thing to do. This card only gives you the word, so you can spot when a text asks for it.


### 8. Group equality

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 8 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-idegal`]

The first two answers are happy with rules that treat everyone alike. This one is not: it says a rule that is the same for everyone has left a group behind.

*The hill-villages letter*

> From an open letter by parents in the Tessel hill villages: 'The county school's entry test is the same for every child: one paper, one day, one room in the city. No rule names the hill villages. Yet in ten years no child from the villages has passed, because the only bus reaches the city after the test begins. ⟦Rules that treat every child alike have left our children behind⟧. ⟦We ask that the rules be changed, with a test day held in the villages, until results come out as fair for the villages as for the city⟧. We do not ask for anyone to be placed above anyone.'

Nobody wrote this test to keep the villages out. It is the same paper, day and room for every child. But the only bus reaches the city after the test begins, so in ten years no child from the villages has passed. A rule that treats everyone alike can still give very different results when people do not start from the same place.

The first answer would call the test fair because it is the same for all. The second would pay for a bus or a tutor and leave the test alone. These parents say the test itself is the problem and ask for it to change. That is asking for *equity*, though the letter never uses the word.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find what is the same for everyone: one paper, one day, one room in the city.** It names no group, so it looks fair.
2. **Find the group with different results: no child from the villages has passed in ten years.** The unfairness shows in the results.
3. **Check that it blames the rules: "Rules that treat every child alike have left our children behind."** Blaming the rules is what sets this answer apart.
4. **Find what it asks for: change the rules, with a test day held in the villages.** The ask is a changed rule, not a gift.

This is **Group equality**. "Group" because the text is about groups of people, and "equality" because it asks for fair results across groups, with no group placed above another.

You may also hear this called “identity politics” or “social justice” or “identity-egalitarianism”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Group equality**.

### 12. The question to ask of every text about rights

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 12 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-does`]

The question and its three answers, in one place.

**The question:** **“What does it want the government to do for people?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other two.

- **“Protect their rights, and otherwise leave them alone”**
  - Give this answer when the text wants each person free to speak, believe, own and trade, and the government kept to a few jobs like courts, police and defense, or asks nothing of it.
  - It leads to **Classical liberalism**.
- **“Protect their rights, and give everyone a fair start”**
  - Give this answer when the text wants each person’s rights protected, and the government to give everyone a fair start, like schools, health care or help when out of work.
  - It leads to **Modern liberalism**.
- **“Change the rules that hold some groups back”**
  - Give this answer when the text says rules that treat everyone the same still leave some groups behind, by race, sex or origin, and wants them changed until results are fair.
  - It leads to **Group equality**.

**Why it matters**

All three put people’s rights first and disagree about what people are owed: freedom from a government that does too much, a fair start the government pays for, or fair results for groups the rules leave behind.

Three speakers can all put rights first, all talk about the same clinic, and still want three different things. The topic, the speaker and how strongly they feel do not tell you which. Only what they ask the government to do does.

**How to answer it**

1. **Find the sentence where the text says what it wants done.** The ask tells the three apart; the rights do not.
2. **Listen for "leave us alone" or "keep out": that is probably “Protect their rights, and otherwise leave them alone”.** The text wants the government to stay out of the way.
3. **Listen for "give", "pay for" or "provide": that is probably “Protect their rights, and give everyone a fair start”.** The text wants the government to hand something over.
4. **Listen for rules that "treat everyone alike" and have left a group behind: that is probably “Change the rules that hold some groups back”.** Blaming a rule is what sets this answer apart.
5. **Keep the two ends apart: Classical liberalism says the same rules for everyone are enough, and Group equality says they leave a group behind.** These two disagree the most.
6. **Do not go by the topic: a text about a school may want the government to stay out of it, pay for it, or change a rule about it.** A service or a group can be mentioned without being asked for.
7. **Put your finger on the exact words that show your answer.** If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Some texts show two answers at once. If a text asks for a fair start for everyone and also blames a rule that treats everyone alike for leaving a group behind, the answer is **“Change the rules that hold some groups back”**. The test for each pair is below.

- Classical liberalism or Modern liberalism: Does the text ask the government only to protect rights and then stay out? Or does it also ask the government to give people something, like a school, a doctor or help while out of work?
- Modern liberalism or Group equality: Does the text blame a rule that treats everyone alike for leaving a group behind? Or does it ask for the same help for everyone and blame no rule?
- Classical liberalism or Group equality: Does the text say the same rules for everyone are enough? Or does it say they leave a group behind and must change?

### 14. A fair start for everyone, and mill hands against owners

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 14 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `exception`, id `exc-class`]

Sometimes the first question overrules this one. This text says everyone is owed a fair start, and also sets working people against owners.

*The mill hands’ leaflet*

> From a leaflet by the Pell Hill mill hands: 'Everyone is owed a fair start: a school, a doctor, and help when the work runs out. ### 18. What to carry away

*Unit Five · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 18 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap`]

The unit in one place.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What does it want the government to do for people?
- Protect their rights, and otherwise leave them alone → Classical liberalism
- Protect their rights, and give everyone a fair start → Modern liberalism
- Change the rules that hold some groups back → Group equality

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Classical liberalism**: each person’s freedom to speak, believe, own and trade put first, and the government kept to a few jobs like courts, police and defense, or asked for nothing.
- **Modern liberalism**: each person’s rights put first, plus the government giving everyone a fair start, like schools and health care, and no group said to be held back by the rules.
- **Group equality**: groups by race or sex, rules that treat everyone the same yet leave some groups behind, a call to change them until results are fair, and no group put above another.

**To carry away**

- Ask what the text wants the government to do, and find the words that say it. All three put rights first, so rights do not tell them apart. The ask does: **“Protect their rights, and otherwise leave them alone”**, **“Protect their rights, and give everyone a fair start”** or **“Change the rules that hold some groups back”**.
- The topic never decides. A text about a school may want the government to stay out of it, to pay for it, or to change a rule about it.
- If a text asks for a fair start and also blames a rule that treats everyone alike for leaving a group behind, the answer is **“Change the rules that hold some groups back”**.
- If it also sets working people against owners, holds up old customs as the guide, or puts one people first, the first question decides it, and the name is not one of this unit’s.
