### 2. Linear growth

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 2 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-lin`]

First: an amount that gets the same top-up, or the same cut, every time.

*The toddler’s jar*

> A toddler has a jar with $12 in it. ⟦Every week her grandmother puts in $3 more⟧, and nothing is ever taken out. How much will the jar hold after 10 weeks?

The jar holds $15 after week 1, $18 after week 2 and $21 after week 3. Each week adds the same $3, whether the jar holds $12 or $120.

So over 10 weeks the jar gains 10 times $3, and you add that to the start. The same steps work backward: to find how long until a target, divide the distance to it by $3.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the one amount that changes: the money in the jar.** Everything else in the problem is about this one amount.
2. **Find the change each time, and check it is the same size: $3 every week.** A plain figure beside “every week”, “every day” or “every month” is the same size each time.
3. **Find what it asks: the amount after 10 weeks.** You can ask for the amount later or for the time to a target, and the steps are the same.

This is **Linear growth**. Drawn week by week, the jar’s amount makes a straight line, because every week adds the same $3.

You may also hear this called “straight-line growth” or “a flat rate”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Linear growth**.

### 4. Worked: a drip bag emptying to 250 ml

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 4 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `solved`, id `solved-lin-2`]

Here are the steps for **Linear growth** with real numbers, worked backward: the target is given, and the time is what is missing. The amount goes down instead of up.

**The problem**

> A hospital drip bag holds 1,000 ml of fluid at the start, and it empties by 125 ml every hour. After how many hours will 250 ml be left in the bag?

**The working, step by step**

- Find where it starts and how much it changes each time: Start: 1,000 ml. Each hour it goes down by 125 ml

The bag starts at 1,000 ml and loses 125 ml every hour. The problem gives a target, 250 ml, instead of a time, so the number of hours is what is missing.

- Find how much it must change in all to reach the target: 1,000 − 250 = 750 ml to be taken away

The bag has to go from 1,000 ml down to 250 ml, so 750 ml must go.

- Divide that by how much it changes each time: 750 ml ÷ 125 ml = 6 hours

**You are asked:** This step carries the idea. Every statement below is true of the problem. Before you read the reason, choose the one that explains why this step is done.

- The hours times 125 make the total change, so the hours are the total divided by 125.
- 750 ÷ 125 equals 6, and 6 hours is the answer.
- The bag starts at 1,000 ml, and 250 ml is the target.

**Shown as soon as you answer**

- The one that explains it: The hours times 125 make the total change, so the hours are the total divided by 125.
  - If you chose “750 ÷ 125 equals 6, and 6 hours is the answer.”: That is true, but it is the result of the step, not the reason for dividing.
  - If you chose “The bag starts at 1,000 ml, and 250 ml is the target.”: That is true, but it is where the working began, not why you divide by 125.

Each hour takes away 125 ml, so the total taken away is 125 × the number of hours. You know the total, 750 ml, and not the hours: 125 × hours = 750. A multiplication is undone by a division, so 750 ÷ 125 = 6.

Check by going forward: 6 hours take away 6 × 125 = 750 ml, and 1,000 − 750 = 250 ml, which is the target.

**The result**

After 6 hours the drip bag holds 250 ml. Check by going forward: 1,000 − 125 × 6 = 250.


### 5. Going up 5% means multiplying by 1.05

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 5 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-multiplier`]

A percentage change needs one word, for the number you multiply by. Here it is first, with a price.

*The bookshop novel*

> A bookshop sells a novel for $20. In January the owner puts the price up by 5%, and she works the new price out in two ways. First she takes 5% of $20, which is $1, and adds it: $20 + $1 = $21. Second she multiplies $20 by 1.05, and also gets $21. In the summer sale the same novel goes down by 15%, and she multiplies $20 by 0.85 and gets $17.

Going up 5% leaves a price at 105% of what it was, which is 1.05 times as much. Going down 15% leaves 85%, which is 0.85 times as much. Doubling is multiplying by 2, and halving is multiplying by 0.5.

To find the number, start from 100%. Add the percentage if the amount goes up, or take it away if it goes down, then write the result as a decimal: 100% + 5% = 105% = 1.05, and 100% − 15% = 85% = 0.85.

**The word for this.** *Multiplier*: the number an amount is multiplied by each time: going up 5% is a multiplier of 1.05, going down 15% is a multiplier of 0.85, and doubling is a multiplier of 2.

The number you multiply by is called the *multiplier*. Above 1 it makes the amount bigger, and below 1 it makes it smaller.


### 6. Exponential growth

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 6 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-expg`]

Next: an amount that changes by a percentage of itself each time.

*The savings account*

> A saver puts $2,000 into an account that ⟦pays 4% interest a year⟧. She ⟦leaves all the interest in the account⟧. How much will she have after 3 years?

In year 1 the interest is 4% of $2,000, which is $80, so the account holds $2,080. In year 2 it is 4% of $2,080, which is $83.20, so the account holds $2,163.20. The second year’s interest is bigger because it is taken on more money.

So each change is a share of what the amount is now, and going up 4% means multiplying by 1.04, once for each year. A doubling works the same way, and so does an amount that halves. The word “percent” does not decide it, and neither does “interest” or money. What decides it is that each change is a share of the amount.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the one amount that changes: the money in the account.** Everything else in the problem is about this one amount.
2. **Find the change each time: 4% a year.** A percentage or a doubling is a share of the amount, not a fixed number.
3. **Check the share is taken on what the account holds now: the interest is left in.** That is why each change is bigger than the one before.
4. **Find what it asks: the amount after 3 years.** A time and a missing amount means multiplying that many times.

This is **Exponential growth**. “Exponential” means growing by repeated multiplying: the amount is multiplied again, once for each time it changes.

You may also hear this called “compound growth” or “interest on interest” or “exponential decay”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Exponential growth**.

### 8. Worked: orders at a bakery after 4 weeks

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 8 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `solved`, id `solved-expg-1`]

Here are the steps for **Exponential growth** with real numbers: a bakery whose orders grow 20% every week, with every step written out.

**The problem**

> A bakery takes 250 orders this week, and the number of orders grows by 20% every week. About how many orders will it take after 4 weeks?

**The working, step by step**

- Turn the change into the number you multiply by each time: Up 20% each week: 100% + 20% = 120%, which is 1.2

Up 20% leaves the orders at 120% of what they were, which is 1.2 as a decimal. So every week the orders are multiplied by 1.2, and that is the *multiplier*.

- Multiply the start by it once for each time the amount changes: Week 1: 250 × 1.2 = 300; Week 2: 300 × 1.2 = 360; Week 3: 360 × 1.2 = 432; Week 4: 432 × 1.2 = 518.4

**You are asked:** This step carries the idea. Every statement below is true of the problem. Before you read the reason, choose the one that explains why this step is done.

- Each week’s 20% is taken on that week’s orders, so you multiply the latest result, not the start.
- 250 × 1.2 = 300 is the first line.
- There are 4 lines, one for each week.

**Shown as soon as you answer**

- The one that explains it: Each week’s 20% is taken on that week’s orders, so you multiply the latest result, not the start.
  - If you chose “250 × 1.2 = 300 is the first line.”: That is true, but it does not say why the next line starts from 300 and not from 250.
  - If you chose “There are 4 lines, one for each week.”: That is true, but it does not say why each line uses the line before.

A 20% rise is 20% of that week’s orders. In week 1 that is 20% of 250, which is 50, so orders reach 300. In week 2 it is 20% of 300, which is 60, so they reach 360. The rise grew from 50 to 60 because the 20% was taken on a bigger number. Multiplying by 1.2 takes that 20% and adds it in one go.

Multiplying 250 by 1.2 every week would forget that week 2’s rise is on 300. Adding 50 four times, 250 + 4 × 50 = 450, makes the mistake from the other side, and comes out 68 too low.

- Round at the end, and give the answer: 518.4 rounds to 518 orders, which is the answer after 4 weeks

Orders are whole numbers, so round to 518. Round only now: rounding every week would knock the later weeks a little off.

**The result**

After 4 weeks the bakery takes about 518 orders a week. Adding 50 every week instead would give 450, which is 68 too few.


### 9. Linear growth or Exponential growth: telling them apart

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 9 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-lin-expg`]

Both follow an amount that changes again and again, and for a while the numbers are close. Here they are side by side.

**Problem A**

> A shop’s website had 2,000 visitors in its first week, and it gains 100 more visitors every week. How many visitors will it have after 6 weeks?

**Problem B**

> A shop’s website had 2,000 visitors in its first week, and its visitors grow by 5% every week. How many visitors will it have after 6 weeks?

**What to compare.** Both problems have the same shop, the same 2,000 visitors and the same “every week”. Compare one thing: is the change the same number each time, or a share of what there is?

**You are asked:** Which problem gives the answer **“It is multiplied by the same number each time”**? (Problem A / Problem B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Problem B.

**Why this one and not the other**

The first problem says the website gains 100 more visitors every week: the same number each time, so the answer is **“It goes up or down by the same number each time”**. The second says its visitors grow by 5% every week: a share of what there is, so the answer is **“It is multiplied by the same number each time”**.

In week 1 they look the same, because 5% of 2,000 is 100. By week 6 the first has 2,000 + 6 × 100 = 2,600 visitors and the second has about 2,680. After 30 weeks it is 5,000 against about 8,644. What differs is whether next week’s rise is still 100, or 5% of a bigger amount.

**How to tell them apart**

Is the amount changed by the same number each time, such as $50 a month, or by the same share of itself each time, such as 5% a month or a doubling?


### 10. A percentage, and still the same number each year

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 10 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `exception`, id `exc-interest-out`]

Most percentages mean **Exponential growth**. This problem has a percentage and is **Linear growth** all the same.

*The bond that pays out*

> A man buys a bond for $5,000 that pays 3% interest a year. The interest is paid out to him each year, and the $5,000 itself never changes. How much interest will he have been paid in total after 8 years?

This problem has the usual signs of **Exponential growth**: 3% interest a year, money, years. But it is **Linear growth**.

**You are asked:** This looks like **Exponential growth**. Before you read why it is **Linear growth**, tap the words in the problem that settle it.

The pieces you can tap:
1. “A man buys a bond for $5,000 that pays 3% interest a year.”
2. “The interest is paid out to him each year, and the $5,000 itself never changes.”
3. “How much interest will he have been paid in total after 8 years?”

**Shown as soon as you tap.** The words are “The interest is paid out to him each year, and the $5,000 itself never changes.”.
- If you tapped “A man buys a bond for $5,000 that pays 3% interest a year.”: This has a percentage in it, so it looks like **Exponential growth**. The words that settle it are about what happens to the interest.
- If you tapped “How much interest will he have been paid in total after 8 years?”: That is the question. The words that say how the amount changes each time come before it.

**Why this is Linear growth and not Exponential growth**

The words that settle it are “The interest is paid out to him each year, and the $5,000 itself never changes.” Every year the bond pays 3% of the same $5,000, which is $150. The total paid grows by the same $150 a year: after 8 years, 8 × 150 = $1,200.

In the savings account the interest stayed in, so each year’s share was taken on more money. Here it is paid out, so every change is the same size.

**How to tell them apart**

Is the amount changed by the same number each time, such as $50 a month, or by the same share of itself each time, such as 5% a month or a doubling?

When a problem has a percentage, ask what it is a percentage of. If the amount grows, it is **Exponential growth**. If the amount stays the same, every change is the same size, and it is **Linear growth**.

### 11. Logarithm

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 11 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-logsolve`]

Same amount, turned around: you are given the target, and the time is what is missing.

*The bacteria dish*

> A lab dish holds 500 bacteria, and the number grows by 20% every hour. ⟦How many hours will it take to reach 1,000 bacteria?⟧

This is the same sort of amount as in the savings account: it is multiplied by 1.2 every hour. But the savings problem gave the time and asked for the amount. This one gives the amount to reach and asks for the time.

You could multiply and count: 600 after 1 hour, 720 after 2, 864 after 3, 1,036.8 after 4. The dish passes 1,000 during the fourth hour. For 20 or 30 hours that takes too long, so you count with the log button on a calculator.

The words “how long” do not decide it, because an amount that has the same number added every hour can be asked about in the same words. What decides it is that each change is a share of the amount, and that the problem gives a target instead of a time.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the amount and how it changes: the bacteria, up 20% every hour.** A percentage or a doubling means the amount is multiplied each time.
2. **Find the target: 1,000 bacteria.** A target given means the time is what is missing.
3. **Check the question asks for a time: “How many hours will it take…”.** The missing number is a number of hours, not a number of bacteria.

This is **Logarithm**. It is named for the log button that finds it, which answers “how many times must I multiply by 1.2 to reach 2?”

You may also hear this called “doubling time” or “the log function”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Logarithm**.

### 13. A chart where each gridline is ten times the last

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 13 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-logscale`]

The worked problem coming up counts multiplications with logs, and logs come from a chart you may not know. Here it is first, with a museum chart.

*The museum chart*

> A museum draws a chart of animal weights. The gridlines up the side are labeled 1 g, 10 g, 100 g, 1,000 g and 10,000 g, and they are drawn the same distance apart. A mouse of 10 g sits on the 10 g gridline. A hen of 1,000 g sits two gridlines above the mouse, so a visitor who reads the chart works out that the hen is 10 × 10 = 100 times as heavy.

On an ordinary chart, equal spaces mean equal amounts: the gridlines might read 0, 10, 20, 30. On this chart, equal spaces mean equal multiples: each gridline is 10 times the one below, so the labels go 1, 10, 100, 1,000, 10,000. Going up one gridline multiplies by 10, going up two multiplies by 100, and going up three multiplies by 1,000.

**The word for this.** *Log scale*: a chart scale where each equal gap means ten times as much, so 1, 10, 100 and 1,000 sit evenly spaced.

So a *log scale* draws repeated multiplying: every gridline up is one more multiplication by 10. Watch out: an amount that is multiplied each time draws as a straight line on one, so very fast growth can look calm. Read the labels up the side before you judge a chart.


### 14. Worked: how many years until $1,500 becomes $3,000

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 14 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `solved`, id `solved-logsolve-1`]

Here are the steps for **Logarithm** with real numbers: an account where the interest stays in, and the question is how long until it holds a target.

**The problem**

> A saver has $1,500 in an account that pays 6% interest a year, and she leaves all the interest in the account. After how many years will the account hold $3,000?

**The working, step by step**

- Turn the change into the number you multiply by each time: Up 6% each year: 100% + 6% = 106%, which is 1.06

Up 6% leaves the account at 106% of what it was, which is 1.06 times as much. Every year it is multiplied by 1.06, and that is the *multiplier*.

- Divide the target by the start, to see how many times bigger it must get: $3,000 ÷ $1,500 = 2

Only how many times bigger the target is matters, not the dollar amounts. So the question becomes: how many times must 1.06 be multiplied to make 2?

- Use the log button: divide the log of that number by the log of the number you multiply by: log 2 = 0.3010 and log 1.06 = 0.0253, so 0.3010 ÷ 0.0253 = 11.90

**You are asked:** This step carries the idea. Every statement below is true of the problem. Before you read the reason, choose the one that explains why this step is done.

- A log turns multiplying into adding, so the number of multiplications is one log divided by the other.
- log 2 = 0.3010 and log 1.06 = 0.0253, from the calculator.
- $3,000 is 2 times $1,500, so the target is double the start.

**Shown as soon as you answer**

- The one that explains it: A log turns multiplying into adding, so the number of multiplications is one log divided by the other.
  - If you chose “log 2 = 0.3010 and log 1.06 = 0.0253, from the calculator.”: That is true, but those are the numbers, not the reason for dividing them.
  - If you chose “$3,000 is 2 times $1,500, so the target is double the start.”: That is true, but it is the step before, not the reason for using logs.

Counting how many times 1.06 must be multiplied is hard to do directly, and a log makes it easy. The log of a number is how far up a *log scale* it sits: the log of 10 is 1, the log of 100 is 2 and the log of 1,000 is 3. Every time a number is multiplied by 10, its log goes up by 1. So a log turns multiplying into adding.

The same holds for 1.06: each multiplication by 1.06 adds 0.0253 to the log of the account. To become 2 times bigger, the log has to rise by 0.3010. So the question is how many times 0.0253 must be added to reach 0.3010. That is a division: 0.3010 ÷ 0.0253 = 11.90.

- Check it with whole numbers of times, then round: Starting from $1,500, 11 multiplications by 1.06 give about $2,847, still under the target; 12 multiplications give about $3,018, over it. So the target is reached during the 12th year. Rounded, the answer is about 11.9 years

The division gives a count with a decimal part, so check it against whole years by multiplying.

**The result**

The account holds $3,000 after about 11.9 years, which is during the 12th year.

### 16. A one-time change

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 16 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-oneoff`]

Last: an amount that changed once and then stopped.

*The gym fee*

> A gym charged $30 a month for years. ⟦In March it started charging $36 a month⟧, and ⟦it has charged $36 a month ever since⟧. What will the gym charge after 2 more years?

Month by month the fees read 30, 30, 30, 36, 36, 36: one jump, then a flat line. It is tempting to treat the jump as the start of a pattern, and say the fee goes up $6 each time, or 20% each time. Nothing in the problem says that. The fee in two years is what the problem tells you: $36.

Every kind of problem in this unit has a change, so the change itself does not decide it. What decides it is that the change happened once and has not come again, so only the new amount carries forward. This is the easiest one to mistake for another, because a change made once can be given as a plain figure or as a percentage, and both can look like the start of a pattern.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the amount before and after the change: $30, then $36.** This tells you how big the change was.
2. **Find what happens after the change: “it has charged $36 a month ever since”.** These words say whether the change comes again.
3. **Check that nothing says the change repeats: no “every month” or “each year” on it.** Without a repeat, there is nothing to carry forward.

This is **A one-time change**: the change was made once, and it is not made again.

You may also hear this called “a step change” or “a one-off jump”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **A one-time change**.

### 20. First: the way the amount changes

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 20 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-g1`]

You have seen this question at the foot of each card, with one answer under it. Here it is whole, with every answer, and why you ask it before any sums.

**The question:** **“How does the amount change?”**

**Its answers**

- **“It goes up or down by the same number each time”**
  - Give this answer when the amount goes up or down by the same number every hour, day, month or year ($200 a month, 2 cm an hour), however big it has gotten.
  - Keeps **Linear growth**. Rules out **Exponential growth**, **Logarithm** and **A one-time change**.
- **“It is multiplied by the same number each time”**
  - Give this answer when the amount is multiplied by the same number every hour, day, month or year: it doubles, or it grows or shrinks by the same percent of itself (5% a year).
  - Keeps **Exponential growth** and **Logarithm**. Rules out **Linear growth** and **A one-time change**.
- **“It changed once, then stayed the same”**
  - Give this answer when the amount changed one time and has not changed since, so nothing repeats.
  - Keeps **A one-time change**. Rules out **Linear growth**, **Exponential growth** and **Logarithm**.

**Why it matters**

Adding and multiplying give nearly the same numbers at first and very different numbers later, so the wrong steps give an answer that is far off. An amount that changed once has nothing to carry forward at all.

Put $1,000 at 5% a year beside $1,000 plus $50 a year. After one year both are $1,050. After thirty years the first is about $4,322 and the second is $2,500. Each gives a neat number, and nothing in the number says it is wrong, so only the words of the problem can tell you which one you have.

The same number each time leads to **Linear growth**. Multiplying leads to **Exponential growth** or **Logarithm**, so the second question has to separate those two. A single change leads to **A one-time change**.

**How to answer it**

1. **Find the sentence that says what the amount does: “$3 a week”, “4% a year”, “rose to $36 in March”.** How the change is written tells you most of it.
2. **Compare the size of the second change with the first.** A plain figure stays the same size, and a percentage of a bigger or smaller amount does not.
3. **If the change happened once, stop there.** There is no second change to compare.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

A percentage or a plain figure can point the wrong way. A percentage that is paid out each year gives a total that grows by the same number each time, which is **Linear growth**. A price that went up 8% and stayed is **A one-time change**, not **Exponential growth**: a percentage says how big a change is, never how often it comes. When the problem asks how long, **Linear growth** and **Logarithm** differ in whether each change is the same size. In **A one-time change** the amount has stopped, so it never reaches a target it is not already at.

- Linear growth or Exponential growth: Is the amount changed by the same number each time, such as $50 a month, or by the same share of itself each time, such as 5% a month or a doubling?
- Linear growth or A one-time change: After the change is made, does the problem say it is made again each hour, day, week, month or year, or does the amount stay put?
- Linear growth or Logarithm: When the problem asks how long, is each change the same size, or is each change bigger than the one before?
- Exponential growth or A one-time change: Is the percentage applied again each hour, day, week, month or year, or only once?
- Logarithm or A one-time change: When the problem asks how long, is the amount still changing each hour, day, week, month or year, or has it stopped?

### 22. Second: the amount later, or the time it takes?

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 22 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-g2`]

The first question left the multiplying answer with two names. Here is the second question, with both answers, and why it comes after the first.

### 24. What to carry away

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 24 of 24*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap-growth`]

You have now worked problems of all four kinds on your own. Here is the unit in one place.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

How does the amount change?
- It goes up or down by the same number each time → Linear growth
- It is multiplied by the same number each time → Exponential growth · Logarithm
- It changed once, then stayed the same → A one-time change

Do you need the amount later, or how long it takes?
- What the amount will be after a set time → Linear growth · Exponential growth · A one-time change
- How long until the amount reaches a target → Linear growth · Logarithm · A one-time change

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Linear growth**: one amount that goes up or down by the same number each day, month or year, and a question about what it will be later or when it reaches a target.
- **Exponential growth**: one amount multiplied by the same number each time, like doubling or growing 5% a year, a set time, and the question what it will be by then.
- **Logarithm**: one amount multiplied by the same number each time, like 5% a year, a target it should reach, and the question how long, or how many times, until it gets there.
- **A one-time change**: one amount that changed once and has stayed the same since, and a question about what it will be later or how long until it reaches a target.

**To carry away**

- Before any sums, put two questions to the problem and find the words that answer each. First: **“How does the amount change?”** Then: **“Do you need the amount later, or how long it takes?”**
- Adding the same figure, or taking the same figure away, every time is **Linear growth**. Going forward, multiply the change by how many times and add it to the start, or take it away if the amount goes down. Going backward, take the start off the target and divide by the change each time.
- A percentage, a doubling or a halving each time means multiplying. Find the *multiplier*: 100% plus the percentage if the amount goes up, 100% minus it if it goes down, written as a decimal. Given a time, it is **Exponential growth**: multiply the start by the *multiplier* once for each time, each time on the last result, and round only at the end. Given a target, it is **Logarithm**: divide the log of (target ÷ start) by the log of the *multiplier*.
- A change made once, with the amount staying put, is **A one-time change**. Carry the new amount forward as it is, and do not carry the change forward as if it came again.
- A percentage says how big a change is, never how often it comes. 8% every year is **Exponential growth**, 8% once is **A one-time change**, and interest paid out each year is **Linear growth**.
