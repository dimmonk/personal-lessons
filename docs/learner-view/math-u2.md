### 1. Six whole-number problems that look alike

Before you start calculating a problem about whole numbers, check what it is asking. Six different questions look alike, each needs its own steps, and the wrong steps still give you a neat number.

At a school fair, six questions come up in one afternoon, all about whole numbers. “Can our 67 volunteers stand in equal rows?” “Which prime numbers multiply together to give 60?” “Two ribbons, 60 cm and 84 cm long, are to be cut into pieces of one length with none left over: how long can the pieces be at most?” “One stall restocks every 20 minutes and the other every 30: when do they restock together?” “There are 50 candies for 7 children: how many are left over?” And a child with a calculator asks: “Can the number that multiplies by itself to give 2 be written down exactly?”

### 2. A number that will not split

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 27*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-prime`]

The first kind of problem needs the word “prime”. Here it is in a real example.

*The coach and her squads*

> A coach has 12 players on Monday and 13 players on Friday, and she wants to make teams of equal size, with more than one team and more than one player in each team. On Monday she can make 2 teams of 6, 3 teams of 4, 4 teams of 3 or 6 teams of 2. On Friday she cannot, however she tries: 13 players make only one team of 13, or 13 teams of 1.

Twelve players can make teams in four ways, because 12 shares out evenly by 2, by 3, by 4 and by 6. Thirteen players cannot: nothing between 1 and 13 shares 13 out evenly, so it is one team of 13 or 13 teams of one.

A number like 13, which only 1 and itself share out evenly, is called a prime. Every other whole number is built from primes: 12 is 2 × 2 × 3. The first few are 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 and 13. The number 1 is left out on purpose.

**The word for this.** *Prime number*: a whole number above 1 that no whole number goes into exactly except 1 and itself, such as 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 and 13.


### 3. Prime check

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 3 of 27*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-prime`]

First: one number, and a yes-or-no question about whether it splits.

*The market apples*

> A market stall has 59 apples. The seller wants to ⟦pack them in equal bags, with more than one bag and more than one apple in each bag⟧. ⟦Is that possible?⟧

The seller has one number, 59, and one question: can the apples be packed in equal bags at all? She does not ask how many bags, or how big. Whether 59 is odd or small does not matter: what matters is that she only asks whether it splits.

You could try every smaller number one by one, but for a big number that takes forever. The steps in this unit test only a short list of small numbers, and the answer is just as sure.

**How to spot it**

1. **Count the numbers: one, 59 apples.** Two numbers would be a different problem, coming later in this unit.
2. **Find the rule for the groups: equal bags, more than one bag, more than one apple in each.** Without that rule, one bag of 59 would always work.
3. **Find the question: “Is that possible?” It wants a yes or a no.** No list and no count is asked for.

A problem like this is **Prime check**. If no small number fits it, the number is a *prime number*, and it cannot be split.

You may also hear this called “primality test”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Prime check**.

### 5. The number that multiplies by itself

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 5 of 27*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-sqroot`]

The steps for the first kind stop testing at a certain point, and you need one more word to say where. Here it is in a real example.

*The square patio*

> A paver lays 36 square slabs as one big square, with 6 slabs along each side. He says the side is 6 slabs long, because 6 × 6 = 36. Then he is asked about a second patio of 50 slabs, and finds that 7 × 7 = 49 is one slab short and 8 × 8 = 64 is too many.

The number of slabs along one side of the square patio is the number that, multiplied by itself, gives the total: 6 × 6 = 36. That 6 is the *square root* of 36, written √36 = 6.

Most numbers have no whole number that multiplies by itself to give them. 50 slabs cannot make a square: 7 × 7 = 49 is one slab short, and 8 × 8 = 64 is too many. The side would be between 7 and 8 slabs, about 7.07 on a calculator.

**The word for this.** *Square root*: the number that, multiplied by itself, gives the number you started with: the square root of 36 is 6, because 6 × 6 = 36.


### 6. Worked: is 67 a prime number?

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 6 of 27*

[reviewers only: card kind `solved`, id `solved-prime-1`]

Here are the steps for the first kind on a real problem: a choir of 67 singers, with every step written out.

**The problem**

> A choir has 67 singers. The director wants to stand them in equal rows, with more than one row and more than one singer in each row. Is that possible?

**The working, step by step**

- Find where testing can stop: 8 × 8 = 64 and 9 × 9 = 81, so √67 is between 8 and 9. Test no further than 8

**You are asked:** This step carries the idea. Every statement below is true of the problem. Before you read the reason, choose the one that explains why this step is done.

- The smaller of two numbers that multiply to give 67 is never more than the *square root* of 67.
- 67 is a little more than 8 × 8 = 64, and less than 9 × 9 = 81.
- The primes up to 8 are 2, 3, 5 and 7, and there are only four.

**Shown as soon as you answer**

- The one that explains it: The smaller of two numbers that multiply to give 67 is never more than the *square root* of 67.
  - If you chose “67 is a little more than 8 × 8 = 64, and less than 9 × 9 = 81.”: True, and it is how the stopping point is found. But it does not say why testing can stop there.
  - If you chose “The primes up to 8 are 2, 3, 5 and 7, and there are only four.”: True too, but it comes after the stopping point. It does not say why testing can stop at 8.

Suppose 67 could be split into equal rows. Then two whole numbers multiply to give 67: the number of rows and the number of singers in each row. They cannot both be more than 8, because 9 × 9 = 81 is already more than 67. So one of them is 8 or less, and testing up to 8 will find it.

Testing past 8 only finds the same pair from the other side. If 7 × 9 were 67, testing 7 would already have found it.

- List the primes up to there: Primes up to 8: 2, 3, 5, 7

Any number that fits by 6 also fits by 2 and by 3, so testing the primes covers every number.

- Divide by each prime in turn, looking for an exact fit: 67 = 2 × 33 + 1, with 1 left over; 67 = 3 × 22 + 1, with 1 left over; 67 = 5 × 13 + 2, with 2 left over; 67 = 7 × 9 + 4, with 4 left over. None fits

A prime fits only if nothing is left over: 67 = 2 × 33 + 1 leaves 1, so 2 does not fit, even though 1 is close.

- Say what it shows: No prime up to 8 fits 67 exactly, so 67 is prime

If 67 could be split into equal rows, some prime up to 8 would fit it exactly. None did, so it cannot.

**The result**

67 is a *prime number*. The choir cannot stand in equal rows with more than one row and more than one singer in each: the only layouts are one row of 67, or 67 rows of 1.


### 7. A number that shares another out exactly

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 27*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-factor`]

The second kind of problem takes one number apart, and it needs a word for the numbers that share it out exactly. Here it is in a real example.

*The baker’s boxes*

> A baker has 12 rolls to pack. Boxes of 3 hold all 12 rolls with 4 boxes, and so do boxes of 4, with 3 boxes. Boxes of 5 will not do: 2 boxes of 5 hold 10 rolls and leave 2 over.

The baker’s 12 rolls fit exactly into 4 boxes of 3, or 3 boxes of 4. Boxes of 5 will not do: they leave 2 rolls over. The numbers that share 12 out exactly are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 12, and they come in pairs that multiply to 12: 1 × 12, 2 × 6 and 3 × 4.

**The word for this.** *Factor*: a whole number that goes into another exactly: 3 and 4 are factors of 12, because 3 × 4 = 12.

So 3 and 4 are a *factor* pair of 12, and so are 2 and 6. Every number has at least two factors, 1 and itself, and a prime has no others.


### 8. Prime factors

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 8 of 27*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-factor`]

Second: one number again, but now you want a list, not a yes or a no.

*The cereal-box puzzle*

> A puzzle on a cereal box says that 60 is made by multiplying prime numbers together. ⟦Which prime numbers multiply together to give 60?⟧

The puzzle gives one number and asks which primes multiply together to make it. For 60 they are 2, 2, 3 and 5, because 2 × 2 × 3 × 5 = 60. There is only one such list, whatever order you find the primes in.

Sometimes the same problem is worded as “every way it splits”: the ways to set out 28 tables in equal rows, or the team sizes that 24 players allow. The steps are the same, because once you have the primes you can list every way.

**How to spot it**

1. **Count the numbers: one, 60.** Two numbers would be a different problem.
2. **Find what is asked for: “which prime numbers multiply together to give 60”.** The answer is a list of numbers.
3. **Check it is not only “can it be split?”: here you want the actual primes.** A yes or a no would leave the question unanswered.

A problem like this is **Prime factors**. Its answer is the list of primes that multiply to make the number.

You may also hear this called “prime factorization” or “prime decomposition”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Prime factors**.

### 10. Worked: the primes that make 84

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 10 of 27*

[reviewers only: card kind `solved`, id `solved-factor-1`]

Here are the steps for the second kind on a real problem, with every step written out.

**The problem**

> A puzzle in the staff newsletter says: write 84 as a product of prime numbers. Which prime numbers are they?

**The working, step by step**

- Find the smallest prime that divides the number exactly: 2 divides 84 exactly: 84 ÷ 2 = 42

Start with the smallest prime so none is skipped: 2 fits 84 at once, so 84 = 2 × 42.

- Do the same to what is left, again and again, until what is left is prime: 42 ÷ 2 = 21; 21 ÷ 3 = 7; 7 is prime, so stop

**You are asked:** This step carries the idea. Every statement below is true of the problem. Before you read the reason, choose the one that explains why this step is done.

- A prime cannot be split any further, so you are done only when every piece left is a prime.
- 42 splits by 2 to give 21, and 21 splits by 3 to give 7.
- 84 is an even number, so 2 fits it first of all.

**Shown as soon as you answer**

- The one that explains it: A prime cannot be split any further, so you are done only when every piece left is a prime.
  - If you chose “42 splits by 2 to give 21, and 21 splits by 3 to give 7.”: True, and it is this step’s working. But it does not say why the step carries on until a prime is left.
  - If you chose “84 is an even number, so 2 fits it first of all.”: True, and it is why 2 fits first. But it does not say when the splitting ends.

After 84 = 2 × 42, the 42 can still be split, because it is not prime. It splits into 2 × 21, and the 21 splits into 3 × 7. Now 2, 2, 3 and 7 are all prime, and none can be split any further.

If you stopped at 84 = 2 × 42, one piece in your list could still be split, so it would not be a list of primes.

- Write the number as the product of every prime split off: 84 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 7

Multiply back to check nothing was dropped: 2 × 2 = 4, 4 × 3 = 12, and 12 × 7 = 84. The 2 is there twice because 84 split by 2 twice.

**The result**

84 is made of the primes 2, 2, 3 and 7: 84 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 7. The 2 is there twice, because 84 split by 2 twice.

### 12. Greatest common factor

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 12 of 27*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-hcf`]

Third: two numbers, and the biggest equal piece that fits both.

*The pepper trays*

> A cook has 12 red peppers and 18 green peppers. She wants to ⟦fill trays that each hold the same number of peppers, with red peppers in some trays and green in the others and none left over⟧. ⟦What is the largest number of peppers a tray can hold?⟧

The cook has 12 red peppers and 18 green ones, and wants trays of one size with none left over. Trays of 2 work (6 red trays and 9 green), and so do trays of 3 and of 6. Trays of 4 do not: 18 peppers leave 2 over. The biggest that works for both is 6: 2 red trays and 3 green. Since 6 goes into both 12 and 18 exactly, 6 is a *factor* of both.

You can check the answer: it is never more than the smaller of the two numbers, because a piece cannot be bigger than what it is cut from.

**How to spot it**

1. **Count the numbers: two, 12 peppers and 18 peppers.** One number would be a different problem.
2. **Find the cutting: trays that all hold the same number, none left over.** Both numbers must split exactly into that size.
3. **Find the biggest: “What is the largest number of peppers a tray can hold?”** You want the biggest size that fits both, never more than the smaller number.

A problem like this is **Greatest common factor**. “Common” means both numbers have it, and “greatest” means the biggest one.

You may also hear this called “greatest common divisor” or “GCF” or “GCD”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Greatest common factor**.

### 14. Worked: the biggest equal piece for 60 and 84

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 14 of 27*

[reviewers only: card kind `solved`, id `solved-hcf-1`]

Here are the steps for the third kind on a real problem, with every step written out.

**The problem**

> A craftsman has a rectangular panel 60 cm by 84 cm. He wants to cut it into square tiles, all the same size, with no waste. What is the largest side the squares can have?

**The working, step by step**

- Break each number into primes: 60 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 5; 84 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 7

A piece that fits 60 is built only from primes of 60, and a piece that fits 84 only from primes of 84.

- Keep the primes that are in both lists, as many times as both lists have them: Both have 2 × 2 × 3

**You are asked:** This step carries the idea. Every statement below is true of the problem. Before you read the reason, choose the one that explains why this step is done.

- A piece that fits into both numbers can only be built from primes that both numbers have.
- Both numbers have 2 × 2 × 3 among their primes.
- 60 has a 5 in its primes, and 84 has a 7.

**Shown as soon as you answer**

- The one that explains it: A piece that fits into both numbers can only be built from primes that both numbers have.
  - If you chose “Both numbers have 2 × 2 × 3 among their primes.”: True, but that is what this step finds. It does not say why only the shared primes are kept.
  - If you chose “60 has a 5 in its primes, and 84 has a 7.”: True, and it shows what is left out. But it does not say why those primes cannot be in the piece.

To fit into 60, a piece must be built from primes that 60 has. To fit into 84, it must be built from primes that 84 has. So a piece that fits both uses only primes that are in both lists. The 5 is only in 60 and the 7 only in 84, so neither can be used.

Both numbers have two 2s, so the piece can use two. Each has one 3, so it uses one. Using more of any prime would leave one number short.

- Multiply the shared primes: 2 × 2 × 3 = 12

Check by dividing: 60 = 12 × 5 and 84 = 12 × 7, so 12 fits both exactly, with nothing left over.

**The result**

The largest square tile has a side of 12 cm, and the panel is cut into 5 tiles across and 7 down.

### 15. Least common multiple

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 15 of 27*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-lcm`]

Fourth: two numbers again, but each is how often something repeats.

*The two drummers*

> Two drummers start together. ⟦One hits a drum every 3 seconds and the other every 4 seconds⟧. After how many seconds do they ⟦next hit their drums together⟧?

The first drummer hits at 3, 6, 9, 12 seconds and so on. The second hits at 4, 8, 12 and so on. The first number on both lists is 12, so they next hit together after 12 seconds.

You can check the answer: it is never less than the bigger of the two numbers, because something that repeats every 4 seconds cannot meet anything before 4 seconds have passed.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the two repeats: one drum every 3 seconds, one every 4.** Each number is how often something happens, not an amount to cut up.
2. **Find the start: the drummers “start together”.** Both counts begin at the same moment.
3. **Find the question: “next hit their drums together”.** You want the first time both happen at once.

A problem like this is **Least common multiple**. A multiple is what you get by counting in steps of a number, so 3, 6, 9 and 12 are multiples of 3, and “least common” means the first one on both lists.

You may also hear this called “LCM”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Least common multiple**.

### 19. Remainder

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 19 of 27*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-modrem`]

Fifth: a count shared into groups, or going round a loop.

*The bags of rolls*

> A baker ⟦packs 29 rolls into bags of 6⟧. ⟦How many rolls are left over once every bag is full?⟧

29 rolls fill 4 bags of 6, which uses 24 rolls. The 5 rolls left are too few for another bag, so 5 is the answer. It is always less than the size of one group: at 6 or more, another bag could be filled.

The same idea answers questions about a loop. The 7 days of a week go round and round, so a count of days ends on one of the seven. Every whole week brings you back to the day you started, so only what is left over moves you on.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the count: 29 rolls.** It is the one number that gets shared out or counted round.
2. **Find the group size or the loop: bags of 6.** It is the size of one full group, or the length of one loop.
3. **Find the question: “How many rolls are left over once every bag is full?”** You want the part that does not make a whole group, or where the count ends.

A problem like this is **Remainder**. It is what is left over after all the whole groups, or how far past the last whole loop a count ends.

You may also hear this called “clock arithmetic” or “modular arithmetic”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Remainder**.

### 22. Irrational number

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 22 of 27*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-irrat`]

Sixth: a number you measure, and whether it can be written down exactly.

*The metal sheet*

> A square sheet of metal has an area of 5 m². Its side is the number that multiplies by itself to give 5. ⟦Can the side be written exactly, as a fraction or a decimal that ends?⟧

A half is exactly 0.5 and a third is exactly 1/3, but the side of this sheet is different. It is between 2 and 3, because 2 × 2 = 4 and 3 × 3 = 9, and no fraction multiplies by itself to give exactly 5. A calculator’s 2.236067977 stops only because it ran out of room. So the answer is no: you can only round it, to about 2.24 m.

For the *square root* of a whole number, ask whether the number is a whole number multiplied by itself, like 36 = 6 × 6. If it is, the root is that whole number, and exact. If it is not, the root can never be written exactly. Pi can never be written exactly either.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the number you are asked about: the side of a square sheet with an area of 5 m².** It is a measured number, not a count of things.
2. **Check it is a *square root* or pi: here, “the number that multiplies by itself to give 5”.** Those are the numbers that can fail to be exact.
3. **Find the question: “Can the side be written exactly?”** Nothing is shared out, and nothing repeats.

A problem like this is **Irrational number**. “Rational” means it can be written exactly as a fraction, and “ir” in front means it cannot.

You may also hear this called “a number with no exact fraction”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Irrational number**.

### 27. What to carry away

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 27 of 27*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap-whole`]

You have now worked problems of all six kinds on your own.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What do you need to know about the numbers?
- Whether a number splits evenly at all → Prime check
- Every way a number splits, or the primes that multiply to make it → Prime factors
- The biggest equal piece two numbers both split into → Greatest common factor
- When two repeating things line up again → Least common multiple
- What is left over, or where a count ends on a loop → Remainder
- Whether a number can be written exactly → Irrational number

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Prime check**: one whole number, and the question whether any whole number other than 1 and itself goes into it exactly.
- **Prime factors**: one whole number, and a question about every way it splits into equal groups, or which prime numbers multiply to make it.
- **Greatest common factor**: two whole numbers, and the question what the biggest piece size is that both split into with nothing left over.
- **Least common multiple**: two things that each repeat every so many seconds, days or turns, and the question when they next happen at the same time.
- **Remainder**: a count, what it is shared out or counted around (groups of 6, the 7 days of a week), and the question what is left over or where the count ends.
- **Irrational number**: a square root of a whole number, or pi, and the question whether it can be written exactly, as a fraction or as a decimal that ends.

**To carry away**

- Before any working, ask what the problem wants to know about its numbers, and find the words that say it. If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet. The question is: **“What do you need to know about the numbers?”**
- One number leads to **Prime check**, a yes or a no, or to **Prime factors**, a list. Two numbers lead to **Greatest common factor**, the biggest piece that fits both, or to **Least common multiple**, the first time two repeats meet. A count with one group size or one loop leads to **Remainder**. A root or pi, asked about whether it can be written exactly, leads to **Irrational number**.
- The numbers do not tell you the kind. 12 and 18 can ask for the biggest equal piece, 6, or for the first time two repeats meet, 36.
- With two numbers you can check the answer. The biggest equal piece is never more than the smaller number, and the first meeting is never less than the bigger one.
- For **Prime check**: find where testing can stop, list the primes up to there, and divide by each in turn. One exact fit means the number splits. No fit up to the stopping point means it is a prime.
- For **Prime factors**: split off the smallest prime that fits, do the same to what is left until a prime is left, and write the number as the product of the primes you split off. Multiply back to check. To list every way it splits, multiply some of the primes together in every combination, and leave out 1 and the number itself if the problem wants more than one group and more than one in each.
- For **Greatest common factor** and **Least common multiple**: break both numbers into primes. For the biggest equal piece, keep the primes both numbers have and multiply them. For the first meeting, keep every prime either number has, as many times as the one with more of it, and multiply them.
- For **Remainder**: find how many whole groups, or whole loops, fit in the count, take them away to see what is left over, and move on from the start by that much. What is left over is always less than one group.
- For **Irrational number**: the *square root* of a whole number can be written exactly only if the number is a whole number multiplied by itself. Otherwise you can only round it, however many digits you show. Pi can never be written exactly.
