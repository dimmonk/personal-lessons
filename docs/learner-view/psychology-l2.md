# Psychology: A mind talking itself into something

Revision 1, draft (the screen carries the draft line). Part: A mind talking itself into something. Where the subject ends up: After this subject you can hear a story about someone, or catch one in your own head, and say what is going on, whether it is one moment or a lasting pattern, the name that fits or that there is too little to tell, and what to do about it, when someone reaches for "narcissist", "gaslighting" or "toxic".

## The why

A friend who says she never gossips spends lunch on a coworker's divorce, then says, "That was not gossip, I was worried." Your uncle keeps a timeshare he hates: "We have paid into it for ten years." Both sound like reasons; they are different machines.
In about twenty minutes you will learn four ways a mind talks itself into something, and the honest change of mind.

## Step 1: Three checks

The five questions still run in order. Question 1, what is going on, has four answers: a mind talking itself into something, a move made on someone, a lasting way someone is (years, many people), or an ordinary reaction. Question 2, one moment or a pattern: a reaction to a hard time, once or twice, over and over for months or years, or the story does not say.
These stories are the first answer: one person reasoning toward what they want. Three checks tell them apart.
A. What came first? An act against their own belief, then the mind eases the clash: dissonance. An answer they want, then looking for reasons: motivated reasoning. The evidence, then the belief moves: an honest change of mind.
B. How is the evidence treated? A belief already held, what fits it waved through, what goes against it picked apart or never looked at: confirmation bias.
C. Which way does the reason point? Back at money or time already spent: sunk cost. This check wins when another also fits.

## Step 2: Act first, or answer first

Dissonance: what you did clashes with what you believe or how you see yourself. Facing it changes the act. Dodging it eases the clash: an excuse, a jab at whoever brought the bad news, making it not matter, or the belief sliding to fit what was done.
Motivated reasoning starts with the answer the person wants. If the answer was picked before the looking, it is motivated reasoning, even when the looking is one-sided too.

| A | B |
|---|---|
| Before seeing a single house, she decided on the Maple Street one, then toured five others to list what was wrong with them. | She bought the Maple Street house in a rush. Six months later, with the roof leaking, she said, "Honestly, a leaky roof is character." |

Compare: In the first, the answer came before the looking. In the second, the act came before the story.

## Step 3: Two standards, and what is spent

Confirmation bias needs only a belief already held. The test: would they accept this evidence if it pointed the other way?

| A | B |
|---|---|
| She believes in her horoscope. She counts every time it was right and forgets the misses. | She believes in her horoscope. She writes its predictions down first, then checks every one. |

Compare: The first has two standards. The second has one.

Sunk cost: what is spent is gone either way; only what the next step is worth counts. Dissonance often feeds it: quitting would admit the waste.
An honest change of mind is not a bias: the evidence led, and they can say what moved them, and what would have moved them the other way.

## Step 4: The signs, and what to do

Dissonance: an act clashes with a belief or with the person's picture of themselves, and the mind eases the clash after the act: an excuse, a jab at the source, making it not matter, or the belief sliding to fit what was done.
Confirmation bias: evidence for the belief waved through, evidence against it picked apart, by a standard the other side never meets.
Motivated reasoning: the answer was chosen first, and the looking came after to back it up.
Sunk cost: the reason to go on points back at what is spent ("I have put in so much"), not forward at what the next step is worth.
An honest change of mind: new evidence came in and the belief moved, even against what the person wanted.
What to do. Dissonance: say the clash plainly, then change the act or the belief. Confirmation bias: one standard for both sides. Motivated reasoning: say what would change your mind before you look. Sunk cost: judge the next step on its own worth. An honest change of mind: nothing to fix.
Ordinary: the facts show a normal reason (a fair point, a slip put right); nothing to fix. Too little to tell: too few facts to see what is going on; find out more.

## Step 5: A worked example

Before the job on her team was even posted, Rachel told her husband it should go to her nephew: "He needs a break." When six applications came in, she opened his first and spent the evening writing down every reason he was the best fit. Nobody has been hired yet.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**Question 1. What is going on here?**
- A mind talking itself into something
- A move made on someone
- A lasting way someone is
- An ordinary reaction or slip: none of these

The working, one step at a time:
1. Question 1. What is going on? Rachel is reasoning toward an answer she wants. Nobody has been hired, told or acted on yet. Question 1: a mind talking itself into something.
1. What came first, the nearest look-alike, and what would prove the read wrong. Then name it. What came first: the answer, "it should go to her nephew", before the job was posted, because she wants it. Then the looking, which hunts for reasons. Look-alike: dissonance, which needs an act first and a story after; nothing has been done yet. Answer, then looking: motivated reasoning. It would be wrong if she had written down what the job needs before reading, and judged all six by it.

Then the answer: A mind talking itself into something / Motivated reasoning. The answer was picked before the job was even posted, because she wants it, and the reading hunted for reasons. Answer, then looking: motivated reasoning. No act has happened, so it cannot be dissonance.
