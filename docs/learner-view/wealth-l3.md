# Wealth Preservation: What it costs a year, in dollars

Revision 1, live. Part: What it costs a year, in dollars. Where the subject ends up: Run a planner's yearly check on your own US accounts: list everything, see what money you need soon is exposed and what single loss could ruin you, find the certain leaks (yearly costs in dollars, a missed match, tax in the wrong place, foreign filings) and the risks you did not choose (one big bet, a drifted mix), check who inherits and who can act, and change only what the check calls for.

## The why

A fund’s cost is a small-looking percent. In dollars it is not small: 0.95% of $38,400 is $364.80 every year, whether the fund did well or badly.
Here you read a fee sheet, turn the percents into dollars, and decide: change a fund, or leave it alone. Then you do the same on your own accounts.

## Step 1: The rule

Every fund takes a yearly cost, a percent of what you hold. Fee sheets call it the expense ratio. To get dollars: what you hold × the percent ÷ 100. Then add the plan’s administrative fee, which the fee sheet and your quarterly statement show.
Change a fund only when another fund of the same kind, on the same list, costs less: switch to that one. Leave it alone when nothing of its kind costs less. A fund’s name does not tell you its cost; the fee sheet does.
Inside a 401(k) or an IRA, a switch is not taxed. In a brokerage account, selling a fund at a gain is taxed, and that tax weighs against the switch.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “Pick the cheapest fund on the list.”
The right line: Pick the cheapest of the same kind. A bond fund costs less than a stock fund, but it holds something else, so it is not a swap.

## Step 2: A worked example

A statement, "Lakeside Manufacturing 401(k)" (a tab named "Statement", one of a household's papers: one open at a time, the first at the start, and the one with a marked line after the answer):

> Your funds  |  Balance  (the column heads)
> Summit Large Company Fund  |  $38,400
> Keystone Total Bond Fund  |  $12,600
> Total  |  $51,000
> Administrative fee taken this quarter  |  $24.00
> (Balances on the last day of the quarter.)

A fee sheet, "Lakeside Manufacturing: what the funds cost" (a tab named "Fee sheet", one of a household's papers: one open at a time, the first at the start, and the one with a marked line after the answer):

> Fund  |  Kind  |  Expense ratio  (the column heads)
> Summit Large Company Fund  |  Large US companies  |  0.95%
> Crestline 500 Fund  |  Large US companies  |  0.04%
> Pioneer US Growth Fund  |  Large US companies  |  1.20%
> Harbor Bond Fund  |  Bonds  |  0.60%
> Keystone Total Bond Fund  |  Bonds  |  0.07%
> Bayline Income Fund  |  Bonds  |  0.85%
> Administrative fee  |  Taken from your account  |  $96 a year
> (The expense ratio is a percent of what you hold in the fund, taken out of it every year.)
> (The administrative fee pays for running the plan. It is shown here, and on your quarterly statement.)

The working, one step at a time:
1. Find each fund’s expense ratio on the fee sheet. Summit Large Company Fund costs 0.95%. Keystone Total Bond Fund costs 0.07%.
1. Turn each cost into dollars, and add the administrative fee. Summit Large Company Fund: $38,400 × 0.95% = $364.80. Keystone Total Bond Fund: $12,600 × 0.07% = $8.82. Fee $96. Together $469.62 a year.
1. For each fund held, look for one of the same kind that costs less. Summit Large Company Fund holds large US companies and costs 0.95%. Crestline 500 Fund holds the same and costs 0.04%. Keystone Total Bond Fund is already the cheapest of the bonds.
1. Name the fund to switch to. Crestline 500 Fund: the same kind, at 0.04%. Keystone Total Bond Fund holds bonds, so it is not a swap. Inside a 401(k) the switch is not taxed.

Then the answer: Summit Large Company Fund: 0.95%; Keystone Total Bond Fund: 0.07% / $469.62 a year / Change it / Crestline 500 Fund. Your two funds and the fee cost $469.62 a year. Summit Large Company Fund costs 0.95%, and Crestline 500 Fund holds the same kind of companies for 0.04%, so switching saves about $349.44 a year.

## Step 5: On your own accounts

The same step on the learner's own data. Nothing here is scored, stored as a try or logged: it is kept in the learner's own account with their sign-in, with a copy on this phone, and shared with no one (the sheet, the dated changes and the moments marked).

**The sheet, when it is empty.** "Your accounts": Type what your statements say: the kind of account, where it is held, its balance, and each fund in it. A fund’s yearly cost is on your plan’s fee sheet; leave it blank if you do not have it yet. Only you can see this: it is saved with your Google sign-in and never shared. Do not type an account number, a login or a password.

- Kind of account (choose one)
- A name you will know it by (optional) (words)

- The highest it was this year (optional) (a number, in dollars)
- Funds, one row for each, added with "Add a fund":
  - Name of the fund (words)
