### 2. Three places, three rules for the number of seats

*Unit Seven · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 41*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-rule`]

The first group is about how the seats are shared out among the states, because the numbers in the second group come out of it.

*Ruth and Pablo*

> Ruth lives in one of the biggest states, with about forty million people. Her cousin Pablo lives in one of the smallest, where a few hundred thousand people share a single representative in the House of Representatives. Ruth’s state has dozens of representatives. Yet in the Senate their two states are exactly equal: each has two senators.

Congress has two chambers, the House of Representatives and the Senate, and they are built on different rules. The House is built on people: a state with more people has more representatives. Ruth’s state has dozens, and Pablo’s has one. The Senate is built on states: every state has two senators, however big or small, so Ruth’s state and Pablo’s are equal there. That was the compromise of 1787: big states got more seats in the House, and small states got equality in the Senate.

The Supreme Court follows neither rule. The number of justices, the judges who sit on it, is not in the Constitution. Congress sets it by law.

Hold each rule with its place. The words “shared out among the states” are used for both chambers, and the answers differ.

### 13. Every job has its own clock

*Unit Seven · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 13 of 41*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-term`]

The first two groups were about how many people sit in each place. This group is about how long each of them keeps the job.

*Zofia’s question*

> Zofia is nine. Her father Marek hears about the House of Representatives on the news at every election, about the Senate only sometimes, and about the President every fourth year. “Is every job on a different clock?” she asks. Marek says it is, and that the judges in his court stories are on a clock too, only a much longer one.

A term is the length of time someone holds a job before it is up again. From shortest to longest: a member of the House serves two years. The President serves four, and so does the Vice President, who is elected along with the President. A senator serves six. A federal judge serves “during good behavior”, which in practice means for life.

A judge’s clock is the odd one out: it is not a number of years, and a federal judge does not run for election.

The two numbers easiest to swap are the House’s two years and the Senate’s six. The House is the short one.

### 20. When there is an election, how much is up?

*Unit Seven · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 20 of 41*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-up`]

A term says how long a job lasts. This group asks a different question: when an election comes, how much of the House, of the Senate or of the federal judges is up?

*Two campaign offices*

> On the night before an election, Sofia, who volunteers for a woman running for the House of Representatives, tells Jon that every House seat is up tomorrow, so the whole chamber could look different by morning. Jon, who volunteers for a man running for the Senate, says his race is one of only some of the Senate’s seats, so most senators will keep their jobs whatever happens. Sofia asks about their neighbor, a federal judge. Jon says she is not on any list at all.

In the House, all of it is up. Every seat comes up every two years, so one election can change the whole House.

In the Senate, about a third is up. Terms are six years and the seats are spread out, so about a third come up every two years, and most senators keep their jobs through any one election.

Federal judges are never up. The President nominates a judge, which means puts a name forward, and the Senate confirms it. The judge then serves for life, in practice.

### 30. If the President cannot serve

*Unit Seven · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 30 of 41*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-line`]

The Vice President and the Speaker are both in the last group. This group puts them in order, because they have a second job in common: they stand behind the President.

*A teacher’s question*

> Mr. Okafor teaches a citizenship class. He asks: “Suppose the President resigned tonight. By tomorrow morning, somebody would be sworn in. Who would it be? And if that person could not do it either, who would be next?” Nobody in the class can answer yet.

The line of succession is the order in which people take over a job. If the President dies, resigns or is removed, somebody takes over at once, and the order is fixed in advance.

First in line is the Vice President, who is elected along with the President. Next is the Speaker of the House.

Each of the two has another job as well, the tie vote in the Senate and leading the House. These two facts are asked only about the order.

### 34. What a person must be to be President

*Unit Seven · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 34 of 41*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-pres`]

The line of succession says who would take over. This last group is about who may hold the job at all.

*Rui and Vera*

> Rui is fifty. He came to the country at twenty, became a citizen at thirty, and has lived here ever since. His daughter Vera was born a citizen, has lived here all her life, and is twenty-eight. At a quiz night someone asks: “Could Rui be President? Could Vera?” Both say no, each for a different reason. A retired teacher adds that there are two more conditions neither of them has had to think about.

Three conditions are about what a person must be: at least thirty-five years old, a citizen from birth, and a resident for fourteen years. Each must be met. Rui became a citizen at thirty, so he was not a citizen from birth. A citizen from birth is called a natural-born citizen. Vera, at twenty-eight, is not yet thirty-five.

The fourth is a limit once someone is in the job. The Twenty-second Amendment, added in 1951, says no one may be elected President more than twice.

Thirty-five is an age and fourteen is years of living in the country. The age is the bigger number.

### 41. What to carry away

*Unit Seven · rev 3 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 41 of 41*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap-nums`]

You have now met every fact in the unit, in its group. This card puts them together, and then adds what to carry.

**What fixes the number of seats**

- **How are the seats in the House of Representatives shared out among the states?** By population: a state with more people has more representatives
- **How are the seats in the Senate shared out among the states?** Equally: every state has two senators, however big or small the state is
- **How is the number of justices on the Supreme Court fixed?** By Congress: it sets the number by law, and the Constitution does not

**How many people sit in each place**

- **How many voting members does the House of Representatives have?** 435
- **How many senators are there in all?** 100
- **How many justices sit on the Supreme Court?** 9

**How long each job lasts**

- **How long is a term in the House of Representatives?** For two years
- **How long is a term in the Senate?** For six years
- **How long is the term of the President, and of the Vice President who is elected along with the President?** For four years
- **How long does a federal judge serve?** For life, in practice

**How much is up at one election**

- **When there is an election, how many of the House of Representatives’ seats are up?** All of them
- **When there is an election, how many of the Senate’s seats are up?** About a third of them
- **When there is an election, how many federal judges are up?** None of them

**Where tax bills begin, who leads the House, and who settles a tie**

- **In which chamber must a bill to raise taxes begin?** The House of Representatives
- **What is the title of the leader of the House of Representatives?** The Speaker of the House
- **Who settles a vote in the Senate when the senators split evenly?** The Vice President

**First in line, and next**

- **If the President dies, resigns or is removed, who is first in line to take over?** The Vice President
- **Who is next in line after the Vice President?** The Speaker of the House

**What it takes to be President**

- **What age must a person have reached to be President?** Be at least thirty-five years old
- **How must a person have become a citizen to be President?** Be a citizen from birth
- **How long must a person have lived in the country as a resident to be President?** Have been a resident for fourteen years
- **How many times may one person be elected President?** Be elected no more than twice

**To carry away**

- The House is built on people and is the big chamber, 435. The Senate is built on states, two each, 100 in all. The Supreme Court’s nine is set by Congress.
- Terms: the House two years, the President four, the Senate six, a federal judge for life. At an election all of the House is up, about a third of the Senate, and no judge.
- Tax bills begin in the House, whose leader is the Speaker. The Vice President settles a Senate tie, is first in line to be President, and the Speaker is next.
- To be President: at least thirty-five, a citizen from birth, a resident for fourteen years, and never elected more than twice.
- Offices stay and people change. Look up who holds each office now.
