### 2. Why people crossed the ocean

*Unit Nine · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 2 of 51*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-hist-came`]

Why people left home for the colonies.

*Four on the dock*

> Four people stand on the dock of a seaport in the early years of the colonies, waiting to board the same ship. Eliza is leaving because she is not allowed to follow her own religion where she lives, and she wants a place where she is. Tom wants a say in how he is governed, which he does not have at home. Hana has no work, and she has heard that a person can earn a better living on the other side of the ocean. Rafael and his family were attacked at home because of who they are, and they are leaving to be safe.

Each person on the dock wants something different. Eliza wants to follow her own religion: that is religious freedom. Tom wants a say in how he is governed: political liberty. Hana wants better work and pay: economic opportunity.

Rafael’s family is different. They are not heading toward something, they are running from it. Persecution means being harmed or punished for who you are or what you believe.

To keep the four straight, ask one question: did the person come to get something, or to get away from something? Three reasons are about getting something. Persecution is the only one about getting away.

### 8. Who else was in the colonies

*Unit Nine · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 8 of 51*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-hist-who`]

Not everyone came by choice, and some people were there before anyone arrived.

*Three in one field*

> Three people work in the same field on a farm in one of the early colonies. Joan signed an agreement in England to work for the farmer for a set number of years in return for her passage across the ocean, and when the years are over, the debt is paid. Samuel was taken from his home by force, brought across the ocean on a ship and sold to the farmer. Nobody asked him, and he is not free to leave. Mika’s family belongs to a Native nation whose people have lived along the river for generations, long before any ship arrived, and she comes to the edge of the field to trade.

Joan, Samuel and Mika belong to three different groups.

Joan was an indentured servant. She agreed to work a set number of years to pay for her trip across the ocean, and when the years were up, the debt was paid.

Samuel was enslaved. From 1619, many people were brought to the colonies by force. An enslaved person was treated as someone else’s property and could not leave. Nobody asked Samuel, and he made no agreement.

Mika’s people, a Native nation, were there long before any ship came. The colonists did not settle empty land.

To tell Joan from Samuel, ask: did the person agree to the work, and could they leave when it was done?

### 13. The quarrel with Britain: taxed with no say

*Unit Nine · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 13 of 51*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-hist-quarrel`]

What came between the colonists and Britain.

*The tea fee*

> A town of two thousand people is told by a council that meets in a far-off capital that every household must now pay a new fee on tea. Nobody in the town ever voted for a member of that council, and nobody on it has ever visited. The town writes to the council and gets no answer. At a meeting the baker says: ‘It isn’t the size of the fee. They take our money, and we have no say in who takes it.’ That night, a few of the younger people carry the next delivery of tea down to the river and tip the whole load in.

The baker’s complaint was the colonists’ complaint. The size of the fee was not the problem. The problem was that the people who set it were never chosen by the town, and the town could not remove them.

The colonies were ruled from Britain. Parliament, Britain’s lawmakers, taxed the colonists, but the colonists had never voted for anyone in it. They said a tax needs the agreement of the people who pay it, and shortened that to “no taxation without representation”. Representation means that someone you voted for sits in the group that taxes you.

In 1773, in Boston, colonists protested the tax on tea by throwing a ship’s whole cargo of tea into the harbor. This is the Boston Tea Party. Like the baker, they were upset about who set the tax, not about the price of tea.

The same idea, that money is taken and spent only by people the public can vote out, is the history behind the name **The power of the purse**.

### 18. The founding as a chain of years

*Unit Nine · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 18 of 51*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-hist-chain`]

The quarrel led somewhere: the same story as a row of years.

*Mei’s wall chart*

> Mei is making a wall chart for her class on how the country began. She lays her cards in a row from left to right: a quarrel over taxes, then a declaration, then a war, then a new plan of government. But she keeps swapping the year the Constitution was written and the year it took effect, and she has put the Declaration of Independence next to the Constitution as if the two were the same event.

Mei’s chart puts the story in the order it happened, each event leading to the next. In 1773 came the Boston Tea Party. On July 4, 1776, the colonies adopted the Declaration of Independence, which announced the break with Britain. The war for independence ended in 1783.

Then the new country needed a government. Its first plan, the Articles of Confederation, left the country almost powerless, so in 1787 delegates wrote a better one: the Constitution. It took effect in 1789, and George Washington, who had led the army, became the first President. He is called the Father of Our Country. The Bill of Rights was added in 1791, and the capital moved to Washington, D.C., in 1800.

Two swaps trip people up. The Declaration (1776) announced the break, and the Constitution (1787) set up the government. And the Constitution has two years: it was written in 1787, and it took effect in 1789. A plan is written first and starts working later.

### 24. How the country grew, and what it cost

*Unit Nine · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 24 of 51*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-hist-growth`]

How the country grew in the sixty years after 1800.

*The museum wall*

> A museum in a western city has a wall called ‘Sixty years of growth’, with four panels. The labels under the panels have been torn off. One panel shows a map with a huge new area shaded in. One shows a flag over a fort at dawn, with the words of a song beside it. One shows a map with the south-west corner shaded in. One shows a long line of people walking away from a river, and nobody in the line looks back.

Three of the panels are three events, and each one involves another country. The huge new area is the Louisiana land: in 1803 the country bought it from France, which roughly doubled its size. It was a purchase, not a war. The flag over the fort, with the song, is the War of 1812, fought against Britain. The Star-Spangled Banner, the national anthem, was written during it. The south-west corner is the war with Mexico, from 1846 to 1848, after which the country gained California and the Southwest.

The fourth panel is the cost. All of this land was already home to Native nations. They were removed from it by signed agreements, by purchase and by force. In the 1830s came the forced marches now called the Trail of Tears.

### 29. The question every new state raised

*Unit Nine · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 29 of 51*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-hist-slavery`]

The country grew, and growth kept bringing back one question.

*The new state*

> A territory in the west has enough people to ask to become a state. In the capital, the members of Congress spend a whole week arguing, and not about roads or taxes. They argue about one question: will the new state allow slavery or not? After the week they agree on a deal, but everyone in the room knows that the question will come back with the next new state.

The members of Congress in the story are not arguing about roads or taxes. Every time a new state joined, the country had to decide whether slavery would be allowed there. The founders had put that question off, and growth brought it back every time.

Congress tried deals. In 1820 it let in Missouri as a slave state and Maine as a free one, and in 1850 it made a second deal. Each deal bought time without settling anything.

In 1857 the Supreme Court gave its answer in the Dred Scott decision. It ruled that Black people could not be citizens, and that Congress could not ban slavery in the territories, the lands that were not yet states. That second ruling is an example of **Beyond Congress’s power**. Most people now see the decision as one of the worst the Court ever made. The first ruling did not last: in 1868 the Fourteenth Amendment overturned it by making everyone born here a citizen.

### 40. Freed, citizens, vote: the three amendments

*Unit Nine · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 40 of 51*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-hist-amend`]

The war ended, and three amendments settled what freedom meant, in order.

*Three neighbors on a porch*

> On a porch in a southern town, a few years after the war, three neighbors talk. The first says: ‘Nobody can own me any more.’ The second says: ‘My son was born here, and the Constitution now says that he is a citizen.’ The third says: ‘And now the Constitution says that nobody can stop me voting because of my race.’ Each of them is talking about a different change to the Constitution, and the three changes came one after another.

Each neighbor on the porch is talking about a different amendment, and the three came in a row: freed, citizens, vote. They were added in 1865, 1868 and 1870, during Reconstruction. That is the name for the years from 1865 to 1877, when the country tried to rebuild after the war and settle what freedom meant.

The first neighbor says nobody can own him any more. That is the Thirteenth, in 1865, which abolished slavery. The second says her son is a citizen. That is the Fourteenth, in 1868, which made everyone born here a citizen and promised due process and equal protection. The third says nobody can stop him voting because of his race. That is the Fifteenth, in 1870. A citizen and a voter are not the same thing, so these are two separate steps.

Due process means the government must follow fair steps before it punishes you or takes something from you. Equal protection means the law protects everyone equally.

The Fourteenth matters most for the rest of this course. It put people’s rights above every state, which is why a court can stop a state or a city from breaking a right: that is **Blocked by a right**. It is also where birthright citizenship comes from. Since 1868, everyone born here has been a citizen.

### 46. When the troops left: a promise that went unkept

*Unit Nine · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 46 of 51*

[reviewers only: card kind `concept`, id `con-hist-after`]

Reconstruction ended in 1877. This is what came next.

*The courthouse and the school*

> A man whose right to vote is written in the Constitution goes to the courthouse to vote, and he is told that he must first pay a fee. On his way home he passes a school that his children are not allowed to attend, because it is for white children only. The federal troops that were once stationed in the area have gone, and nobody has come to stop any of it.

The man in the story runs into two things. The separate school is segregation: laws that kept Black and white people apart in schools, transport and public places. The fee to vote is a poll tax, one of the barriers used to keep Black citizens from voting.

In 1877 federal troops left the South and Reconstruction ended. Southern states then passed segregation laws and used poll taxes and other barriers for most of the next century. The three amendments stayed in the Constitution, but for most Black Southerners they were not made real until the 1960s.

**Four reasons for coming**

- **What did the deals of 1820 and 1850 do about the quarrel over slavery?** They bought time without settling it

- **What is it called when a state declares that it is leaving the Union?** Secession
- **What did the seceding states’ own declarations name as the cause of their leaving?** Slavery
- **Which document, in January 1863, declared the people enslaved in the rebelling states free?** The Emancipation Proclamation
- **Who was President throughout the Civil War?** Abraham Lincoln

**The Thirteenth, Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments**

- **Which amendment abolished slavery?** The Thirteenth
- **Which amendment made everyone born here a citizen, and promised due process and equal protection?** The Fourteenth
- **Which amendment said that the vote cannot be denied because of race?** The Fifteenth

**The end of Reconstruction, and what followed**

- **In which year did federal troops leave the South, and Reconstruction end?** 1877
- **What did Southern states pass after 1877 that kept Black and white people apart in schools, transport and public places?** Segregation laws
- **In which decade were the three amendments finally made real for most Black Southerners?** The 1960s

**To carry away**

- People came to the colonies for religious freedom, political liberty and economic opportunity, or to escape persecution. Indentured servants worked for years to pay for the trip, enslaved people were brought by force, many from 1619, and Native nations were already there.
- The quarrel with Britain was about who sets the tax. Parliament taxed people who had voted for nobody in it: “no taxation without representation”.
- The Declaration of Independence is 1776. The Constitution was written in 1787 and took effect in 1789, with Washington the first President.
- The country grew by buying land from France in 1803 and by wars with Britain, from 1812, and with Mexico, from 1846 to 1848. Native nations were removed from the land by agreements, purchase and force, including the Trail of Tears.
- Each new state raised the question of slavery, and the Dred Scott decision of 1857 ruled that Black people could not be citizens. Eleven Southern states seceded after Lincoln’s election and named slavery as the cause. The war ran from 1861 to 1865.
- Freed, citizens, vote: the Thirteenth Amendment (1865), the Fourteenth (1868, the one behind **Blocked by a right**) and the Fifteenth (1870). Reconstruction ended in 1877. Segregation laws and poll taxes followed, and most Black Southerners could not use the amendments until the 1960s.
