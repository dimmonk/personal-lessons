## Part 1 of 2: Working backward from a result, and a rate

### 1. Four types of problem with a number missing

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient-unknown`]

When a problem leaves a number out, such as how long a field is or how many of each item were bought, work out which of four types it is before you start. Each type has its own steps, and the wrong steps still give a tidy number that looks right.

A fencing firm tells you what its fence came to, and you want the length of the field. A recipe is written for ten pancakes and you are making twenty-four. A shop sold two items at two prices, and all you know is how many it sold and what it took. A rug is 3 m longer than it is wide and covers 28 m², and you want its width.

In Unit One, all four got the same answer: **“A missing number in a formula, rate or totals”**. Here you tell them apart. A wrong answer looks just as neat as a right one, so first look at what the problem gives you, and only then work it out.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“What is this problem about?”** Its answers:

- **“How whole numbers split and repeat”**: give this answer when you are given whole numbers and asked about equal groups, leftovers, which numbers multiply to make one, when repeats line up, or if a number can be written exactly.
- **“A missing number in a formula, rate or totals”**: give this answer when one or two numbers are left out, and you are given a formula, a rate such as so much for each thing, or totals that they have to fit. **This unit is about these problems.**
- **“An amount changing over time”**: give this answer when one amount changes by the same number added or multiplied each month or year, or changes once, and you are asked what it will be or when it reaches a target.
- **“Counting ways and chances”**: give this answer when you are asked how many ways something can be chosen or ordered, how likely at least one of several things is, or how likely a test result is to be right.
- **“Right triangles, or one shape at two sizes”**: give this answer when there is a right triangle, or two things of exactly the same shape at different sizes, and you are asked for a length, an area or a volume, or how they compare.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. Rearranging a formula

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-rearr`]

First: you know what a calculation came to, and you need the number that went into it.

*The field fence*

> A fencing firm works out the length of fence for a rectangular field this way: ⟦add the field’s length and width, then double the total⟧. A field is 7 m wide and ⟦needs 38 m of fence⟧. How long is the field?

The firm gave you its way of working out the fence, and what it came to: 38 m. One number it used, the length, is missing. It is like a friend saying “I thought of a number, added 7, doubled it, and got 38”: you work back from 38 to find what they thought of.

To find it, list everything done to the missing number, then undo each thing, starting with the one done last.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the calculation: add the length and width, then double the total.** This is the rule the result came from.
2. **Find the result: 38 m of fence.** You work back from this number.
3. **Find the number you are not told: the length of the field.** The width, 7 m, is given, so only the length is missing.
4. **Check that only one number is missing.** Two missing numbers would be a different type.

This is **Rearranging a formula**. The *formula* is told in words here, and you work it backward from its result.

You may also hear this called “changing the subject of a formula” or “solving for x”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Rearranging a formula**.

### 4. Worked: the list price of a jacket

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `solved`, id `solved-rearr-1`]

Now the steps with real numbers: a jacket, a discount and sales tax.

**The problem**

> A shop takes $8 off the list price of a jacket and then multiplies what is left by 1.2 to add the sales tax. A customer pays $54. What was the list price?

**The working, step by step**

- List what is done to the missing number, in the order it is done: Start from the list price. First 8 is taken away from it, then the total is multiplied by 1.2. The result is 54

The shop takes $8 off the list price, then multiplies by 1.2 for the tax. You need this order to undo it.

- Write the undoing of each one, last one first: Taking away 8 is undone by adding 8; multiplying by 1.2 is undone by dividing by 1.2. Last one first: dividing by 1.2, then adding 8

**You are asked:** This step carries the idea. Every statement below is true of the problem. Before you read the reason, choose the one that explains why this step is done.

- The last thing done sits on the outside, so it comes off first, like shoes before socks.
- Taking away 8 is undone by adding 8, and multiplying by 1.2 by dividing by 1.2.
- The result is 54, which is the number you start the undoing from.

**Shown as soon as you answer**

- The one that explains it: The last thing done sits on the outside, so it comes off first, like shoes before socks.
  - If you chose “Taking away 8 is undone by adding 8, and multiplying by 1.2 by dividing by 1.2.”: True, but that only lists the undoing. It does not say why the dividing comes first.
  - If you chose “The result is 54, which is the number you start the undoing from.”: True, but it says nothing about the order of the undoing.

The shop took 8 off first and multiplied by 1.2 second, so the multiplying is on the outside. Add the 8 back first and you get 54 + 8 = 62, and 62 ÷ 1.2 is about 51.67, which is wrong.

Divide by 1.2 first: 54 ÷ 1.2 = 45, the price with the 8 already off. Then add the 8 back.

- Apply the undoing to the result, one at a time: 54 ÷ 1.2 = 45; 45 + 8 = 53

Each line uses the number from the line before: 45 is the price with the $8 already off, and adding 8 puts it back.

- Check by running the calculation forward: 53 − 8 = 45; 45 × 1.2 = 54, which is the 54 the problem gives

The shop’s calculation on $53 should give the $54 in the problem. If it does not, an undoing was wrong or came in the wrong order.

**The result**

The list price was $53. Taking $8 off gives $45, and multiplying by 1.2 to add the sales tax gives the $54 the customer paid.


### 5. Proportion

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 5 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-prop`]

Second: you know how much goes with how many, and you need the amount for a different number.

*The pancake recipe*

> A recipe for pancakes ⟦uses 250 g of flour for 10 pancakes⟧. How much flour is ⟦needed for 24 pancakes⟧?

The recipe is written for 10 pancakes and you are making 24. So much flour goes with so many pancakes, and that stays true at any size: twice the pancakes needs twice the flour.

To scale it, find how many times as big 24 is as 10, then make the flour that many times as big.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the rate: 250 g of flour for 10 pancakes.** It says how two things go together: so much for so many.
2. **Find the new amount: 24 pancakes.** It is a new number of one of the two things in the rate.
3. **Find what you are asked for: the flour for 24 pancakes.** It is the other thing in the rate, at the new amount.
4. **Check that nothing is added on top and no result is given.** A fixed charge, or a result to undo, would make it **Rearranging a formula**.

This is **Proportion**. The two numbers stay in step: whatever happens to one happens to the other.

You may also hear this called “scaling by a rate” or “the unitary method” or “direct proportion”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Proportion**.

### 7. Worked: the price of 12 kg of apples

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `solved`, id `solved-prop-1`]

Now the steps with real numbers: apples at a market stall.

**The problem**

> A market stall sells 8 kg of apples for $6. How much do 12 kg cost?

**The working, step by step**

- Match the new amount to the same thing in the rate: The rate is 8 kg of apples for 6 dollars. The new amount is 12 kg of apples, so it is matched to the 8 kg of apples in the rate

The new amount is in kilograms, so compare it with the 8 kg in the rate, not with the dollars.

- Find how many times as big the new amount is: 12 ÷ 8 = 1.5, so the new amount is 1.5 times as big as 8

8 kg goes into 12 kg 1.5 times. A result below 1 would mean a smaller amount.

- Make the other number that many times as big: 6 × 1.5 = 9

**You are asked:** This step carries the idea. Every statement below is true of the problem. Before you read the reason, choose the one that explains why this step is done.

- A rate keeps its two numbers in step, so 1.5 times the apples costs 1.5 times the price.
- 6 × 1.5 = 9, which is the price of 12 kg of apples at this rate.
- 12 is more than 8, so the price has to be more than 6.

**Shown as soon as you answer**

- The one that explains it: A rate keeps its two numbers in step, so 1.5 times the apples costs 1.5 times the price.
  - If you chose “6 × 1.5 = 9, which is the price of 12 kg of apples at this rate.”: True, but that is the arithmetic. It does not say why the price is multiplied.
  - If you chose “12 is more than 8, so the price has to be more than 6.”: True, but it does not say what to do to the price.

The rate says 8 kg of apples and $6 go together. Twice the apples, 16 kg, costs twice as much, $12. Half the apples, 4 kg, costs half as much, $3.

Here 12 kg is 1.5 times 8 kg, so the price is 1.5 times $6.

- Check the direction: 12 kg of apples is more than 8 kg of apples, so the answer should be more than 6 dollars, and 9 is more

More apples must cost more than $6, and $9 does. This check catches a rate scaled the wrong way.

**The result**

12 kg of apples cost $9.


### 8. A price for each unit, with a charge on top

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 8 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `exception`, id `exc-bill`]

Real problems are less tidy. A price for each unit looks like a rate, so watch this bill.

*The electricity bill*

> An electricity bill has a standing charge of $8, plus 25 cents for each unit of electricity used. This month’s bill is $38. How many units were used?

The bill gives 25 cents for each unit of electricity, which is so much for so many, and it asks for a number of units. That looks like **“A rate to scale up or down”**. Yet the answer is **“A formula and the result it came to”**.

**You are asked:** This looks like **Proportion**. Before you read why it is **Rearranging a formula**, tap the words in the problem that settle it.

The pieces you can tap:
1. “An electricity bill has a standing charge of $8, plus 25 cents for each unit of electricity used.”
2. “This month’s bill is $38.”
3. “How many units were used?”

**Shown as soon as you tap.** The words are “An electricity bill has a standing charge of $8, plus 25 cents for each unit of electricity used.”.
- If you tapped “This month’s bill is $38.”: That is the result. It does not show whether a charge is added on top.
- If you tapped “How many units were used?”: That is the question. The words that settle it are in the first sentence.

**Why this is Rearranging a formula and not Proportion**

Look at what else is in the bill: a standing charge of $8 that you pay whether you use 1 unit or 1,000. So the bill is not just 25 cents times the units. It is 8 plus 0.25 times the units.

There is no new amount to scale a rate to. There is a result, the $38 bill, and you are asked what number of units produced it. A price for each thing with a fixed amount on top is **“A formula and the result it came to”**.

**How to tell them apart**

Is there a calculation and its result, or a fixed charge added on top of a price for each one? Or is there only a rate and a new amount of the same thing?

When a problem shows both **“A rate to scale up or down”** and a fixed amount added on top of the rate, like a service-call fee or a flat monthly charge, the answer is **“A formula and the result it came to”**.

Take away the $8 and ask for the cost of 120 units at 25 cents each, and it would be **“A rate to scale up or down”**.

### 9. System of equations

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 9 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-simul`]

Third: two numbers are missing, and you are given two facts about them.

*The club’s balls*

> A sports club ⟦bought 14 balls, some footballs at $6 each and some volleyballs at $9 each⟧, and ⟦spent $96 in all⟧. How many of each did it buy?

Two numbers are missing: how many footballs and how many volleyballs. The first fact is a count: together they are 14. The second is a total: footballs cost $6, volleyballs cost $9, and together they came to $96.

One fact alone is not enough: there are many ways to buy 14 balls, and many ways to spend $96. Only one pair fits both. Use the count to leave a single missing number in the total, then undo it like any calculation.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the two missing numbers: the footballs and the volleyballs.** Two missing numbers is the first sign.
2. **Find the count: 14 balls in all.** It is the first fact, and it is about both missing numbers.
3. **Find the total: $96 in all, at $6 and $9 each.** It is the second fact, and it is about both as well.
4. **Check that each fact alone leaves many answers.** Only the two together give one pair.

This is **System of equations**. Each fact is an equation, a statement that two amounts are equal, and you work out both numbers together.

You may also hear this called “simultaneous equations” or “two equations with two unknowns”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **System of equations**.

### 11. Worked: pens and notebooks for an office

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 11 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `solved`, id `solved-simul-1`]

Now the steps with real numbers: pens and notebooks, a count and a bill.

**The problem**

> An office bought 20 items, some pens at $2 each and some notebooks at $5 each, and paid $61 in all. How many pens and how many notebooks did it buy?

**The working, step by step**

- Give each missing number a letter, and write the two facts: x is the number of pens and y is the number of notebooks. The count fact: x + y = 20. The totals fact: 2 × x + 5 × y = 61

The count fact says how many items there are. The totals fact says what they cost: 2 × x is what all the pens cost.

- Use the count fact to write one letter using the other: From x + y = 20, x = 20 − y

There are 20 items and y of them are notebooks, so the pens are the rest: 20 − y.

- Put that into the totals fact, so that only one letter is left: 2 × (20 − y) + 5 × y = 61

**You are asked:** This step carries the idea. Every statement below is true of the problem. Before you read the reason, choose the one that explains why this step is done.

- With x swapped for 20 − y, only one letter is left, and one letter has one answer.
- 2 × (20 − y) is what the pens cost, and 5 × y is what the notebooks cost.
- The totals fact says the whole bill is $61, and the bill has to come out right.

**Shown as soon as you answer**

- The one that explains it: With x swapped for 20 − y, only one letter is left, and one letter has one answer.
  - If you chose “2 × (20 − y) is what the pens cost, and 5 × y is what the notebooks cost.”: True, but it does not say why x is swapped out.
  - If you chose “The totals fact says the whole bill is $61, and the bill has to come out right.”: True, but it does not say why x is swapped out.

On its own the totals fact has many answers. 2 × x + 5 × y = 61 fits 28 pens and 1 notebook, and it fits 3 pens and 11 notebooks.

The count fact says there are 20 items, and only one of those pairs adds up to 20. Swapping x for 20 − y builds that into the totals fact, so only y is left.

- Solve for the letter that is left: 2 × 20 = 40, so 40 − 2 × y + 5 × y = 61; that is 40 + 3 × y = 61; 3 × y = 61 − 40 = 21; y = 21 ÷ 3 = 7

Multiply out the parentheses, then put the y parts together: 5 × y − 2 × y is 3 × y. Then undo it like any calculation: take away 40, then divide by 3.

- Find the other number from the count fact: x = 20 − 7 = 13

There are 20 items and 7 are notebooks, so 13 are pens.

- Check both facts: 13 + 7 = 20; 2 × 13 + 5 × 7 = 26 + 35 = 61. Both hold

Check the facts as first written, because only that shows the pair fits both.

**The result**

The office bought 13 pens and 7 notebooks.


### 12. Rearranging a formula or System of equations: telling them apart

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 12 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-rearr-simul`]

These two look alike in a shop: a total of prices can have one missing number or two.

**Problem A**

> Mia buys 5 plants at $9 each and some pots at $4 each, and pays $73. How many pots?

**Problem B**

> A garden center sold 12 items, some plants at $9 each and some pots at $4 each, and took $73 in all. How many plants and how many pots were sold?

**What to compare.** Both problems are at the same garden center, with plants at $9, pots at $4 and a bill of $73. Compare one thing: how many numbers are missing?

**You are asked:** Which problem gives the answer **“Two missing numbers and two facts about them”**? (Problem A / Problem B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Problem B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In A, Mia buys 5 plants and some pots, and pays $73. Only one number is missing, how many pots, and one calculation has a result to undo. That is **“A formula and the result it came to”**.

In B, the garden center sold 12 items, plants and pots, for $73, and nobody says how many of either. Two numbers are missing, and two facts are given: the count and the total. That is **“Two missing numbers and two facts about them”**.

The prices and the total are the same in both. What changes is how many numbers are missing: one or two.

**How to tell them apart**

How many numbers are left out, and how many separate facts are given about them? One number and one result, or two numbers and two facts?


### 13. A number multiplied by itself

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 13 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-squared`]

The last type uses one word you may only half know. Here it is first, with tiles you can picture.

*The square of tiles*

> A tiler lays square tiles to make a bigger square. A square with 5 tiles along each side takes 5 × 5 = 25 tiles, and one with 6 along each side takes 6 × 6 = 36. A square with 12 along each side takes 12 × 12 = 144 tiles.

The tiles in a square are the side times itself: 5 × 5 = 25, 6 × 6 = 36, 12 × 12 = 144. A number times itself comes up so often that it has its own word, *squared*, and a small raised 2 to write it: 5² is 25. It is not doubling: 5² is 25, not 10.

Letters work the same way. If the side of a square is x, its area is x × x, written x². A whole group can be multiplied by itself too: (x + 3)² means (x + 3) × (x + 3).

A minus number times itself gives a plus number, because a minus times a minus is a plus: −5 × −5 = 25. So 5 and −5 both give 25 when you multiply them by themselves.

**The word for this.** *Squared*: multiplied by itself, and written with a small raised 2: 5 squared, written 5², is 5 × 5 = 25.


### 14. Quadratic equation

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 14 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-quad`]

Fourth: the missing number is multiplied by itself, so it shows up twice.

*The square rug*

> A square rug is made longer: a shop ⟦adds a 2 m strip along one side⟧, so the rug becomes a rectangle with ⟦an area of 48 m²⟧. How long was each side of the square rug?

The rug started as a square, so its width is the missing side. After the strip, its length is that side plus 2. So the area, 48 m², is the missing side times a number with the missing side in it.

That is what makes it different from the first type. Here the missing number is used twice, so you cannot undo it one thing at a time. Instead you add the one number that turns a side of the equation into a number times itself, then undo that with a *square root*.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the result: an area of 48 m².** The missing side has to fit this number.
2. **Find how the sides are linked: the length is the width plus 2.** This is what puts the missing side into the area twice.
3. **Check that the missing side shows up twice: as the width, and inside the length.** Used only once, it would be **Rearranging a formula**.

This is **Quadratic equation**. Think of a square: its area is its side multiplied by itself.

You may also hear this called “a squared unknown”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Quadratic equation**.

Now the steps with real numbers: a banner whose length depends on its width.

**The problem**

> A banner for a trade stand is 3 m longer than it is wide, and its area is 70 m². How wide is the banner?

**The working, step by step**

- Write it as x² + b × x = c, with x² on its own: x × (x + 3) = 70. Multiply out: x × x is x², and x × 3 is 3 × x, so x² + 3 × x = 70

The banner is x wide and x + 3 long, so x shows up twice in the area. Here b is 3, the number in front of x, and c is 70, the area.

- Add the square of half the number in front of x to both sides: Half of 3 is 1.5, and 1.5 × 1.5 = 2.25. x² + 3 × x + 2.25 = 70 + 2.25 = 72.25

**You are asked:** This step carries the idea. Every statement below is true of the problem. Before you read the reason, choose the one that explains why this step is done.

- The extra number makes the left side one number times itself, which a *square root* can then undo.
- Half of 3 is 1.5, and 1.5 × 1.5 = 2.25, so 2.25 is the number to add.
- It is added to both sides, so the equation stays true and balanced.

**Shown as soon as you answer**

- The one that explains it: The extra number makes the left side one number times itself, which a *square root* can then undo.
  - If you chose “Half of 3 is 1.5, and 1.5 × 1.5 = 2.25, so 2.25 is the number to add.”: True, but that is the arithmetic. It does not say why this is the number to add.
  - If you chose “It is added to both sides, so the equation stays true and balanced.”: True, but that is why it goes on both sides, not why it is this number.

x² + 3 × x is not a number times itself yet, so a *square root* cannot undo it. (x + 1.5) times itself comes to x × x + 1.5 × x + 1.5 × x + 1.5 × 1.5, which is x² + 3 × x + 2.25.

So x² + 3 × x is only 2.25 short. Half of 3 is 1.5 because 1.5 × x shows up twice, and 1.5 × 1.5 is the corner piece. The same 2.25 goes on the right side so both sides stay equal.

- Write the left side as one number *squared*: x² + 3 × x + 2.25 = (x + 1.5) × (x + 1.5), so (x + 1.5)² = 72.25

x² + 3 × x + 2.25 is exactly (x + 1.5) times itself, so x is now in one place and a *square root* can undo it.

- Take the *square root* of both sides, keeping both answers: The *square root* of 72.25 is 8.5, and −8.5 × −8.5 is also 72.25, so x + 1.5 = 8.5 or x + 1.5 = −8.5

- Take away half the number in front of x from each: x = 8.5 − 1.5 = 7, or x = −8.5 − 1.5 = −10

- Throw out any answer the problem rules out, and check the one left: −10 cannot be right, because a banner cannot have a width below zero, so x = 7. Check: 7 × (7 + 3) = 7 × 10 = 70

A banner cannot be −10 m wide, so that answer goes. Then 7 goes back into the problem: 7 × 10 = 70.

**The result**

The banner is 7 m wide and 10 m long: 7 × 10 = 70 m².

### 18. The one question that tells the four types apart

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 18 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-a1`]

Here is the question and its four answers in one place.

**The question:** **“What does the missing number have to fit?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other three.

- **“A formula and the result it came to”**
  - Give this answer when you are given a formula, or a calculation in words such as a fee plus so much for each unit, the result it came to, and every number in it but one.
  - It leads to **Rearranging a formula**.
- **“A rate to scale up or down”**
  - Give this answer when you are given so much for so many things, like 300 g of rice for 4 people, and a new number of things, with the rate per thing, not per year, and nothing added on top.
  - It leads to **Proportion**.
- **“Two missing numbers and two facts about them”**
  - Give this answer when two numbers are left out and you are given two separate facts about them, like how many there are in all and what they cost in all.
  - It leads to **System of equations**.
- **“A formula where the missing number is multiplied by itself”**
  - Give this answer when you are given a formula or a fact where the missing number is multiplied by itself (written t², and read “t squared”), and the result it must come to.
  - It leads to **Quadratic equation**.

**Why it matters**

What it has to fit decides how you find it. A formula is undone backward, the last thing done to the missing number undone first. A rate is scaled up or down. Two facts about two missing numbers are combined until only one missing number is left. A missing number multiplied by itself needs steps of its own, and can have two answers.

The wrong steps give a number that looks just as neat as the right one, and nothing in the number says it is wrong. Only the question can tell you which steps to use, and only the words of the problem can answer it. That is why you ask it before any working.

**How to answer it**

1. **Read the last sentence first, to see which number is missing.** The question is usually there.
2. **Find what the rest of the problem gives you.** That is what the missing number has to fit.
3. **A rule and its result? That is “A formula and the result it came to”.** You undo the rule, last thing first.
4. **So much for so many, and a new amount? That is “A rate to scale up or down”.** You scale the rate to the new amount.
5. **Two missing numbers and two facts? That is “Two missing numbers and two facts about them”.** You use one fact to leave one missing number.
6. **The missing number multiplied by itself? That is “A formula where the missing number is multiplied by itself”.** It needs steps of its own.
7. **Find the exact words that show your answer.** If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Some problems show two answers at once. A price for each thing with a fixed charge on top is **“A formula and the result it came to”**, not **“A rate to scale up or down”**. A rule and its result with the missing number in it twice is **“A formula where the missing number is multiplied by itself”**, not **“A formula and the result it came to”**.

- Rearranging a formula or Proportion: Is there a calculation and its result, or a fixed charge added on top of a price for each one? Or is there only a rate and a new amount of the same thing?
- Rearranging a formula or System of equations: How many numbers are left out, and how many separate facts are given about them? One number and one result, or two numbers and two facts?
- Rearranging a formula or Quadratic equation: Does the missing number appear once in the calculation, or is it multiplied by itself?

### 20. What to carry away

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 20 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap-unknown`]

You have now worked problems of all four types on your own.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What does the missing number have to fit?
- A formula and the result it came to → Rearranging a formula
- A rate to scale up or down → Proportion
- Two missing numbers and two facts about them → System of equations
- A formula where the missing number is multiplied by itself → Quadratic equation

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Rearranging a formula**: a formula, or a calculation in words, the result it came to, and one number in it that you are not told.
- **Proportion**: so much for so many of something (300 g of rice for 4 people), a new number of that thing, and the question what the first amount becomes.
- **System of equations**: two numbers you are not told, and two separate facts about them, such as how many there are in all and what they cost in all.
- **Quadratic equation**: a formula or a fact where the missing number is multiplied by itself, and the result it must come to.

**To carry away**

- Before any working, ask what the problem gives that the missing number has to fit, and find the words that say it. The question is: **“What does the missing number have to fit?”**
- A rule and its result is **Rearranging a formula**. So much for so many and a new amount is **Proportion**. Two missing numbers and two facts is **System of equations**. A missing number multiplied by itself is **Quadratic equation**.
- The numbers do not tell you the type. The same 28 m² rug can be **Rearranging a formula**, if you are asked for a length from a width, or **Quadratic equation**, if you are asked for a width when the length is 3 m more.
- Two types can pass for another. A price for each thing with a fixed charge on top is **Rearranging a formula**, not **Proportion**. A rule with the missing number in it twice is **Quadratic equation**, not **Rearranging a formula**, even when it comes with a result.
- For **Rearranging a formula**: list what is done to the missing number, undo each thing starting with the last, and check by running the *formula* forward.
- For **Proportion**: find how many times as big the new amount is, make the other number that many times as big, and check that more of one means more of the other.
- For **System of equations**: use the count fact to leave one missing number in the totals fact, solve it, find the other number from the count fact, and check both facts.
- For **Quadratic equation**: write the equation as x² + b × x = c, add half of b, *squared*, to both sides, take the *square root*, keep both answers, take away half of b, and throw out any answer the problem rules out.
