# US Civics & History: Who can stop it

Revision 1, live. Part: Who can stop it. Where the subject ends up: Answer the citizenship interview’s civics questions aloud from memory, and, reading a news story about a US law, order or ruling, say who acted, whether it was theirs to do, who can stop it, and whether to take the story as it stands or check who is behind it first.

## The why

A government act is not always the last word. Six things can stop or undo one. A story often names more than one, but only one is at work now: under way, or about to happen. Find it.

## Step 1: Six ways to stop it

A plain table, "The six checks":

> What  |  How it stops it  (the column heads)
> A veto, and a vote to override it  |  A president, governor or mayor can refuse to sign. Lawmakers can pass it anyway with a large vote: in Congress, two-thirds of each house.
> A court  |  Once someone has sued, a court can block a law or order that breaks the Constitution or another law. No court sits above the Supreme Court.
> A new law  |  Lawmakers can pass a new law that changes or cancels an old one.
> A higher level’s law  |  Federal law wins over a state or local law that clashes with it; state law wins over a city or county rule.
> The voters  |  Voters can elect different lawmakers, governors, mayors or a different president.
> An amendment  |  It changes the Constitution itself. Two-thirds of both houses of Congress must vote for it, and three-fourths of the states must approve it.

At work means under way or about to happen: a veto promised, a lawsuit filed, a vote planned, a higher level saying its law wins, an election coming on this question, an amendment begun.

## Step 2: A worked example

A news story, from "The Metro Post":

> # Congress passes pay raise for federal workers
>
> Congress passed a bill on Tuesday that raises the pay of federal workers by 4 percent. The president said she will veto it when it reaches her desk. Supporters said they do not have enough votes to pass it over a veto.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**Which check does the story show at work?**
- A veto, and a vote to override it
- A court
- Lawmakers passing a new law that changes or cancels it
- A higher level’s law: federal over state and local, state over local
- The voters
- An amendment to the Constitution

The working, one step at a time:
1. Find who acted: The story says Congress passed a bill that raises the pay of federal workers.
1. Find the check at work: A president can refuse to sign a bill. Congress can pass it anyway only if two-thirds of the House and two-thirds of the Senate vote for it again.

Then the answer: A veto, and a vote to override it. A president can refuse to sign a bill. Congress can pass it anyway only if two-thirds of the House and two-thirds of the Senate vote for it again.

## Step 3: A wrong idea

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “The courts can fix anything.” A court acts only when someone brings it a dispute, and it cannot change the Constitution.
The right line: Read for the check at work now. A lawsuit counts only once it is filed; an election only when it is coming and this is the question.

## Step 4: Two ways to cancel a rule

| A | B |
|---|---|
| A judge cancels a rule after neighbors sue. | Lawmakers cancel a rule by passing a new one. |

Compare: The first needs a lawsuit; the second needs a vote.
