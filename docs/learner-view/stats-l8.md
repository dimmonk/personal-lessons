# Statistical Claims: Read the chart’s numbers

Revision 1, live. Part: Read the chart’s numbers. Where the subject ends up: When a headline and its article, a chart, an ad or a post on your phone is about to make you believe, share or act on a number, you can say whether the number shows what it is used to claim, point to the line that decides it (or say what is missing), and say how big it really is in plain counts, such as from 3 in 1,000 to 2 in 1,000 over ten years.

## The why

A chart can make a small change look huge, or a return to normal look like a boom, without a single wrong number.
Studies of charts found that knowing the trick does not stop it working: people still judge by how tall the bars look. What works is reading the numbers. Here you learn to do that every time.

## Step 1: Read the numbers, not the picture

First, look where the scale on the side starts. If it starts above zero, the bars or the line show only the top part of each number, so a small change looks big.
Second, work out the real change from the numbers: the change, divided by where it started, times 100. From 200 to 210 is a change of 10; 10 ÷ 200 × 100 = 5, so up 5%. A fall works the same way: from 200 to 150 is down 50, and 50 ÷ 200 × 100 = 25, so down 25%. The box takes the size of the change, up or down, so you type 25 for both; read which way it went off the chart. No change is 0.
Third, look at the time the chart covers. A line that starts at a low year shows a rise from the low, not from the usual level. Measure from a usual year, and look for earlier years where the claim needs them.
Then check the claim against the numbers, as you did with a risk: “doubles” needs about 100% more and “triples” about 200% more; “by half” is about 50%, “by a third” about 33%, “by a quarter” about 25%, “by a fifth” about 20% and “by a tenth” about 10%, up or down. If the numbers fit, it holds up, even when the scale starts above zero. If they show less, it does not show it. If the chart and the text leave out what the claim needs, such as earlier years for “highest ever” or the size of each town for a total, you cannot tell; a chart that covers all the years there are can show “ever”.
Tap the sentence that gives the numbers the claim rests on. If only the chart gives them, tap Not given.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “If one bar is three times as tall, the number is three times as big.”
The right line: Only when the scale starts at zero. On a scale that starts at 98, the numbers 100 and 104 make bars 2 and 6 steps tall: three times as tall, and only 4% bigger.

## Step 2: A worked example

A post, from "Sam Ortega · @samshops · 4h":

> Grocery prices at Hillview Market have doubled in a year. Look at this! 😡 Time to shop somewhere else.

A bar chart, "Hillview Market: a basket of 20 groceries". The scale runs from 49 to 53, in dollars, and does not start at zero. May 2024 50, May 2025 52. The values are written on it. Source: Hillview Market price board.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**What is this claiming?**
(the options come in an order that depends on the seed of the question)
- Groceries at Hillview Market cost about twice what they did a year ago
- Groceries at the other markets in town cost about twice what they did a year ago
- Hillview raised its prices to make more money

The working, one step at a time:
1. Find what the post claims.: “Doubled” says the basket costs about twice what it did.
1. Find the sentence with the numbers.: Neither sentence gives a number; only the chart does, so Not given.
1. Read the scale and work out the real change.: The scale starts at 49, not 0. The bars say $50, then $52: a change of $2. 2 ÷ 50 × 100 = 4, so up 4%. The second bar looks three times as tall only because the scale starts at 49.
1. Check the claim against the numbers.: Doubling would be up about 100%. The numbers show 4%, so the chart’s own numbers show the post goes too far.

Then the answer: Groceries at Hillview Market cost about twice what they did a year ago / Not given / 4% / Does not show it. The scale starts at 49, so the bars show only the top few dollars of each price, and $52 looks three times as tall as $50. The numbers say up $2, which is 4%, not a doubling.
What you would need to see: You would need the basket to cost about $100 now, twice $50.
