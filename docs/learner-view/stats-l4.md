# Statistical Claims: A test says yes

Revision 1, live. Part: A test says yes. Where the subject ends up: When a headline and its article, a chart, an ad or a post on your phone is about to make you believe, share or act on a number, you can say whether the number shows what it is used to claim, point to the line that decides it (or say what is missing), and say how big it really is in plain counts, such as from 3 in 1,000 to 2 in 1,000 over ten years.

## The why

A test that is usually right can still be wrong most of the times it says yes. The headline says how well the test works. It does not say how many of the people who test positive really have it.
People who study how doctors read tests found what works best: picture 1,000 people and count them through the test. You will do that here, and then you can see through a claim like “99% accurate” in a minute.

## Step 1: Count 1,000 people

To see what a positive result means, count 1,000 people through the test.
First, how many of the 1,000 have it? Second, how many of those does the test find? Third, how many of the others does it flag anyway? Those are the false alarms. Fourth, add the two kinds of positives. How many of them are real?
To take “5 in 100” of a number, divide by 100 and multiply by 5: 5 in 100 of 980 is 980 ÷ 100 × 5 = 49.
For example, if 20 in 1,000 have it, and the test finds 8 in 10 of them (16), and flags 5 in 100 of the other 980 (49), then 65 test positive and 16 are real: about 1 in 4.
Then judge the headline by that count, whatever its words (“90% accurate”, “usually right”). If most of the positives are real, “a positive most likely means you have it” holds up. If most are false alarms, it does not. If the text leaves out how common it is, or how often the test flags people who do not have it, you cannot tell, unless it says in words that most positives turned out wrong.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “The test is 80% accurate, so a positive means an 80% chance I have it.”
The right line: “Accurate” can mean several things. In these questions the text says which: it is how many of the people who have it the test finds. None of them tells you how many of the positives are real: count the 1,000, then see.

## Step 2: A worked example

A news story, from "Eastbay Courier · Health":

> # New blood test is 80% accurate: a positive result means you most likely have Verrin syndrome
>
> The test finds 80 of every 100 people who have Verrin syndrome, and wrongly flags 5 of every 100 who do not. Among adults in the county, about 20 in every 1,000 have Verrin syndrome.
> The test is offered at county clinics.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**What is this claiming?**
(the options come in an order that depends on the seed of the question)
- Most people who test positive have Verrin syndrome
- A negative result means you most likely do not have Verrin syndrome
- For people with symptoms, a positive result means you most likely have Verrin syndrome

The working, one step at a time:
1. Find what the headline claims. “A positive result means you most likely have it” says most people who test positive have Verrin syndrome.
1. Find how common it is. The second sentence: about 20 in every 1,000 adults have Verrin syndrome.
1. Count 1,000 people through the test. 20 have it, and the test finds 8 in 10 of them: 16. The other 980 do not have it, and the test wrongly flags 5 in 100 of them, 980 ÷ 100 × 5 = 49 false alarms. In all 65 test positive, and 16 of them have it.
1. Check the headline against that. 16 of 65 is about 1 in 4. A positive result most likely does not mean you have it, so the headline says more than the numbers show.

Then the answer: Most people who test positive have Verrin syndrome / Among adults in the county, about 20 in every 1,000 have Verrin syndrome. / 16 of the 65 who test positive have it / Does not show it. Out of 1,000 people, 20 have Verrin syndrome and the test finds 16 of them. Of the 980 who do not, it wrongly flags 49, so 65 test positive and only 16 of them have it, about 1 in 4: most positives are false alarms, because so few people have it.
What you would need to see: You would need a test with fewer false alarms, or a group of people among whom many more have it.

A picture follows:
Out of 1,000 people: a row of 1,000 dots for each count, the first ones marked and read left to right, top to bottom: Have it, 20 in 1,000; Have it and test positive, 16 in 1,000; Do not have it but test positive, 49 in 1,000.
