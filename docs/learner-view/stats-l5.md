# Statistical Claims: Compared with what

Revision 1, live. Part: Compared with what. Where the subject ends up: When a headline and its article, a chart, an ad or a post on your phone is about to make you believe, share or act on a number, you can say whether the number shows what it is used to claim, point to the line that decides it (or say what is missing), and say how big it really is in plain counts, such as from 3 in 1,000 to 2 in 1,000 over ten years.

## The why

“Up 55%”, “twice as much”, “the best in the county”: each one is a comparison, and a comparison is only as good as what it compares with.
A number with nothing to compare it to tells you little. A number compared with the wrong thing can tell you the opposite of the truth. People who check claims first find what the number is compared with, then check that it is fair.

## Step 1: Compared with what

First say what the claim means: “up 55%” says things are worse than before; “twice as much” says one does more than the other. Then find the comparison: tap the sentence that gives the two numbers the headline compares (this year and an earlier one, one town and another). If no sentence gives both, tap Not given.
Then check that the comparison is fair. Same period: this June against last June, not against a quiet month, and not against one odd year. Same way of counting at both ends. Rates, not totals, when the sizes differ: crimes per 1,000 people, not crimes. The same mix inside the totals: compare groups of the same kind.
A number people are paid or praised for raising can rise without anything getting better. Look at what was counted.
Then the verdict. A fair comparison that shows the claim: it holds up. One the text itself shows is unfair, or that shows less: it does not show it. If what you would need is missing, such as the size of each town, you cannot tell.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “If the numbers are right, the comparison is right.”
The right line: The numbers can all be true and still compare the wrong things. A true count compared with an unfair starting point can say the opposite of what happened.

## Step 2: Fair and unfair

| A | B |
|---|---|
| A post, from "Lantern Bakery · 1d":  > Lantern Bakery sold 2,100 loaves in June, up from 1,900 in June last year. Same store, same hours. | A post, from "Lantern Bakery · 1d":  > Lantern Bakery sold 2,100 loaves in June, up from 700 in January. |

Compare: The same bakery and the same 2,100 loaves. Which compares like with like? The first compares June with June. The second starts from January, the slowest month, so any later month looks like a jump.

## Step 3: The same mix inside the totals

Two hospitals treat patients who are mildly ill and patients who are badly ill. Northside sends home 905 of its 1,000 patients, about 90%. Southside sends home 676 of its 1,000, about 68%. Northside looks better.
Now compare like with like. Of the mildly ill, Southside sends home 98% and Northside 95%. Of the badly ill, Southside sends home 60% and Northside 50%. Southside does better with each kind of patient.
Its total is lower only because most of its patients are badly ill: 800 of its 1,000, against 100 of Northside’s. When two groups hold a different mix, compare them kind by kind.

## Step 4: A worked example

A news story, from "Lakeview Beacon · Public Safety":

> # Break-ins up 55% in Lakeview
>
> Lakeview police counted 62 break-ins in the first three months of this year, up from 40 in the same months last year.
> In the same months of the four years before that, counted the same way, police recorded between 60 and 66 break-ins each year. Chief Dana Wells said patrols will increase on weekends.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**What is this claiming?**
(the options come in an order that depends on the seed of the question)
- Lakeview is having more break-ins than it usually does
- Break-ins are up 55% in Lakeview’s county
- Fewer weekend patrols make break-ins rise in Lakeview

The working, one step at a time:
1. Find what the headline claims.: “Up 55%” tells a reader that break-ins are worse than they have been: more than Lakeview usually has.
1. Find what it is compared with.: The first sentence: the same months last year, when there were 40.
1. Check that the comparison is fair.: In the four years before, the count was 60 to 66 each year. Last year’s 40 was one odd low year, so “up 55%” compares this year with that dip, and 62 is a usual year.

Then the answer: Lakeview is having more break-ins than it usually does / Lakeview police counted 62 break-ins in the first three months of this year, up from 40 in the same months last year. / Does not show it. “Up 55%” is true against last year, 62 against 40, but last year was an odd low year. In the four years before, the count was 60 to 66 each year, so 62 is a usual year for Lakeview, not more than usual.
What you would need to see: You would need this year’s count beside several years, which the story gives: it shows a usual year.
