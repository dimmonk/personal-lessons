# Basic Math: Percents backward and stacked

Revision 1, live. Part: Percents backward and stacked. Where the subject ends up: Work out the number a situation needs (a total, a price per one, a scaled amount, a percent or the price before one, how much paint or soil to buy, how high a ladder reaches, what savings grow to or a loan costs) and tell whether it is sensible, when a receipt, a recipe, a sale tag, a room or an offer hands you some numbers and a question and nobody names the method.

## The why

You paid $63 after 25% off: what was the price? Adding 25% back gives the wrong answer, and a store raising a price 20% and then taking 20% off does not land where it started.
Here you use the same rule as for a tip or a raise, 100% is the amount the percent is of, when that amount is the one you need.

## Step 1: Backward: divide by the multiplier

You paid $63 after 25% off. The 25% was of the price before, so that price is 100% and you paid 75% of it: paid = before × 0.75. Backward: before = $63 ÷ 0.75 = $84.
A total with 7% tax is 107% of the price before tax, so the price is the total ÷ 1.07.
Rough answers backward: ÷ 0.75 is the price plus a third of it; ÷ 0.80, plus a quarter; ÷ 1.25, less a fifth; ÷ 1.50, less a third. Some wrong ways land close to the right answer (tax taken off the total, percents added): only the exact answer and a look back that multiplies back catch them.
One percent after another: each is of the amount at that moment, so multiply each in turn. Sales tax after a discount is on the sale price, the amount at that moment. 20% up then 20% down is × 1.20 × 0.80 = × 0.96: 4% less than at the start. Never add the percents.
Words can mislead: a sign that says "now $96, 20% more than last year" invites × 1.20, but the 20% was of last year’s price, the unknown whole, so last year was $96 ÷ 1.20 = $80. Decide by which amount is 100%, not by the word.
A rate going from 6% to 7% went up 1 percentage point. As a percent of the old rate, that is 1 ÷ 6 × 100, about 17%.

A bar for the whole, "The price before the sale, $84, is 100%", cut into parts as wide as their percents: You paid $63 75%, 25% off, $21 25%.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “25% came off, so add 25% of $63 back: $78.75.”
The right line: The 25% was of the bigger price before. Adding it back to the smaller price falls short: divide $63 by 0.75.

## Step 2: A worked example

A receipt, "Outlet Store":

> Jacket  |  25% off the tag
> You paid  |  $63.00
> Returns  |  within 30 days

The working, one step at a time:
1. Say what is asked and what is given. Asked: the price before the sale. Given: you paid $63.00 after 25% off. The return days do not matter.
1. Make a rough answer in your head. You paid 75%, three quarters of the old price, so the old price is bigger: add a third of it back. About $84.
1. Name the kind of problem and draw it. Backward: the price before is the whole, 100%, and you paid 75% of it. So paid = before × 0.75, and before = paid ÷ 0.75.
1. Work it out exactly on the calculator, then look back. On the calculator: $63.00 ÷ 0.75 = $84.00. Look back: near the rough $84, and more than you paid.

Then the answer: $84 / $84.00. You paid 75% of the old price, so the old price is $63.00 ÷ 0.75 = $84.00.

A picture follows:
A bar for the whole, "The price before the sale, $84.00, is 100%", cut into parts as wide as their percents: You paid 75%, Came off 25%.
