## Part 1 of 2: People chasing a figure, and a changed count

### 1. A number went up. Did the thing it counts go up?

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 16*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

Before you believe "burglaries are up 30%" or "waiting times are down a third", check what else could have moved the figure. It is usually one of three things, and each needs a different check.

You have met claims like these. "Reported burglaries are up 30% since the new hotline." "Diagnoses have doubled in ten years." "Nine in ten deliveries now arrive on time." Each one gives you a figure and tells you what to make of it: more crime, more illness, better service.

A figure is not the thing it stands for. It is what someone wrote down, counted or measured. Most of the time the two move together. Sometimes the figure moves and the real thing stands still.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“Where does this claim first go wrong?”** Its answers:

- **“Who was counted”**: give this answer when the people or things the figure came from do not match the group the claim is about, or are too few to trust.
- **“What the number counts”**: give this answer when the figure could rise, fall or differ without the real thing it is taken to show doing the same. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“What it is compared with”**: give this answer when the figure is a percentage change, a test’s accuracy, or a total beside another total, and the claim leaves out what you need beside it to read it fairly.
- **“What caused what”**: give this answer when the claim says one thing made another happen, and the same result could have come about another way.
- **“Nothing goes wrong”**: give this answer when every part the claim uses checks out in order, and none of them goes wrong.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. Gaming the target

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 16*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-proxy`]

Start with the one every workplace knows: a figure that decides who gets paid.

*The delivery bonus*

> A delivery firm ⟦gives each driver a $200 bonus when 95 of every 100 of that driver's parcels are marked 'delivered on time', and a driver marks a parcel delivered by tapping a button on a handheld⟧. Last quarter 80 of every 100 parcels were marked on time. This quarter 96 of every 100 were, and the firm's report says: 'On-time delivery is up from 80% to 96%.' Customers' own confirmation messages, which the bonus does not use, show 79 of every 100 parcels reaching them on time last quarter and 79 of every 100 this quarter.

The report says on-time delivery rose by 16 of every 100 parcels (96 − 80 = 16), and nothing in it is false: the drivers really did mark 96 in 100 on time. But customers’ own messages show 79 in 100 on time in both quarters. The figure moved by 16 and the real thing moved by 0.

Getting a parcel to the door on time is hard. Tapping the button is free. Once $200 depends on the tap, the cheapest way to get more taps is to tap sooner: at the door before the customer comes to it, or from the van. So the taps went up and the parcels did not.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find who is paid on the figure: each driver gets $200 at 95 in 100.** People work on what they are paid for.
2. **Find who makes the figure: the driver taps the button himself.** The people who gain from a high figure should not be the ones who write it down.
3. **Look for the easy way to raise it: tap “on time” before the parcel arrives.** If raising the figure is easier than raising the real thing, expect it.

This is **Gaming the target**. Being paid on a figure is not enough by itself: the people also need an easy way to raise it without raising the real thing.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Look for a count that nobody is paid on: customers’ own messages, or a test with new questions.** If that count stayed flat while the figure rose, the real thing did not move.
2. **Until you find one, repeat only what the figure says: “marked on time”, not “on time”.** That keeps what you know apart from what you assume.

You may also hear this called “Goodhart’s law” or “teaching to the test” or “hitting the target but missing the point”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Gaming the target**.

### 4. Gaming the target or A real change: telling them apart

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 16*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-proxy-real`]

Two headlines can quote the same rise. In one the figure was worked on, and in the other it moved because the buses did.

**Story A**

> A city bus company gives its drivers a bonus when at least 90 of every 100 of their trips are logged 'on time'. At the end of each trip the driver presses 'on time' or 'late' on a panel. Over the past year the share of trips logged on time rose from 78 of every 100 to 90 of every 100, and the company's report says: 'Our buses are now on time 90% of the time.'

**Story B**

> A city bus company's depot computer logs the time each bus reaches each stop, from the bus's satellite position, and sets it beside the published timetable. The timetable did not change this year. No driver or manager is paid or ranked on the figure, and drivers cannot alter it. Over the past year the share of trips on time rose from 78 of every 100 to 90 of every 100, and the company's report says: 'Our buses are now on time 90% of the time.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the same bus company and give the same figure: trips on time rose from 78 of every 100 to 90 of every 100. Compare one thing: who makes the figure, and whether they gain if it is high.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Nothing goes wrong”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the drivers get a bonus at 90 in 100, and each driver presses “on time” or “late” himself. The figure can reach 90 without a single bus running earlier. That is **Gaming the target**.

In Story B nobody is paid on the figure, and a computer logs each trip from the bus’s satellite position against a timetable that did not change. Only the buses running earlier could move it from 78 to 90. That is **A real change**.

**How to tell them apart**

Who makes the figure, and who gains if it is high? Could they raise it without more of the real thing happening?


### 5. A new way of counting

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 5 of 16*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-defshift`]

In **Gaming the target** the people changed what they did. Now nobody does anything different: what changes is how the figure is made.

*The jobless count*

> A city's job office reports: 'Joblessness fell from 9% to 6% this year.' ⟦Last year the office counted as jobless every adult who had no job and had looked for work in the past twelve months. This year it counts only adults who have no job and have looked for work in the past four weeks⟧. Of the same 1,000 adults in the city who want work, 90 were counted jobless last year and 60 this year. The number of those 1,000 with no job at all was 90 last year and 90 this year.

Count it last year’s way and this year is still 90 in 1,000, which is 9%. The fall to 6% comes from 30 adults (90 − 60 = 30) who still have no job but stopped being counted, because they have not looked for work in the past four weeks.

Every figure has a definition behind it: an exact rule for what goes in the count. “Jobless” can mean no job at all, or no job and looking this month, or no job and looking this year, and each gives a different number for the same city. None of these is wrong. The trouble starts when the definition changes between the two ends of a comparison and the claim does not say so. Nobody has to be dishonest, and a new definition is often an improvement.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the change in what counts as jobless: last year, looked in the past twelve months; this year, looked in the past four weeks.** Two numbers made by different definitions are not the same measure.
2. **Check whether the real thing moved: the same 1,000 adults, and 90 of them still have no job.** If the real thing stood still while the figure moved, the counting did the moving.
3. **Look for the small print: “revised”, “now includes”, “no longer counts”, “new method”.** The change is usually written there, not in the headline.

This is **A new way of counting**. It covers a new definition of what counts, like this one, and a new tool that does the counting: a different scale, a meter, or the same one moved somewhere else.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Ask for the figure counted both ways in the same period: last year’s definition and this year’s, on the same adults.** The gap between the two is the part of the move that the counting made.
2. **If you cannot get it, compare only numbers made the same way: this year against this year.** Numbers made two ways cannot be set side by side.

You may also hear this called “a change of definition” or “a new measuring method”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **A new way of counting**.

### 7. A new way of counting or A real change: telling them apart

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 16*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-defshift-real`]

Here the second way again, beside a claim that holds. The headline is the same.

**Story A**

> A ski area reports: 'Average snow depth in February rose from 90 cm to 120 cm over the past ten years.' In year six the ski area moved its measuring pole from an open slope to a hollow behind the lodge, where wind drifts snow. A second pole left on the open slope read 91 cm in the first February and 92 cm in the tenth.

**Story B**

> A ski area reports: 'Average snow depth in February rose from 90 cm to 120 cm over the past ten years.' Every February the staff have read the same measuring pole, on the same open slope, at the same hour, and the pole has never been moved or replaced. No other pole on the mountain reads differently.

**What to compare.** Both stories come from the same ski area and give the same figure: average snow depth in February rose from 90 cm to 120 cm over ten years. Compare one thing: whether the pole that measures the snow stood in the same place all along.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Nothing goes wrong”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the ski area moved its pole in year six, into a hollow where wind drifts snow. A second pole left on the open slope read 91 cm, then 92 cm, so the snow barely changed (92 − 91 = 1 cm), and nearly all of the 30 cm rise comes from where the pole stands. That is **A new way of counting**.

In Story B the same pole has stood in the same place all along, and no other pole on the mountain reads differently. Only the snow could have moved the figure. That is **A real change**.

**How to tell them apart**

Was the figure counted with the same definition and the same tool all the way through? Did either change on a date?

## Part 2 of 2: More people looking, and one whole claim

### 8. More looking, not more happening

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 8 of 16*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-detection`]

The last way needs no pushing and no new definition. The counting stays the same, and a lot more of it gets done.

*The screening van*

> County leaders warn: 'Thyroid diagnoses in our county have quadrupled, from 20 a year to 80. The illness is spreading fast.' Over the same years the county ⟦began sending a free screening van to towns, and the number of people given the thyroid exam rose from 1,000 a year to 5,000⟧. It is the same exam, and a result is called positive by the same standard. Of those examined, 20 in 1,000 were diagnosed in the first year, which is 2 in every 100, and 80 in 5,000 in the last, which is 1.6 in every 100.

Diagnoses rose four times, from 20 to 80. But the number examined rose five times, from 1,000 to 5,000. If the illness had really spread, the share found among those examined would have gone up. It went down, from 2 in every 100 to 1.6.

A diagnosis only happens when someone is examined. Before the van, many people with the illness were never examined, so they were never counted. The van found more of what was already there. It did not make more of it.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find what the figure counts: how many diagnoses were found.** A count of what was found depends on how hard anyone looked.
2. **Look for more effort at finding: a free screening van, and 5,000 examined instead of 1,000.** More tests, more cameras and an easier way to report all do the same thing.
3. **Check the same standard was used both times: the same exam, the same bar for a positive.** If the standard changed too, the rise could come from the standard.

This is **More looking, not more happening**. “Detection” means finding something that was already there to be found.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Ask how many were looked at each time, not only how many were found: 1,000, then 5,000.** A count of what was found means little without how much looking there was.
2. **Divide the number found by the number looked at: 20 in 1,000, then 80 in 5,000.** A share that stays level or falls means only the looking grew.
3. **Until you have that share, say “more were found”, not “more is happening”.** That is all the count shows.

You may also hear this called “detection bias” or “surveillance bias”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **More looking, not more happening**.

**Story A**

> A nature club reports: 'The number of bird species recorded at Lake Ellis rose from 12 to 31 in two years. More kinds of bird are living there.' In the first year four volunteers searched the shore for 10 hours a month in all. In the second year fifteen volunteers searched it for 60 hours a month in all.

**Story B**

> A nature club reports: 'The number of bird species recorded at Lake Ellis rose from 12 to 15 in two years.' The same four volunteers searched the same stretch of shore for 10 hours a month in all, in both years, on the same days of the month, and a species counts when it is seen by the same standard.

### 11. A new way of counting or More looking, not more happening: telling them apart

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 11 of 16*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-defshift-detection`]

These two get mixed up because in both a count of what was found went up, and the people looked at may be the same. This card shows what separates them.

**Story A**

> A clinic's lab reports: 'Vitamin D deficiency has tripled: 20 of the 1,000 people tested last year were found deficient, and 60 of the 1,000 tested this year.' In March the lab replaced its blood analyzer with a new model. On 50 blood samples tested on both, the new analyzer read about 4 points lower than the old one on each. A person is called deficient at a reading below 20 on either.

**Story B**

> A clinic's lab reports: 'Vitamin D deficiency has tripled: 20 people were found deficient last year, and 60 this year.' The lab has used the same blood analyzer for five years, and a person is called deficient at a reading below 20. Last year 1,000 people were tested. This year, after the clinic began offering the test to every patient over 40, 3,000 were tested. That is 2 found in every 100 tested last year, and 2 in every 100 this year.

**What to compare.** Both stories come from the same lab and give the same figure: vitamin D deficiency “tripled”, from 20 people found to 60. Compare one thing: did a different tool decide what counts as a find, or the same tool used on more people?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“More looking for it”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the lab tested 1,000 people each year, but this year’s new analyzer reads about 4 points lower on the same blood. A person is called deficient below 20, so the lower reading puts more people under the line: 20 found, then 60. The tool changed what counts as a find. That is **A new way of counting**.

In Story B the lab used the same analyzer and the same line for five years, but tested 3,000 people instead of 1,000. That is 2 found in every 100 tested both years: 20 in 1,000, then 60 in 3,000. Same tool, more testing. That is **More looking, not more happening**.

**How to tell them apart**

Did a different definition or tool decide what counts as a find, or the same one used on more people or more often?


### 12. The question you have been answering all along

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 12 of 16*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-measure`]

Since the delivery bonus you have asked the same question after each new name. Here it is with its three answers in one place.

**The question:** **“What else could push this figure up or down?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other two.

- **“People chasing the figure itself”**
  - Give this answer when the people measured are paid or judged on the figure, like a target or a bonus, and could raise it without more of the real thing it is meant to show.
  - It leads to **Gaming the target**.
- **“A new rule or tool for counting it”**
  - Give this answer when what counts, or the tool that counts it, changed during the time the figure covers, like a new definition or meter, and that alone could move the figure.
  - It leads to **A new way of counting**.
- **“More looking for it”**
  - Give this answer when more effort went into finding the thing during that time, like more tests or cameras, and finding more could raise the figure with no more of it happening.
  - It leads to **More looking, not more happening**.

**Why it matters**

The three names differ by what moved the figure instead of the real thing. Each sends you to a different check: what people did to the figure, what changed in the counting, or how hard anyone looked.

So the check is different each time, and a check for one is no use for another. For **Gaming the target**, look for a count that nobody is judged on. For **A new way of counting**, look for the figure counted both ways in the same period. For **More looking, not more happening**, divide the number found by the number looked at.

**How to answer it**

1. **Read the whole claim, the last sentence too.** The sentence that says how the figure is made is often at the end.
2. **First look for Gaming the target: someone paid or ranked on the figure who also makes it.** If the people who make the figure gain from it, doubt it first.
3. **Next look for A new way of counting: a definition or a tool that changed on a date.** A change on a date can move the figure on its own.
4. **Then look for More looking, not more happening: more tests, more cameras or an easier way to report.** More looking finds more, even when nothing more is happening.
5. **None of the three? Believe the move: A real change.** Then only the real thing could have moved the figure.
6. **Find the exact words that show your answer.** If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Some claims look like two of these at once, such as a new phone system and a bonus on short calls. Each pair below has one question that separates it.

- Gaming the target or More looking, not more happening: Did the extra effort go into raising the number, or into finding the thing it counts? Who gains if the count is high?
- A new way of counting or More looking, not more happening: Did a different definition or tool decide what counts as a find, or the same one used on more people or more often?
- Gaming the target or A new way of counting: Did the way of counting change on a date? Or did the people who make the figure gain from a high one, with a way to raise it?

### 15. What to carry away

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 15 of 16*

What else could push this figure up or down?
- People chasing the figure itself → Gaming the target
- A new rule or tool for counting it → A new way of counting
- More looking for it → More looking, not more happening

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Gaming the target**: a figure people are paid, ranked or judged on, a rise in it, and a way they could raise it without more of the real thing it is meant to show.
- **A new way of counting**: a figure that rose or fell, and a change at the same time in what counts or in the tool that counts it, which could move the figure on its own.
- **More looking, not more happening**: a count of how many were found, more effort put into finding them, like more tests or cameras, and the rise taken as more of the thing happening.

**To carry away**

- Before you trust a figure that moved, ask: **“What else could push this figure up or down?”** Then find the words in the claim that answer it. If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.
- A figure is not the real thing it stands for. Someone counted it, in some way, with some amount of effort. Each of the three names is one way that can move the figure while the real thing stays put.
- The arithmetic usually settles which one: a second count from someone who gains nothing, the same people counted both ways, or the share found among those looked at.
- If you check all three and none fits, believe the move. That is **A real change**.
- Naming one of these does not make a claim false. It tells you what the figure cannot show yet, and what you would need to see.
