# Geography: Placing a place you have never heard of

Revision 1, live. Part: Placing a place you have never heard of. Where the subject ends up: After this subject you can put a named place on a map with no names, say what it borders and what water it is on, name a place that is lit, tell a country by its flag, and say roughly how far it is from you, when a place is named in the news, by a person or in a travel plan with no map in sight.

## The why

A story names a town you have never heard of, and the story means nothing until you know where it is. In about twelve minutes you will place ten towns, ports and regions you have never met by the part of the world they sit in, from the clue the story gives, and then work out which way two places lie from each other without a map, where the states and continents that hold them mislead.

## Step 1: How to place a place you have never met

A story rarely gives a map. It gives a clue: a country, a state, a coast, a neighbor. Use the same three questions. Area: which country, state or coast do the words name? Those are the deciding words; the name of the place itself tells you nothing. Touches: where is that, and what is it next to? Water: which ocean or sea does it face? Then tap the part of the world that holds it, one of the parts you placed in parts 1b and 1c. North America has three: Northern America (Canada, the United States, Greenland), Central America (Mexico down to Panama) and the Caribbean. South America stands on its own. Europe has four: Northern, Western, Southern and Eastern. Africa has five: Northern, West, Central, East and Southern. Asia has six: Western, Central, South, East, Southeast and Northern (Russia east of the Urals). Oceania here has two: Australia and New Zealand, and Melanesia. Mexico is in Central America on this map. After every answer the feedback gives the deciding words and the part.

## Step 4: The traps: north and east

Memory straightens maps: South America sits straight below North America in most heads, and Europe level with the United States. Judging two places by the state or continent that holds them goes wrong the same way. Latitude counts degrees north or south of the equator; longitude counts degrees east or west of Greenwich, so a bigger number of degrees west is farther west. Work out each answer from the two places, not from their units. These pairs are practice only: the check and the final test leave them out.

## Step 5: A worked example

Reno and San Diego. There is no map: say where the first lies from the second.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**Is Reno north or south of San Diego?**
- North
- South

The working, one step at a time:
1. Say what the larger units suggest. Reno is in Nevada and San Diego is in California, and Nevada lies east of California. So the units say Reno is east.
1. Compare the two places themselves. Reno is at 39.5° N and 119.8° W; San Diego is at 32.7° N and 117.2° W. A bigger number of degrees west is farther west: 119.8° W is farther west than 117.2° W.
1. Answer from the places. Reno is north of San Diego and west of it. The map after the answer shows both.

Then the answer: North / West. Reno is at 39.5° N and 119.8° W; San Diego is at 32.7° N and 117.2° W. So Reno is north of San Diego and west of it. Nevada lies east of California, yet Reno lies west of San Diego: judge two places by the places, not by the units that hold them.
