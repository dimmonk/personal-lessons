Before you work out how long a strip must be to run across the corner of a door, how tall a real bridge is from its model, or how much more soup a bigger pot holds, check which of four types of problem it is. The wrong steps still give you a number, and nothing in the number tells you it is wrong.

### 2. Pythagorean theorem

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-pyth`]

First type: you know two sides of a *right triangle* and want the third.

*The hike*

> A hiker ⟦walks 3 km due east and then 4 km due north⟧ on flat ground. She then wants to walk straight back to her starting point. How long is the walk straight back?

The answer is not 3 + 4 = 7. That is how far she walks, and the straight way back is shorter.

Here is why it works. Picture a square of floor tiles on each side of the triangle. On the 3 km side the square is 3 × 3 = 9 tiles. On the 4 km side it is 4 × 4 = 16 tiles. On the walk back it is 9 + 16 = 25 tiles, and a square of 25 tiles is 5 tiles along each edge, because 5 × 5 = 25. So the walk back is 5 km.

So, to find the third side: multiply each side you know by itself, add the two results, then find the number that multiplies by itself to make the total. If you were given the longest side, take one result away from the other instead of adding. The √ button on a calculator does the last part.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the *right triangle*: the walk east and the walk north meet at a square corner, and the walk straight back closes it.** These steps only work when one corner is square.
2. **Check what you are given: two sides, 3 km and 4 km, and no angle.** Two lengths and no angle is all these steps need.
3. **Check what you are asked: how long the walk straight back is, the side nobody gave you.** It is a length, and it is the third side.

A problem like this is **Pythagorean theorem**: two sides in, the third side out.

You may also hear this called “Pythagoras’ theorem” or “a² + b² = c²”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Pythagorean theorem**.

### 4. Worked: the path across a 30 m by 40 m yard

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `solved`, id `solved-pyth-1`]

A rectangular yard, a path from one corner to the opposite corner, and the steps for the first type with real numbers.

**The problem**

> A rectangular yard is 30 m wide and 40 m long. A path runs straight across it, from one corner to the opposite corner. How long is the path?

**The working, step by step**

- Find the longest side: The path is opposite the square corner of the yard, so it is the longest side. You know the two shorter sides, 30 m and 40 m

Later you add if you want this side, and take away if you were given it.

- Multiply each side you know by itself: 30 × 30 = 900; 40 × 40 = 1,600

The tile squares on the sides are what add up, not the sides themselves.

- Add the two results: 900 + 1,600 = 2,500

**You are asked:** This step carries the idea. Every statement below is true of the problem. Before you read the reason, choose the one that explains why this step is done.

- The tile squares on the two shorter sides together hold as many tiles as the square on the longest side.
- Adding 900 and 1,600 gives 2,500, a bigger number than either.
- The path across is shorter than walking round two edges.

**Shown as soon as you answer**

- The one that explains it: The tile squares on the two shorter sides together hold as many tiles as the square on the longest side.
  - If you chose “Adding 900 and 1,600 gives 2,500, a bigger number than either.”: True, but it only describes the sum. It does not say why you add.
  - If you chose “The path across is shorter than walking round two edges.”: True, and a good check on the answer, but it does not say why you add.

Try a triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5. The tile square on the side of 3 holds 3 × 3 = 9 tiles, the one on the side of 4 holds 4 × 4 = 16 tiles, and the one on the longest side, 5, holds 5 × 5 = 25 tiles. And 9 + 16 = 25.

That is true of every *right triangle*, whatever its size: 30, 40 and 50 give 900 + 1,600 = 2,500 = 50 × 50. It is not true without a square corner. So you add because the two short squares really do make up the long square.

- Find the number that multiplies by itself to make that result: 50 × 50 = 2,500, so the path is 50 m

The 2,500 tiles make one big square and the path is one edge of it: the *square root* of 2,500, which the √ button gives.

**The result**

The path is 50 m long. Going round two edges of the yard would be 70 m, so the path across saves 20 m, and a straight line is shorter than the two sides, as it should be.


### 5. Trigonometry

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 5 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-trig`]

Second type: you know one side and an angle in degrees, instead of two sides.

*The ski lift*

> A ski lift cable ⟦is 200 m long and rises at an angle of 30° above level ground⟧. How high above its bottom station is the top station?

It looks like too little to go on. But in a *right triangle*, one angle settles the shape, and then one length settles the size. A 30° slope always climbs half as far as the cable is long: 10 m of cable climbs 5 m, 100 m climbs 50 m, and 200 m climbs 100 m.

First name the sides from the angle. The side opposite the angle is the one that does not touch it. The side next to the angle is the one that touches it and is not the longest. The longest side is always opposite the square corner.

Your calculator keeps these fixed comparisons in three buttons, with the calculator set to degrees. Sin gives opposite ÷ longest: type 30 and you get 0.5, the half you just saw. Cos gives next to ÷ longest. Tan gives opposite ÷ next to.

To find the missing side, use the button that joins the side you know and the side you want. Here the cable is the longest side and the height is opposite the angle, so use sin: 200 × sin 30° = 200 × 0.5 = 100 m.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the *right triangle*: the cable, the ground, and the height straight up from the ground.** The height meets level ground at a square corner.
2. **Check what you are given: one side, the 200 m cable, and one angle, 30°.** The angle does the work of a second side.
3. **Check what you are asked: how high the top station is, another side of the triangle.** It is a length, and you do not have it yet.

A problem like this is **Trigonometry**: one side and an angle in, another side out.

You may also hear this called “sin, cos and tan” or “SOH CAH TOA”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Trigonometry**.

### 7. Worked: the height of a tower measured at 35°

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `solved`, id `solved-trig-1`]

A tower, an angle and one length, and the steps for the second type with real numbers.

**The problem**

> A surveyor stands on level ground 30 m from the foot of a church tower. She measures the angle from the ground up to the top of the tower, and it is 35°. How tall is the tower?

**The working, step by step**

- Name the three sides from the angle you were given: The angle is 35°. The longest side is the straight line from her feet to the top of the tower. The side opposite the angle is the height of the tower. The side next to the angle is the 30 m along the ground

The side opposite the angle does not touch it, and the side next to it touches it and is not the longest.

- Pick the calculator button that joins the side you know and the side you want: You know the side next to the angle (30 m) and want the side opposite it. Tan joins those two: tan = opposite ÷ next to

**You are asked:** This step carries the idea. Every statement below is true of the problem. Before you read the reason, choose the one that explains why this step is done.

- Each button joins two of the three sides, so you pick the one that joins the side you know and the side you want.
- The surveyor stands 30 m from the foot of the tower.
- The angle she measured is 35°.

**Shown as soon as you answer**

- The one that explains it: Each button joins two of the three sides, so you pick the one that joins the side you know and the side you want.
  - If you chose “The surveyor stands 30 m from the foot of the tower.”: True, but it does not say which of the three buttons to use.
  - If you chose “The angle she measured is 35°.”: True, and every button needs it, but it does not say which one to use.

There are three buttons, and each joins two of the three sides. Sin joins the side opposite the angle and the longest side. Cos joins the side next to the angle and the longest side. Tan joins the side opposite the angle and the side next to it.

Here you know the side next to the angle and want the side opposite it. The longest side is not in the problem at all, so the button is tan.

- Write the button’s sum with your numbers in: tan 35° = height ÷ 30

The side you want goes on top, and the side you know goes underneath.

- Get the side you want on its own: height = 30 × tan 35°

When the side you want is on top, multiply the side you know by the button’s value; when it is underneath, divide.

- Read the button’s value off the calculator, set to degrees, and finish the sum: tan 35° = 0.7002; 30 × 0.7002 = 21.006, so about 21.0 m

The calculator must be set to degrees because the angle is in degrees.

**The result**

The tower is about 21.0 m tall. That is less than the 30 m along the ground, as it should be for an angle under 45°.


### 8. Pythagorean theorem or Trigonometry: telling them apart

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 8 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-pyth-trig`]

Both find a side of a *right triangle*, and the same ramp can be given either way.

**Problem A**

> A builder makes a ramp up to a loading dock. The ramp is 6.5 m long along its slope, and it ends 6 m from the foot of the dock wall, on level ground. How high is the dock?

**Problem B**

> A builder makes a ramp up to a loading dock. The ramp is 6.5 m long along its slope, and it rises at an angle of 21° above level ground. How high is the dock?

**What to compare.** Both problems are about the same ramp, and both ask how high the loading dock is. Compare one thing: what is given besides the length of the ramp?

**You are asked:** Which problem gives the answer **“One side and one angle of a right triangle”**? (Problem A / Problem B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Problem B.

**Why this one and not the other**

Where the ramp ends 6 m from the foot of the wall, you have the ramp, 6.5 m, and one more length, 6 m. That is two sides and no angle: **“Two sides of a right triangle”**. The steps take the squares away: 6.5 × 6.5 = 42.25, 6 × 6 = 36, and 42.25 − 36 = 6.25, so the dock is 2.5 m high.

Where the ramp rises at 21°, you have the ramp, 6.5 m, and an angle. That is one side and one angle: **“One side and one angle of a right triangle”**. The steps use the sin button: 6.5 × sin 21° = 6.5 × 0.3584 = 2.33 m.

The ramp, the dock and the question are the same. Only what is given besides the ramp changes: a second length, or an angle.

**How to tell them apart**

Besides the one length that everyone can see, is a second length given, or an angle in degrees?

### 9. Similar shapes

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 9 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-similar`]

Third type: two things of exactly the same shape at different sizes, like a model and the real thing, or a photo and its enlargement.

*The footbridge model*

> A town council has ⟦a model of a new footbridge, an exact copy of it at a smaller size⟧. On the model the span measures 20 cm and the tower is 12 cm tall. The real span is 10 m. How tall is the real tower?

The model is an exact copy, so every length on the real bridge is the same number of times longer than the matching length on the model: the span, the tower, the arch, every cable. If one direction were stretched more than another, it would not be the same shape.

You can measure the span on both, so use it to find how many times longer the real bridge is. The span is 10 m, which is 1,000 cm, against 20 cm on the model: 1,000 ÷ 20 = 50 times longer. So the real tower is 12 × 50 = 600 cm, which is 6 m.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the two things of the same shape: the model and the real footbridge.** One is an exact copy of the other at a different size.
2. **Find a length measured on both: the span, 20 cm on the model and 10 m on the real bridge.** It shows how many times longer the real bridge is.
3. **Find the part you know on one only: the tower, 12 cm on the model.** This is the length you carry over to the real bridge.
4. **Check you are asked for a length: how tall the real tower is.** Asking how much area or volume would be a different type.

A problem like this is **Similar shapes**: a length you know on one thing, carried over to its copy.

You may also hear this called “similar triangles” or “scale drawings”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Similar shapes**.

### 11. Worked: how high an enlarged photo is

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 11 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `solved`, id `solved-similar-1`]

A photo and its enlargement, and the steps for the third type with real numbers.

**The problem**

> A photo is 10 cm wide and 15 cm high. It is enlarged as an exact copy so that it is 25 cm wide. How high is the enlargement?

**The working, step by step**

- Find a length that is measured on both things: The width is 10 cm on the photo and 25 cm on the enlargement. The height, which you want, is known on the photo only: 15 cm

The length measured on both shows how much bigger the enlargement is.

- Work out how many times longer the bigger one is: 25 ÷ 10 = 2.5

**You are asked:** This step carries the idea. Every statement below is true of the problem. Before you read the reason, choose the one that explains why this step is done.

- Every length on an exact copy is made the same number of times longer, so the number from the width works for the height too.
- The width goes from 10 cm to 25 cm, which is 2.5 times longer.
- The enlargement has to be wider than the photo.

**Shown as soon as you answer**

- The one that explains it: Every length on an exact copy is made the same number of times longer, so the number from the width works for the height too.
  - If you chose “The width goes from 10 cm to 25 cm, which is 2.5 times longer.”: True, but it does not say why the same number works for the height.
  - If you chose “The enlargement has to be wider than the photo.”: True, but it does not say why the same number works for the height.

An exact copy keeps its shape. If the width were 2.5 times longer and the height only 2 times longer, the picture would look squashed. So every length grows by the same number, and the width, which you know on both, gives it: 25 ÷ 10 = 2.5.

- Multiply the length you know by that number: 15 × 2.5 = 37.5 cm

Multiply when the length you know is on the smaller one, and divide when it is on the bigger one.

**The result**

The enlargement is 37.5 cm high.


### 12. A shadow makes a triangle, and is also a copy

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 12 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `exception`, id `exc-shadow`]

A shadow can make a problem look like the first type when it is really the third.

*The tree and its shadow*

> A woman 1.7 m tall stands in the sun, and her shadow on level ground is 2 m long. At the same moment a tree beside her casts a shadow 14 m long. How tall is the tree?

The woman is 1.7 m tall and her shadow is 2 m long. She stands straight up on level ground, so her height, her shadow and the line from her head to the tip of her shadow make a *right triangle*, and two of its sides are given. That looks like **“Two sides of a right triangle”**. But the answer here is **“The same shape at two sizes”**.

**You are asked:** This looks like **Pythagorean theorem**. Before you read why it is **Similar shapes**, tap the words in the problem that settle it.

The pieces you can tap:
1. “A woman 1.7 m tall stands in the sun, and her shadow on level ground is 2 m long.”
2. “At the same moment a tree beside her casts a shadow 14 m long.”
3. “How tall is the tree?”

**Shown as soon as you tap.** The words are “At the same moment a tree beside her casts a shadow 14 m long.”.
- If you tapped “A woman 1.7 m tall stands in the sun, and her shadow on level ground is 2 m long.”: That gives a *right triangle* and two of its sides, but the height you want is not on this triangle.
- If you tapped “How tall is the tree?”: That is the question. The words that settle it put the height you want on a second thing.

**Why this is Similar shapes and not Pythagorean theorem**

Look at what you are asked. The triangle you are given is the woman’s, but the height of the tree is not a side of it. It is a side of a second triangle, made by the tree, its shadow and the line from the top of the tree to the tip of its shadow.

The sun is at the same angle for both at the same moment, so the two triangles have the same shape. That makes the woman and the tree two things of exactly the same shape, with the shadow measured on both: 2 m and 14 m. The tree’s shadow is 14 ÷ 2 = 7 times as long, so the tree is 7 times as tall: 1.7 × 7 = 11.9 m.

**How to tell them apart**

Is the length you want a side of the same triangle whose other sides are given, or a length on a second thing of the same shape?

When a problem shows both **“Two sides of a right triangle”** and a second thing of the same shape at a different size, with the length wanted on that second thing, the answer is **“The same shape at two sizes”**.

If the problem had asked how far it is from the top of her head to the tip of her shadow, that line is the third side of her own triangle, and the answer would be **“Two sides of a right triangle”**.


### 13. First question: what the problem gives you

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 13 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-s1`]

Here is the first question and its three answers in one place.

**The question:** **“What are you given to work with?”**

**Its answers**

- **“Two sides of a right triangle”**
  - Give this answer when there is a right triangle, like a wall and the floor, you are given the lengths of two of its sides, and there is no angle besides the square corner.
  - Keeps **Pythagorean theorem**. Rules out **Trigonometry**, **Similar shapes** and the area or volume of a bigger or smaller copy.
- **“One side and one angle of a right triangle”**
  - Give this answer when there is a right triangle and you are given the length of one side and one other angle in degrees, like a ramp that rises at 5° or a ladder at 70° to the ground.
  - Keeps **Trigonometry**. Rules out **Pythagorean theorem**, **Similar shapes** and the area or volume of a bigger or smaller copy.
- **“The same shape at two sizes”**
  - Give this answer when there are two things of exactly the same shape, one bigger than the other, like a model and the real thing, or a person and a lamp post with their shadows.
  - Keeps **Similar shapes** and the area or volume of a bigger or smaller copy. Rules out **Pythagorean theorem** and **Trigonometry**.

**Why it matters**

Each is worked out differently. Two sides of a right triangle give the third by squaring them. One side and one angle give another side, because the angle fixes how the sides compare. Two things of the same shape are compared by how many times longer one is than the other.

The numbers cannot tell you the type: 3 and 4 can be two sides of a *right triangle*, or the same part measured on a model and on the real thing. What the problem gives you can.

**How to answer it**

1. **Say what each number in the problem is: a side of a *right triangle*, an angle in degrees, or a length on one of two copies.** What a number does decides the type, not how big it is.
2. **Two sides and no angle besides the square corner? That is “Two sides of a right triangle”.** The third side comes from squaring the two you have.

### 15. Square-cube law

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 15 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-sqcube`]

Fourth type: two things of the same shape again, but now you are asked about area or volume.

*The two mats*

> A small square mat measures 1 m along each side. A large mat of exactly the same shape measures 3 m along each side. ⟦How many times more floor does the large mat cover?⟧

The answer is not 3. The large mat is 3 times as long and 3 times as wide, so it is easy to say it covers 3 times as much floor. But look at what fits on it. Mats the size of the small one fit 3 along its length and 3 rows across: 3 × 3 = 9. The large mat covers 9 times as much floor.

A solid goes one direction further. A box 3 times as long, 3 times as wide and 3 times as tall is filled by 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 small boxes. So when something is 3 times longer, a length is 3 times as much, an area is 3 × 3 = 9 times as much, and a volume is 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 times as much.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the two things of the same shape: the small mat and the large mat.** It is two things of one shape, as in the third type.
2. **Find how many times longer the bigger one is: 3 m along each side against 1 m.** That one number is all the working needs.
3. **Check what you are asked about: how much floor the large mat covers, an area and not a length.** A length would be the third type, and an area or a volume is this one.

A problem like this is **Square-cube law**. The name says it: for an area, square the number of times longer (3 × 3); for a volume, cube it (3 × 3 × 3).

You may also hear this called “area and volume grow faster than length”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Square-cube law**.

### 18. Similar shapes or Square-cube law: telling them apart

*Unit Six · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 18 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-similar-sqcube`]

Both start from a copy at another size, so they are easy to mix up.

**Problem A**

> A printer prints a poster as an exact copy of a postcard. The postcard is 10 cm wide and 15 cm high, and the poster is 40 cm wide. How high is the poster?

**Problem B**

> A printer prints a poster as an exact copy of a postcard. The postcard is 10 cm wide, and the poster is 40 cm wide. The postcard uses 2 g of ink. How much ink does the poster use?

**What to compare.** Both problems are about the same postcard, printed as a poster that is 10 cm wide on the card and 40 cm wide on the poster. Compare one thing: what does each problem ask for?

**You are asked:** Which problem gives the answer **“How much area or volume something has”**? (Problem A / Problem B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Problem B.

**Why this one and not the other**

When the printer asks how high the poster is, that is **“How long, high or far something is”**. The poster is 40 ÷ 10 = 4 times longer, so its height is 15 × 4 = 60 cm: multiply by 4 once.

When the printer asks how much ink the poster uses, ink covers a surface, so that is **“How much area or volume something has”**. The same 4 times longer gives 4 × 4 = 16 times as much ink, so the poster uses 2 × 16 = 32 g. A surface has two directions, so multiply by 4 twice.

Same copy, same 4 times longer. Only what is asked changes: a length is multiplied once, and an area is multiplied by itself.

**How to tell them apart**

Does the problem ask how long a part is, or how much surface or room inside?


### 19. Second question: how long, or how much area or volume

The first question leaves two types together, because both start from a thing and its copy at another size. Here is the second question and its two answers in one place.

**The question:** **“Do you need a length, or an area or volume?”**

**Its answers**

- **“How long, high or far something is”**
  - Give this answer when you are asked how long, how high, how far or how wide something is.
  - Keeps **Pythagorean theorem**, **Trigonometry** and **Similar shapes**. Rules out **Square-cube law**.
- **“How much area or volume something has”**
  - Give this answer when you are asked how much surface (glass, pizza, floor) or how much space inside (paint in a can, water in a pot) something has, or how many times more of either.

When a copy is made some number of times longer, every length grows that many times, but the area grows by that number times itself, and the volume by that number three times over. So which one you need decides how you work it out.

3. **“How much paint”, “how much glass”, “how much water”, or “how many times more” of something that covers a surface or fills a solid, ask for an area or a volume.** A surface grows in two directions and a solid in three.
4. **A cost follows the area or the volume: glass for a pane with 4 times the area costs 4 times as much.** You pay for what you cover or fill.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What are you given to work with?
- Two sides of a right triangle → Pythagorean theorem
- One side and one angle of a right triangle → Trigonometry
- The same shape at two sizes → Similar shapes · Square-cube law

Do you need a length, or an area or volume?
- How long, high or far something is → Pythagorean theorem · Trigonometry · Similar shapes

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Pythagorean theorem**: a right triangle, the lengths of two of its sides, and the question how long the third side is.
- **Trigonometry**: a right triangle, the length of one side, one other angle in degrees, and the question how long another side is.
- **Similar shapes**: two things of exactly the same shape at different sizes, one part measured on both, another on one, and the question how long it is on the other.
- **Square-cube law**: two things of exactly the same shape at different sizes, how many times longer one is than the other, and the question how many times more area or volume it has.

**To carry away**

- Before any working, ask what the problem gives you and what it asks for, and find the words that say it.
- Two sides of a *right triangle*: **Pythagorean theorem**. One side and one angle in degrees: **Trigonometry**. Two things of the same shape: **Similar shapes** when you want a length, and **Square-cube law** when you want an area or a volume.
- The setting around the numbers does not tell you the type. The same ramp can be given with two sides or with one side and an angle, and the same two posters can ask how high the big one is, or how much ink it needs.
- For **Pythagorean theorem**: multiply each side you know by itself, add the two results to find the longest side (or take one away from the other if you were given the longest side), then find the number that multiplies by itself to make the result.
- For **Trigonometry**: name the sides from the angle, pick the button that joins the side you know and the side you want, and read its value on a calculator set to degrees. Multiply when the side you want is on top of the button’s sum, and divide when it is underneath.
- For **Similar shapes**: use a length measured on both to find how many times longer the bigger one is. Multiply the length you know by that number if it is on the smaller one, and divide by it if it is on the bigger one.
- For **Square-cube law**: find how many times longer the bigger one is, then multiply two of that number together for an area, or three of it for a volume. Then multiply the known amount by the result.
- If a problem shows a *right triangle* and also a second thing of the same shape, such as a shadow, look at the length you want. A side of that triangle is **Pythagorean theorem**, and a length on the second thing is **Similar shapes**.
