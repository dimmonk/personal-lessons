# US Civics & History: Rights and responsibilities

Revision 1, live. Part: Rights and responsibilities. Where the subject ends up: Answer the citizenship interview’s civics questions aloud from memory, and, reading a news story about a US law, order or ruling, say who acted, whether it was theirs to do, who can stop it, and whether to take the story as it stands or check who is behind it first.

## The why

Some rights belong to everyone living here; a few belong to citizens only. Becoming a citizen adds rights and promises, and the interview has questions on both.
The vote grew in four steps. Keep them in the order they came, and each one is easier to remember.

## Step 1: Everyone, or citizens only

| A | B |
|---|---|
| Rights the list gives to everyone living in the United States: freedom of expression, freedom of speech, freedom of assembly, freedom to petition the government, freedom of religion, and the right to bear arms. | For citizens only: vote in federal elections, run for federal office, and serve on a jury. |

Compare: Speech, religion and the rest are yours now. Voting for president or Congress and sitting on a jury wait until you are a citizen.

## Step 2: The four voting amendments, in order

A plain table, "Who can vote, step by step":

> When  |  What it says  (the column heads)
> 1870, after the Civil War  |  A male citizen of any race can vote.
> 1920  |  Any citizen can vote: women and men can vote.
> 1964  |  You don’t have to pay a poll tax to vote.
> 1971  |  Citizens eighteen and older can vote.

At the interview, describe any one of the four. Each one let more people vote than before.

## Step 3: Promises and duties

A plain table, "What a citizen promises and does":

> Question  |  Answers the list accepts  (the column heads)
> Promises in the Oath of Allegiance  |  Give up loyalty to other countries; defend the Constitution; obey the laws of the United States; serve in the military if needed; serve the nation if needed; be loyal to the United States
> The Pledge of Allegiance shows loyalty to  |  The United States; the flag
> How people become citizens  |  Be born in the United States, as the 14th Amendment says; naturalize; derive citizenship
> Two examples of civic participation  |  Vote; run for office; join a political party; help with a campaign; join a civic group; join a community group; give an elected official your opinion; contact elected officials; support or oppose an issue or policy; write to a newspaper
> One way to serve your country  |  Vote; pay taxes; obey the law; serve in the military; run for office; work for local, state, or federal government
> Why pay federal taxes  |  Required by law; all people pay to fund the federal government; civic duty
> Why men 18 through 25 register for the Selective Service  |  Required by law; civic duty; makes the draft fair, if needed
