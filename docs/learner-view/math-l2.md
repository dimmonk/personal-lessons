# Basic Math: Friendly numbers for times and divide

Revision 1, live. Part: Friendly numbers for times and divide. Where the subject ends up: Work out the number a situation needs (a total, a price per one, a scaled amount, a percent or the price before one, how much paint or soil to buy, how high a ladder reaches, what savings grow to or a loan costs) and tell whether it is sensible, when a receipt, a recipe, a sale tag, a room or an offer hands you some numbers and a question and nobody names the method.

## The why

Rounding each price works for a receipt. For 47 tickets at $66, or a $1,284 cabin split six ways, you need numbers your head can multiply and divide.
Here you learn the three moves that people who are good at rough answers make.

## Step 1: Three moves for a rough answer

Make the numbers friendly: change them to numbers that are easy in your head. 47 × 66 is about 50 × 70. For a share, pick a number near the bill that the group divides evenly: $1,284 among 6 is near $1,200, and $1,200 ÷ 6 = $200.
Make the sum simpler: 83 + 74 + 96 + 82 is about four 80s, so about 320.
Then nudge: 50 × 70 = 3,500, but both numbers went up to get there, so the real answer is a bit less: say 3,200. If one number went up and the other down, they roughly cancel: no nudge. $1,284 is a bit more than $1,200, so the share is a bit more than $200.
For a part of something, use the easy fractions: half, a quarter, three quarters, a third, a tenth. 23 hours out of 45 is about half; half of $720 is $360, and 23 is a bit more than half of 45, so a bit more.
There is no one right rough answer. Anything within about a fifth of the exact one is close enough to catch a mistake.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “To estimate, I do the whole sum in my head, digit by digit.”
The right line: That is slow and easy to get wrong. Change the numbers to friendly ones first; the answer only has to be close.

## Step 2: A worked example

A tag, "Ballgame group tickets":

> Each ticket  |  $66
> Your group  |  47 people
> Doors open  |  6:30 p.m.

The working, one step at a time:
1. Say what is asked and what is given. Asked: what 47 tickets cost. Given: 47 people at $66 each. The time the doors open does not matter.
1. Make the numbers friendly. 47 is close to 50, and $66 is close to $70.
1. Make a rough answer in your head. 50 × 70: 5 × 7 = 35, and the two zeros make 3,500. Both numbers went up to get there, so the real answer is a bit less. Say $3,200.
1. Work it out exactly on the calculator, then look back. On the calculator: 47 × 66 = $3,102. Look back: near the rough $3,200.

Then the answer: $3,200 / $3,102. 47 × $66 is about 50 × $70 = $3,500, and nudged, about $3,200; exactly, it is $3,102.
