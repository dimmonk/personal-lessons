# Psychology: Look-alikes and false friends

Revision 4, draft (the screen carries the draft line). Part: Look-alikes and false friends. Where the subject ends up: After this subject you can hear a story about someone, or catch one in your own head, and say what is going on, whether it is one moment or a lasting pattern, the name that fits or that there is too little to tell, and what to do about it, when someone reaches for "narcissist", "gaslighting" or "toxic".

## The why

A friend says her ex "is such a narcissist" because he panicked every time she left the house. Panic at being left is a different pattern, with a different fix.
In about 20 minutes you will learn to tell five look-alike patterns apart by what drives each one, and what to do about each.

## Step 1: What drives it

The five questions: 1. What is going on? 2. One moment, or a lasting pattern? 3. What does it do for them? 4. What is the nearest look-alike? 5. What would prove the read wrong?
For Question 2, check first for a hard time (grief, stress, illness). "Once or twice": the story says it is rare. "The story does not say": one scene, nothing about other times. A pattern here needs years and many people.
Questions 3 and 4 do the rest: clinicians tell these patterns apart by what drives each one, and what the person does when that is threatened.
Ordinary: the facts show a normal reason (stress, a fair point, plain self-interest). Too little to tell: the facts are too few to see what is going on, so you find out more.

## Step 2: Four drives

Borderline: fear of being left. When someone pulls away, they cling and beg, then turn on that person in fury (the same person all good, then all bad), then cling again. Their sense of self keeps shifting.
Histrionic: a need to be noticed. When attention moves elsewhere, they win it back with a bigger story or bigger feelings. Warm, easily swayed, happy with any audience.
Narcissistic: a need to be admired as better. When criticized, or when praise goes to someone else, they run down whoever has it. Grandiose does it out loud, with contempt or rage. Vulnerable does it quietly: sure they are special and unseen, with cold resentment and withdrawal.
Antisocial: indifference to others. No remorse when caught: routine lies, rules broken on impulse, people used. They do not care what you think of them, only what you can be used for. Psychopathy is the extreme, with almost no empathy; "sociopath" is slang for it.

| A | B |
|---|---|
| Her boyfriend goes on a trip. She calls him ten times a night and says she cannot live without him. | Her boyfriend goes on a trip. She spends the week at parties, telling dramatic stories of how she misses him. |

Compare: The first needs that one person to stay: borderline. The second needs an audience, any audience: histrionic.

## Step 3: Unpleasant is not a diagnosis

The wrong idea: the more someone bothers you, the more disordered they must be. Selfish or rude with a plain reason is ordinary. Shy or easily hurt is no name either: vulnerable narcissism needs the sense of being special and owed.
"Toxic", "narc", "sociopath" and "main character energy" are insults: they name a feeling, not signs. Signs against a name count too (Question 5, what would prove it wrong): warmth, gladness at others' wins, remorse.
Only a clinician diagnoses; these names are guesses to test.

## Step 4: Signs, and what to do

Borderline pattern: for years and with every partner, terror of being left, swings from clinging to fury, self-image that will not hold still.
Histrionic pattern: for years, a need to be noticed, theatrical and warm, happy with any attention, escalating when attention moves elsewhere.
Grandiose narcissism: for years, loud superiority, a need to be admired, little empathy, and contempt or rage at anyone who threatens the image.
Vulnerable narcissism: for years, quiet certainty of being special and unappreciated, cold resentment toward anyone doing better, withdrawal instead of attack.
Antisocial pattern: for years, routine lies and use of people, rules ignored, no remorse, and no care what anyone thinks of them.
Grandiose, vulnerable, histrionic: keep firm limits; do not expect to talk them out of it. Borderline: calm, firm limits, and encourage a therapist; it often improves with treatment. Antisocial: protect yourself; keep money and secrets out of reach. Ordinary: nothing to fix. Too little: find out more.
If anyone's safety is at risk, such as a threat of self-harm, get outside help first, whatever the name.

## Step 5: A worked example

After two dates he stopped answering. She texted eleven times in an hour, wrote "I always ruin everything, please tell me what I did", then sent one furious message calling him evil. She has done the same with her last three boyfriends and her best friend since college, and says she never knows who she is without someone.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**Question 2. One moment, or a lasting pattern?**
- A reaction to a hard time: grief, stress, illness
- Once or twice, with no pattern
- Over and over, for months or years
- The story does not say

The working, one step at a time:
1. Question 2. One moment, or a lasting pattern? No hard time explains it, and it is not one painful loss: three boyfriends and a best friend, since college. Over and over, for years, with many people.
1. Questions 3 to 5. What drives it, what is the nearest look-alike, and what would prove it wrong? Every message is about being left: begging, then fury when no answer comes, and no steady sense of who she is. She is not asking to be admired, so not narcissistic, and not playing to an audience, so not histrionic. Fear of being left, the swing, the shifting self: borderline. It would be wrong if this were the first time, after one loss.

Then the answer: Over and over, for months or years / Borderline pattern. The drive is fear of being left, shown as begging and then fury, with every close person, and a sense of self that will not hold.
