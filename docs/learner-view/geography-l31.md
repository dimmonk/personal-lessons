# Geography: How far

Revision 1, live. Part: How far. Where the subject ends up: After this subject you can put a named place on a map with no names, say what it borders and what water it is on, name a place that is lit, tell a country by its flag, and say roughly how far it is from you, when a place is named in the news, by a person or in a travel plan with no map in sight.

## The why

A friend says a city is "only a few hours away", or a story says a town is far from the capital. Without a number, "near" and "far" tell you nothing. In about sixteen minutes you will judge how far apart 26 pairs of cities are, in a straight line and as a trip, by measuring each pair against pairs you know.

## Step 1: How to judge how far

Two questions for every pair. First, how far apart are they in a straight line? That is the number a map gives and a story leaves out. Second, how would you get from one to the other, and how long would it take? That is what changes a plan: a short drive is an errand, a flight over an ocean is a day. Both are answered in bands, not exact miles, because a band is what you can hold in your head and check. The six bands: under 150 miles; 150 to 500 miles; 500 to 1,500 miles; 1,500 to 3,000 miles; 3,000 to 6,000 miles; over 6,000 miles. For the time, use a rule of thumb, not a timetable: a drive is about 60 miles an hour, and a flight is about 500 miles an hour plus an hour on the ground and in the climb. A map misleads the eye, so never judge a pair by how far apart the dots look: measure it against pairs you know. After every answer the feedback gives the straight-line miles, worked out by the app from the two cities' latitude and longitude, with the working for the trip.

## Step 2: A worked example

How far is San Francisco from Los Angeles?

A map of The United States, north up, with no names on it, opening on one part of it (zoom 2.2, round the point 140 across and 268 down, of 1000 by 523.1), where the button for the whole map goes back to that part. Lit: San Francisco, Los Angeles. The learner can pinch to zoom, drag to move it and use the buttons to zoom in, zoom out and show the whole map.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**About how far apart are they, in a straight line?**
- Under 150 miles
- 150 to 500 miles
- 500 to 1,500 miles
- 1,500 to 3,000 miles
- 3,000 to 6,000 miles
- Over 6,000 miles

The working, one step at a time:
1. Find both cities on the map. San Francisco and Los Angeles are both lit and named. Look at the pair, not at each city alone.
1. Compare the pair with pairs you know. Pairs to measure against, in straight-line miles: Austin to San Antonio about 70; Washington, D.C. to New York City about 200; Dallas–Fort Worth to Chicago about 800; Los Angeles to Chicago about 1,740; New York City to Paris about 3,630; Los Angeles to Sydney about 7,500. San Francisco to Los Angeles is farther apart than Austin to San Antonio and than Washington, D.C. to New York City, and much nearer than Dallas–Fort Worth to Chicago.
1. Pick the distance band. The straight line is about 350 miles: 150 to 500 miles.
1. Turn it into a trip. By road: 350 miles at 60 miles an hour is about 5.8 hours. By air: 1 hour on the ground and in the climb, plus 350 miles at 500 miles an hour, is about 1.7 hours, a short flight. So: a drive of 3 to 9 hours, or a short flight.

Then the answer: 150 to 500 miles / A drive of 3 to 9 hours, or a short flight. San Francisco to Los Angeles is about 350 miles in a straight line, which is the band 150 to 500 miles. By road: 350 miles at 60 miles an hour is about 5.8 hours. By air: 1 hour on the ground and in the climb, plus 350 miles at 500 miles an hour, is about 1.7 hours, a short flight.
