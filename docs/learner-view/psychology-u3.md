## Part 1 of 2: Four things people do, and a normal argument

### 1. Before you say someone is messing with you, check what they actually did

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

Before you say it is **Gaslighting**, **Turning the blame around** or **Love-bombing**, check what was actually said and done. Four things people do to each other really are harmful. Most arguments are none of them, and each of the four needs things you can find in the story.

You have heard all of these. “That never happened.” “You’re imagining things.” “Why would you accuse me of that?” “You’re the one who’s always late.” “I’ve never felt like this about anyone.” Each one can be the sound of something done to a person. Each can also be what anyone says in a normal argument.

A sentence on its own can’t tell you which. Neither can how upset anyone is. What can: what really happened, how often it comes back, and what each person did next.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“What are you looking at?”** Its answers:

- **“A choice and its reasons”**: give this answer when one person explains a choice, a view or an act of their own, and nobody else is the target.
- **“Something done to someone”**: give this answer when one person says or does something to another, about that person or about what happened between them. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“A lifelong pattern”**: give this answer when the same behavior shows up for years, in different places and with different people.
- **“Just a one-off”**: give this answer when one person acts a certain way on one occasion or for a short stretch, often after something happened to them, and there is nothing more to it.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

*(One tap on any of these lines opens the card in Unit One that taught it.)*

What are they doing to the other person?
- Keeps telling them it did not happen → Gaslighting
- Denies it, attacks them, and plays the victim → Turning the blame around
- Showers them with attention, then pulls it away → Love-bombing
- Accuses them of what the accuser is doing → Projection
- Nothing more than it looks like → A normal back-and-forth

- Gaslighting: telling someone again and again that what happened did not happen
- Turning the blame around: caught out, they deny it, attack, and play the victim
- Love-bombing: a flood of attention early on, pulled away later
- Projection: accusing someone of what you are doing yourself
- A normal back-and-forth: a disagreement, a complaint or praise, with nothing more to it

### 2. Gaslighting

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-gaslight`]

First: being told, again and again, that something that really happened did not.

*The car repair*

> In February Tess paid $600 to fix the car she shares with her partner Jonas, because he had texted her: 'I'll pay my half on Friday.' He never did. Since then, ⟦whenever she mentions it, he says, 'I never said I'd pay half. You must have dreamed that up.' In March it was, 'You always get things muddled.' In May it was, 'We've been over this. Nothing like that was ever said.'⟧ Tess now rereads her old messages before she raises anything he has agreed to, and has asked her sister, 'Am I remembering this wrong?'

Tess has proof: Jonas’s text. He still told her, month after month, that it never happened, until she began to trust his version over her own.

Two people who remember something differently can usually settle it by checking: a message, a receipt, someone who was there. One denial can be an honest mix-up. Months of them, about something you can check, with Tess asking her sister “Am I remembering this wrong?”, is not. It does not matter whether Jonas sounds kind or angry.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check that it really happened: Jonas’s text says he will pay half.** If nothing shows it happened, you have a disagreement, not this.
2. **Count how often the denial comes back: February, March and May.** One denial can be an honest mix-up; weeks or months of them is not.
3. **Look at what it did to her: she rereads her messages and asks her sister if she is wrong.** When the other person starts to doubt their own memory, the denial is working.

This is **Gaslighting**. It needs all three steps, not just an argument about what happened.

You may also hear this called “making someone doubt their own mind”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Gaslighting**.

### 4. Turning the blame around

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-darvo`]

Next: someone is asked about something they did, and all of it happens in one conversation.

*The missing register money*

> Marek runs the bar. His manager, Joy, tells him the register was $120 short on his shift, and that ⟦the camera shows him taking two bills from it. 'That's not true,' Marek says. 'You were forty minutes late on Tuesday and nobody said a word to you. I come in on my day off, and this is how I'm treated? I'm the one being picked on here.'⟧

When someone raises something you did, the honest answers stay on it: you say sorry, you explain, or, if you did not do it, you say so. Marek does none of these. He denies it, though the camera shows he did it. Then he attacks Joy, so now the talk is about her lateness. Then he says he is the one being picked on.

By the end, what Joy raised is gone and she is the one on the defensive. One conversation is enough: unlike **Gaslighting**, it does not have to come back.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check he really did it: the camera shows him taking two bills.** Someone wrongly accused may deny and get angry too, so those alone prove nothing.
2. **Find the denial: “That’s not true.”** It is the first of three things, all in reply to being asked.
3. **Find the attack on the person who raised it: “You were forty minutes late on Tuesday.”** The talk turns from what he did to what she did.
4. **Find him playing the one wronged: “I’m the one being picked on here.”** Now he is the victim and she is the problem.

This is **Turning the blame around**: the blame starts with Marek and ends up on Joy. It needs all three, and the story must show he did it.

You may also hear this called “DARVO, short for deny, attack, reverse victim and offender”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Turning the blame around**.

### 6. When a story shows both

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 6 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `exception`, id `exc-memory`]

These two are easy to mix up, because in both the person denies that something happened. In real life the same person can deny, attack and play the one wronged, month after month. Here is one.

*The unpaid invoices*

> Hana runs a design studio with her partner Kit. In spring Kit signed off three invoices to a client and agreed to chase them, and the signed sheet is in the office. Every month since, when Hana raises the unpaid invoices, Kit does the same three things in one go. He says, 'I never signed those.' He says, 'You're always looking for someone to blame.' He says, 'After all the hours I've put in, I'm the one who gets treated like a thief.' By September Hana photographs every document she is given, and last week she asked her accountant, 'Do I have the contract wrong? Am I making this up?'

Look at what Kit does when Hana raises the unpaid invoices: he denies it, he attacks her (“you are always looking for someone to blame”), and he says he is the one who gets treated like a thief. That is all three parts of **Turning the blame around**. Yet this story is **Gaslighting**.

**You are asked:** This looks like **Turning the blame around**. Before you read why it is **Gaslighting**, tap the words in the story that settle it.

The pieces you can tap:
1. “Kit signed off three invoices to a client and agreed to chase them, and the signed sheet is in the office.”
2. “Every month since, when Hana raises the unpaid invoices, Kit does the same three things in one go.”
3. “He says, 'I never signed those.' He says, 'You're always looking for someone to blame.' He says, 'After all the hours I've put in, I'm the one who gets treated like a thief.'”
4. “By September Hana photographs every document she is given, and last week she asked her accountant, 'Do I have the contract wrong? Am I making this up?'”

**Shown as soon as you tap.** The words are “Every month since, when Hana raises the unpaid invoices, Kit does the same three things in one go.”.
- If you tapped “Kit signed off three invoices to a client and agreed to chase them, and the signed sheet is in the office.”: That is what really happened. Both names need it, so it cannot settle which this is.
- If you tapped “He says, 'I never signed those.' He says, 'You're always looking for someone to blame.' He says, 'After all the hours I've put in, I'm the one who gets treated like a thief.'”: That is a denial, an attack and Kit playing the one wronged: all of **Turning the blame around**, which is why this looks like it. But it does not settle which name this is.
- If you tapped “By September Hana photographs every document she is given, and last week she asked her accountant, 'Do I have the contract wrong? Am I making this up?'”: That is what it did to Hana. The words asked for show how often it happens.

**Why this is Gaslighting and not Turning the blame around**

Ask how often. Kit does this every month, about something the signed sheet shows really happened, and Hana has started to doubt herself: she photographs every document and has asked her accountant, “Am I making this up?” That is **Gaslighting**.

When the denial comes back for weeks or months until the other person doubts their memory, the deny, attack and play-the-victim are just how Kit says it each time. They do not make a second thing.

**How to tell them apart**

Is it one conversation, with a denial, an attack and the speaker playing the one wronged? Or does the same denial keep coming back for weeks or months, until the other person doubts their own memory?

When a story shows both **“Denies it, attacks them, and plays the victim”** and the same denial of what happened, again and again over weeks or months, until the other person doubts their own memory, the answer is **“Keeps telling them it did not happen”**.

Real life overlaps, and experts do not all draw this line in the same place. Here each story gets one name, so that two people asking the same questions reach the same answer. When a story shows both, take the one that lasts longer.


### 7. Projection

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-projection`]

Third: an accusation that fits the accuser better than the person accused.

*The expense claims*

> Omar's first expense claim is for exactly the amounts on his receipts. ⟦Dana, who the finance records show has padded her own claims for months, tells the finance manager: 'I wouldn't trust that man. People like him always inflate their claims.'⟧

Some accusations are fair: the person really did it, and the accuser says so. Dana’s is the other way round. The finance records show that she is the one padding her claims, and Omar’s claim matches his receipts to the dollar.

People can do this without a plan. Seeing your own fault in someone else can be easier than seeing it in yourself. Dana may not know where her words come from, but Omar has still been called a cheat by the person who is cheating.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the accusation: “People like him always inflate their claims.”** One person is saying another does something wrong.
2. **Check who the story shows doing it: Dana, for months.** Trust the records, not the accuser’s word.
3. **Check the person accused: Omar’s claim matches his receipts.** If nothing shows Omar doing it, the accusation has nothing behind it.
4. **Check that nobody raised anything with Dana first.** If someone had, her answer might be **Turning the blame around** instead.

This is **Projection**: throwing your own fault onto someone else.

You may also hear this called “accusing others of what you do yourself”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Projection**.

### 9. When a story shows both

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 9 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `exception`, id `exc-own`]

These two are easy to mix up, because in both the person who attacks is guilty of what they say. Sometimes an answer to being asked also accuses the other person of what the speaker did. Here is one.

*The car-share fund*

> Beth keeps the sheet for a car-share fund that she and Gareth pay into. She asks Gareth why nothing has gone in from him since March. Look at Gareth’s answer: he says Beth never pays into anything, but the sheet shows he is the one who has not paid, and that Beth has paid every month. An accusation that fits the accuser and not the person accused is **Projection**. Yet this story is **Turning the blame around**.

1. “Beth keeps the sheet for a car-share fund that she and Gareth pay into.”
2. “She asks Gareth why nothing has gone in from him since March.”
3. “The sheet shows no payment from Gareth since March, and a payment from Beth every month.”
4. “'I paid,' Gareth says. 'You're the one who never pays into anything. You take advantage of everyone. I can't believe I'm being accused by you, after everything I've done for this car.'”

**Shown as soon as you tap.** The words are “She asks Gareth why nothing has gone in from him since March.”.
- If you tapped “Beth keeps the sheet for a car-share fund that she and Gareth pay into.”: That sets the scene. It shows nothing said or done to anyone.
- If you tapped “The sheet shows no payment from Gareth since March, and a payment from Beth every month.”: That shows Gareth doing what he accuses Beth of, which is why this looks like **Projection**. - If you tapped “'I paid,' Gareth says. 'You're the one who never pays into anything. You take advantage of everyone. But it only counts as an answer if someone raised it with him first.

**Why this is Turning the blame around and not Projection**

Look at where the story starts. Beth asks Gareth about the fund, and Gareth answers with a denial, an attack, and “I’m being accused”. That is all three parts, said in answer.

The accusation inside his attack does fit his own fault, but it is part of his answer. He did not start it: it comes after Beth raised the fund.

Did someone raise something with the speaker first, so that the speaker is answering? Or did the speaker start with the accusation?

Real life overlaps, and experts do not all draw this line in the same place. Here each story gets one name, so that two people asking the same questions reach the same answer. When a story shows both, take the one where the person is answering something raised with them.


### 10. Love-bombing

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 10 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-lovebomb`]

Fourth: a rush of attention at the very start of a relationship, taken away weeks later.

*The first month*

> Priya met Callum at a friend's wedding. ⟦By the second date he had called her 'the one'. By the fourth he had bought her a coat, and he was texting her forty times a day.⟧ In the fifth week she said she needed a weekend to herself. ⟦Callum went silent for four days, then wrote: 'I thought you were different from the others who put themselves first.'⟧

Attention is a good thing. Keen friends and partners give praise, gifts and time, and a fast friendship is not a fault. So the first half on its own proves nothing.

What matters is how much attention there is, compared with how long the two have known each other, and what happens to it later. Callum’s lasts only while Priya does what he wants. When she asks for a weekend alone, it stops and comes back as criticism. First she was made to feel very special, then to feel she lost it by saying no, so she works to get it back.

**How to spot it**

1. **Compare the attention with the time: “the one” on the second date, forty texts a day by the fourth.** Warm is fine; far more than the time explains is the warning.
2. **Watch what happens when she says no: Callum goes silent for four days.** The attention was never free: it depended on her going along.
3. **Listen for the criticism: “I thought you were different from the others who put themselves first.”** Praise turning into blame is the second half.
4. **Call it this only if both halves are there.** A flood alone is a keen friend; pulling back alone is a cooling relationship.

This is **Love-bombing**: a lot of attention arriving all at once, then taken away. It can be a friend, a mentor or a boss, not only a partner.

You may also hear this called “running hot and cold”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Love-bombing**.

### 12. A normal back-and-forth

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 12 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-ordexchange`]

Fifth: none of the four. This is the answer you will use most.

*The trash*

> Sam has skipped taking out the trash for the third week in a row. ⟦Priya says, 'I'm fed up with doing it. You said you would. Can we work out a schedule?' Sam says, 'You're right, I forgot. I'll do it tonight, and let's write a schedule.'⟧

Priya is fed up, and says so. Sam owns up and offers a fix. That is all there is. Most of what people say to each other is like this: they complain, disagree, defend themselves, forget things, snap, apologize and say kind things.

Once you know four names, it is tempting to find one in everything. Don’t. **A normal back-and-forth** can be rude, unfair or wrong, and can hurt, and it is still not one of the four. How upset anyone is does not decide it.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find what one person says to the other: Priya complains about the trash.** A complaint, a disagreement, a defense and praise all count.
2. **See how the other answers: Sam says “You’re right, I forgot” and offers to fix it.** Owning up, or disagreeing plainly, is a normal answer.
3. **Check the four one by one: Sam denies nothing, attacks no one, pours on no attention and accuses no one.** If none of the four is there, this is the answer.

This is **A normal back-and-forth**. It is the answer for any story with none of the other four, however rude or unfair it is.

### 18. The question to ask each time

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 18 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-does`]

Here is the question and its five answers in one place.

**The question:** **“What are they doing to the other person?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other four.

- **“Keeps telling them it did not happen”**
  - Give this answer when something really happened, and for weeks or months the person keeps telling the other it did not happen or not that way, until the other doubts their own memory.
  - It leads to **Gaslighting**.
- **“Denies it, attacks them, and plays the victim”**
  - Give this answer when the person did something, the other brings it up, and the person does all three in reply: denies it, attacks the one who brought it up, and plays the victim.
  - It leads to **Turning the blame around**.
- **“Showers them with attention, then pulls it away”**
  - Give this answer when early in a relationship the person gives far more praise, gifts or attention than the relationship so far explains, and later pulls it away or turns critical.
  - It leads to **Love-bombing**.
- **“Accuses them of what the accuser is doing”**
  - Give this answer when the person accuses the other of doing or feeling something, the accuser is the one doing or feeling exactly that, and nothing shows the other person doing it.
  - It leads to **Projection**.
- **“Nothing more than it looks like”**
  - Give this answer when one person disagrees with, complains to or praises the other, and none of the other four is there.
  - It leads to **A normal back-and-forth**.

**Why it matters**

Look at what was done to the other person. How upset anyone is, whether it was meant, and what either person is like do not change the name.

A story can show what was said or done, so that is what the question asks about. The names describe what happened in a story, never what kind of person someone is. “What he said to Tess in February, March and May was **Gaslighting**” can be checked against the story. “He is a gaslighter” can only be argued about.

So two stories can have the same two people, the same upset, even the same words, and get different names. A denial can be **Gaslighting** in one story and the plain truth in another.

**How to answer it**

1. **Find what one person says or does to the other.** The question is about what was done, not how upset anyone is.
2. **Check for Gaslighting: something that really happened, and a denial that keeps coming back for weeks or months.** One denial can be an honest mix-up.
3. **Check for Turning the blame around: the person did it, and in reply they deny it, attack and play the one wronged.** Someone wrongly accused may deny and get angry too.
4. **Check for Love-bombing: far too much attention early on, pulled back later.** One half alone is a keen friend or a cooling relationship.
5. **Check for Projection: the accuser does it, and nothing shows the other person doing it.** A fair accusation is **A normal back-and-forth**, even if the accuser does it too.
6. **None of the four? It is A normal back-and-forth.** Most stories are.
7. **Find the exact words that show your answer.** If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Some stories seem to fit two answers. Each pair below has one question that tells them apart.

- Gaslighting or Turning the blame around: Is it one conversation, with a denial, an attack and the speaker playing the one wronged? Or does the same denial keep coming back for weeks or months, until the other person doubts their own memory?
- Turning the blame around or Projection: Did someone raise something with the speaker first, so that the speaker is answering? Or did the speaker start with the accusation?
- Gaslighting or A normal back-and-forth: Does the story show that the thing really happened? Does the same denial come back for weeks or months? Does the other person now doubt their own memory? Or is it one disagreement that gets settled?
- Turning the blame around or A normal back-and-forth: Does the story show that the person did what was raised with them? And is the answer all three: a denial, an attack on the one who raised it, and playing the one wronged?
- Love-bombing or A normal back-and-forth: Is the attention far more than the time together would explain? Then what happens to it later, when the other person says no or goes their own way?
- Projection or A normal back-and-forth: Who does the story show doing what is being said: the person accused, the accuser, or both?

### 21. What to carry away

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 21 of 21*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap`]

You can now go from the first question to the name on your own.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What are they doing to the other person?
- Keeps telling them it did not happen → Gaslighting
- Denies it, attacks them, and plays the victim → Turning the blame around
- Showers them with attention, then pulls it away → Love-bombing
- Accuses them of what the accuser is doing → Projection
- Nothing more than it looks like → A normal back-and-forth

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Gaslighting**: something that really happened, one person telling the other for weeks or months that it did not happen or not that way, and the other doubting their own memory.
- **Turning the blame around**: something the person really did, someone bringing it up, and all three in reply: they deny it, attack the one who brought it up, and play the victim.
- **Love-bombing**: early in a relationship, far more praise, gifts or attention than the relationship so far explains, later pulled away or turned into criticism.
- **Projection**: one person accusing the other of something, the accuser doing or feeling exactly that themselves, and nothing showing the other person doing it.
- **A normal back-and-forth**: two people, one disagreeing with, complaining to or praising the other, and none of the other four.

**To carry away**

- Say what was done to the other person, and find the words in the story that show it. If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.
- Most stories are **A normal back-and-forth**. Start there, and give another name only when everything it needs is in the story. How upset anyone is does not decide it, and neither does how kind or unkind the words sound.
- Four names need more than the words. **Gaslighting** needs something real, a denial that keeps coming back for weeks or months, and the other person doubting their memory. **Love-bombing** needs the flood and the pulling back. **Turning the blame around** needs one conversation with all three parts, and the story showing the person did it. **Projection** needs the accuser to be doing it, and nothing showing the other person doing it.
- The names describe what was done, never what kind of person did it or what they meant. The same person can do one of these on Monday and have **A normal back-and-forth** on Tuesday.
- These questions sort a short account of what was said or done. They cannot tell you whether you are safe. If you think someone is controlling you, or you are afraid of them, talk to someone you trust or to a professional.
