# Basic Math: Areas and how much to buy

Revision 1, live. Part: Areas and how much to buy. Where the subject ends up: Work out the number a situation needs (a total, a price per one, a scaled amount, a percent or the price before one, how much paint or soil to buy, how high a ladder reaches, what savings grow to or a loan costs) and tell whether it is sensible, when a receipt, a recipe, a sale tag, a room or an offer hands you some numbers and a question and nobody names the method.

## The why

Paint, flooring, carpet and edging are sold by what they cover. Buy too little and the job stops halfway; mix up feet and yards and the quote is three times off.
Here you draw the space, find its area or the distance around it, and work out how much to buy.

## Step 1: Area, then how much to buy

Area is how much surface: length × width, in square feet when the sides are in feet. A wall 12 ft wide and 8 ft high is 96 square feet.
Cut-outs come off: a 3 by 7 ft door is 21 square feet less. An L-shaped floor is two rectangles: find each and add them. The four walls of a room, laid flat, are one strip as long as the distance around the room.
The distance around is a different thing: add the sides. Edging, a fence or trim goes around, so it needs the distance around, in feet.
Use one unit. When a price is per square yard, change the area into square yards: 1 square yard is 3 ft × 3 ft, which is 9 square feet, so divide square feet by 9.
How much to buy: what you need ÷ what one can, box or roll covers. Two coats of paint cover the area twice, so double it first. Always round up: 2.3 cans means 3 cans.

A plan drawn to one scale, each side written in ft: 12 ft by 10 ft (Room); 5 ft by 4 ft (Nook).

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “2.3 cans is closer to 2, so buy 2.”
The right line: Rounding to the nearest is for rough answers. When you buy, 2 cans leave part of the wall bare: round up.

## Step 2: A worked example

A plain table, "Room walls":

> Room  |  14 ft by 12 ft, walls 9 ft high
> Door  |  3 ft by 7 ft
> Trim  |  white, already painted
> Paint, one gallon can  |  covers 250 square feet, $38

A plan drawn to one scale, each side written in ft: 14 ft by 12 ft (Room floor).

The working, one step at a time:
1. Say what is asked and what is given. Asked: the walls’ area to paint, then the cans for two coats. Given: the room 14 by 12 ft, walls 9 ft high, the door, and what a can covers. The trim and the price do not matter here.
1. Make a rough answer in your head. Around the room is 14 + 12 + 14 + 12, about 50 ft; times 9 high, less about 20 for the door: about 430 square feet.
1. Name the kind of problem and draw it. Laid flat, the four walls make one long strip: as long as the distance around the room, 14 + 12 + 14 + 12 = 52 ft, and 9 ft high. Take away the door, 3 × 7 = 21.
1. Work it out exactly on the calculator, then look back. On the calculator: 14 + 12 + 14 + 12 = 52, × 9 = 468, − 21 for the door = 447 square feet. Look back: near the rough 430.
1. Work out how many to buy, and round up. Two coats: 447 × 2 ÷ 250 = 3.58, so 4: always round up, or you run short.

Then the answer: 430 square feet / 447.0 square feet / 4. The walls are 52 ft around and 9 ft high, 468 square feet, less 21 for the door: 447. Two coats need 3.58, so 4: always round up, or you run short.
