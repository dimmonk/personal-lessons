# Scams & Social Engineering: What looks prove nothing

Revision 1, live. Part: What looks prove nothing. Where the subject ends up: When a message, call or offer asks you to act, you stop, see what it wants, and check it through a channel you already had before doing anything, while handling real messages normally; and if something already went out, you make the first calls at once.

## The why

Ten things people believe make a message look real or fake. Each one has fooled someone.
You see each belief corrected, then messages where it would mislead you.

## Step 1: How it looks

A wrong idea, marked wrong: Spelling mistakes mean a scam, and a well-written message is real.
The right line: Scams can be written perfectly, and real messages have typos. Look at what it wants.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: It had the bank’s logo and an official name.
The right line: Anyone can copy a logo or make up an official-sounding name.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: The website had the padlock, so it was safe.
The right line: Most copied websites show the padlock too. It proves nothing.

## Step 2: What they knew and how they sounded

A wrong idea, marked wrong: The number on my screen was my bank’s.
The right line: The name and number on your screen can be changed to show anything.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: They knew my name, my address and my grandson’s name, and it sounded just like him.
The right line: Scammers find a lot about you online, and voices can be copied. Knowing you proves nothing.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: I rang the number myself, so it was them.
The right line: If the number came from the pop-up, the text or a web search, you rang whoever put it there.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: He was so calm, friendly and helpful.
The right line: Sounding like a real company is one of the commonest tricks, and being friendly is part of the script.

## Step 3: Who gets caught

A wrong idea, marked wrong: Only greedy or careless people get caught.
The right line: People who lost money to investment scams knew more about money than people who did not. Anyone busy or worried can be caught.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: We have chatted for months and they never wanted money, so they are real.
The right line: The money comes after the trust. Months of chat is how it starts.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: My bank would have stopped it.
The right line: A payment you make yourself goes through. Once it is sent, it is hard to get back.

## Step 4: A worked example

You bank with Calderwood, and its app is on your phone. Your card ends in 4417.

An email: "Calderwood Bank", Email · Tuesday 9:12 AM.

> Subject: **A secure message for you**
>
> Dear Jordan Lee,
> We have placed a hold on your card ending 4417 to protect you.
> Please sign in through the secure link below to lift it.
> Thank you for banking with Calderwood since 2014.
> (the link in it, drawn as words and never one that can be followed: calderwood-secure.example/sign-in)

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**What do you do?**
- Do what it says
- Check first, a way I already had
- Leave it

The working, one step at a time:
1. See what it wants. It wants you to sign in through its link.
1. See whether you started it. No. You did nothing with your card that would need this.
1. See whether you deal with them. Yes: it uses your bank’s name. Its writing, your name and your card’s last digits change nothing.
1. Choose. Check first, in the Calderwood app.

Then the answer: Check first, a way I already had / The Calderwood app, opened myself. Your name, your card’s last digits and perfect writing prove nothing. You did not start it, and it uses your bank’s name, so you check in the app.

## Step 5: Questions

6 questions, listed, with each inner pair asked one after the other, in a random order. A missed question comes back at least three questions later, until it is right. After each answer: the right answer, the deciding words, the reason, and one line on the learner's own choice if it was wrong.

#### Item 1

You bank with Ashgrove, and its app is on your phone. The email has the Ashgrove logo at the top.

An email: "Ashgrove Bank", Email · Monday.

> Subject: **Important: secure message**
>
> Ashgrove Bank · Member Services
> You have one new secure message about your account.
> Sign in at the link below to read it.
> (the link in it, drawn as words and never one that can be followed: ashgrove-messages.example/in)

**What do you do?**
- Do what it says — if chosen: The page kept your password, and your account was emptied.
- Check first, a way I already had (right)
- Leave it — if chosen: You are safe, but it uses your bank’s name, and the app settles it.

**How do you check?** (opens only after a certain answer)
- The link below the logo — if chosen: The link came with the message, so the sender decides where it goes. A copy of a real page looks the same.
- The Ashgrove app, opened myself (right)
- Reply to the email I got and get the message sent — if chosen: You would be asking the sender, who answers its own question.

After the answer: the deciding words are marked. Reason: A logo and an official name can be copied by anyone. You did not start it, and it uses your bank’s name, so you check in the app.

## Step 6: Questions

8 questions, shuffle, each question on a 20 second clock (the clock only shows the time, counted on the frame clock: at zero the question stops, whatever is not answered is a timeout, a miss that is not a slip, and the screen waits for Next; a live call or chat is not timed). A missed question comes back at least three questions later, until it is right. ## The check

8 new questions, no help. No answer is shown until the end. The questions come in mixed order.

You have 20 seconds for each. The clock only shows the time: nothing moves on until you tap Next. A call is not on a clock.

To pass:
- None did what a scam asked
- None checked through the link, number or reply the message gave
- At most 1 wrong among real messages
