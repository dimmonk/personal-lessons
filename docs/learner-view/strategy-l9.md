# Strategy: Growth that keeps the position

Revision 1, live. Part: Growth that keeps the position. Where the subject ends up: After this you can look at a business decision (expand, add a product, chase a big customer, answer a rival, invest) and tell whether it is a strategy, say where the business makes its money and why, and pick the move that keeps it different in a way rivals cannot copy, when you face that decision in your own business.

## The why

Sooner or later a business is offered growth: a big contract, a second shop, a new line. Saying no to more sales feels wrong.
Strategists check first whether the growth serves the core or pulls away from it, because growth that pulls away can cost a business what made it worth growing. Here you learn to tell the two apart.

## Step 1: Does it deepen the core, or blur it?

Start from the core: what the business does that customers come to it for, and that earns it the most. A line that earns well but that customers could get anywhere is not the core.
Then put one question to the offer: does it serve the same kind of customer, with the same activities?
Yes: the same kind of customer in a new place, more of what the core’s customers already want, done by the same people the same way. It deepens the core.
No: it needs different activities (a night shift, a factory line, other hours, other skills) or a different kind of customer. It blurs the position: the business stops being clearly one thing, and the core gets less of everyone’s time.
Count kinds, not heads. A new van or a new hire doing the same work for the same kind of customer still deepens; the same people doing other work for another kind of customer still blurs.
The money in the offer does not decide it. Deepens means the offer is worth pricing and testing, as the next lessons show; blurs means turn it down, or reshape it until it serves the core. Why it matters: once a business is two things, each new offer looks like the last, and the core that earned the money gets crowded out.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “More sales is always good. We can sort out the rest later.”
The right line: Maytag, the appliance maker, grew its sales about five times between 1985 and 1994 by adding product lines and buying other brands. Its profit on each dollar of sales, 8 to 12 cents in the 1970s and 1980s, averaged under 1 cent from 1989 to 1995. Judge growth by what it does to the core, not by the sales it adds.

## Step 2: Two offers to a bakery

| A | B |
|---|---|
| A wedding planner wants Dana to make the cakes for all of its weddings. Same couples, same bakers, same cakes made for each couple. | A supermarket wants 2,000 plain sheet cakes a week, made on a mixer line by a night shift. |

Compare: Both bring more sales. The first brings more of the customers the core serves, done the same way: it deepens. The second needs new activities for a new customer: it blurs.

## Step 3: A worked example

A written note: "Dana’s memo on an offer", Written in April.

> Wedding cakes are what couples come to us for, and they earn the most of anything we make.
> A supermarket chain wants to buy 2,000 plain sheet cakes a week for its stores.
> We would need a night shift, a mixer line for one standard cake, and a delivery van.
> It would double our sales.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**Does this growth keep the position?**
- It deepens the core
- It blurs the position

The working, one step at a time:
1. Name the core: what customers come for, and what earns the most.: Wedding cakes, made for each couple.
1. Does the offer serve the same kind of customer with the same activities?: No: a supermarket buying one standard cake by the thousand, made on a mixer line by a night shift. Different customer, different activities.
1. Leave the money out of it.: Doubling sales does not change what the offer needs. It would pull the bakery toward a cake factory.

Then the answer: It blurs the position. The core is wedding cakes made for each couple. The offer needs a mixer line, a night shift and a van to make one standard cake for a supermarket: different activities for a different customer. It blurs the position, though it would double sales.
