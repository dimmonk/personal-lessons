## Part 1 of 3: The check, a note that was fine, and a note a little off

### 1. Before you fix a note, check it

*Unit Four · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 1 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

When a note sounds wrong, check it against the song’s own note before you try to fix it. Half the time the note was fine, and when it is off, the check tells you which way, so you know what to try.

A friend winces at the chorus. A line you sing in the car every week suddenly sounds off to you. You play back a recording of yourself and the second verse makes you cringe. You want to fix something, but you do not know what.

A note can be off in four ways, and each has its own fix: a little under, a little over, starting under and sliding up, or a voice that hunts for the note. A quick check against the song’s own note tells you which, before you change anything.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“What bothers you about it?”** Its answers:

- **“How the top notes come out”**: give this answer when the trouble starts where the notes go up: at the top of the line the voice shouts, flips, clamps, cannot reach the note, or goes light in a way you are not sure of.
- **“How the air holds up”**: give this answer when the trouble is with the air: it is gone before the line ends, grabbed in the middle of a word, heard leaking out with the sound, or driven out hard, or you suspect the way you breathe.
- **“Whether you are on the note”**: give this answer when what you doubt is the note itself: whether the note you sing is the song’s note, because it sounds a shade off, you slide into it, you hunt for it, or you simply are not sure. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“How the words sound when they come out”**: give this answer when the notes and the air are fine, and what bothers you is the sound: pinched and nasal, dull and muffled, words that run together, or a voice that only sounds strange recorded.
- **“Only that it is not the record’s voice”**: give this answer when the notes are right, nothing is tight, sore or short of air, the words are clear, and the only complaint is that it does not sound like the singer on the record.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. To find out if a note is off, play it and sing against it

*Unit Four · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 2 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-notecheck`]

Everything in this unit starts with one test, so here it is before the first name.

*The piano app*

> Rosa's sister says her part in a song sounded a little low. Rosa opens a piano app on her phone, plays the note the song starts on and holds it, then sings her own note beside it. Hers sounds lower, so she slides her voice up until the two sound like one note. Because she had to slide up, she knows she was under.

Rosa did not go by the feeling. She measured her note against a steady one, and the way she had to slide told her what was wrong.

If you do not have to slide at all, the note was fine, and there is nothing to fix.

**The word for this.** *The note check*: play the song’s note on a piano app, or pause the recording on it, hold it, sing yours, and slide until the two match; which way you had to slide tells you which way you were off.

Use it before you change anything, because a note that sounds wrong to you is often fine.


### 3. On the note

*Unit Four · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 3 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-onnote`]

Start with the one where nothing is wrong, so you know what a match sounds like.

*The funny chorus*

> On the drive home Hector sings along with a song, and the chorus sounds wrong to him. His daughter in the back seat says, 'Dad, that one sounded funny.' At a red light he ⟦pauses the recording on the chorus's first note and holds it, then sings his own note beside it⟧. ⟦The two sound like one note, and he does not have to slide at all⟧.

Hector felt sure he had missed the note, and his daughter said the chorus sounded funny. But when he ran *the note check*, his note and the song’s note matched. The chorus only sounded wrong.

This happens a lot on recordings, and when a listener frowns. Only the check can say whether the note was really off, so trust it over the feeling.

**How to spot it**

1. **Run the check on the line that sounded wrong: Hector paused the recording on the chorus’s first note and held it.** It gives you something steady to compare with.
2. **Sing your note beside it and listen for a slide: Hector’s note sounded the same, so he had nothing to slide.** If nothing needs to move, the note is not off.
3. **Do not go by the reaction: his daughter said it sounded funny.** A frown shows that someone noticed something, not where your note landed.

This is **On the note**. Nothing is wrong. It has a name so that you check before you start fixing a note that was fine.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Leave the note alone.** There is nothing to fix.
2. **Trust the check over the feeling.** The check can be repeated, and the feeling changes from day to day.
3. **Keep recording yourself and checking now and then.** You learn what a match really sounds like.

You may also hear this called “in tune” or “on pitch”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **On the note**.

### 5. Singing flat

*Unit Four · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 5 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-flat`]

Now the first way to be off: a note that sits a little under the song’s note.

*The tired alto*

> Beatriz sings alto in a community choir. After rehearsal the director tells her the end of her part felt a little low. At home that night she ⟦plays the part's last note on a piano app, holds it, and sings hers beside it⟧. ⟦Hers is a little lower, and she has to slide up to meet it⟧. She has been up since five, and the ends of her phrases come out heavy.

Beatriz’s note was close, but low. She was tired and she sang the ends of her phrases heavily, and a tired, heavy voice loses lift, so the note sags a little.

That is the usual cause of a low note: too little lift or energy, from tiredness or from singing heavily or very softly. The fix is more lift, and not more volume.

**How to spot it**

1. **Run the check on the line: Beatriz played the last note of her part on a piano app and sang hers beside it.** You need a steady note to measure against.
2. **See where your note sits and which way you slide: hers sat lower, and she slid up.** A slide up means you were under, and a slide down means you were over.
3. **Look for the usual cause: Beatriz had been up since five, and her phrases ended heavy.** The cause tells you what to change.

This is **Singing flat**: a small miss, a little under the song’s note, and an easy one to fix.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Think the note a touch higher than it is, and brighten the vowel.** A hint of a smile, or an “ee” feeling, lifts the sound.
2. **Give the air a little more energy, not more volume.** Energy lifts a note, and loudness alone does not.
3. **Stand up and lift your chest.** It gives the note more lift.
4. **Run the check again.** It shows whether the change worked.

You may also hear this called “under the note” or “off pitch on the low side”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Singing flat**.

### 8. Singing sharp

*Unit Four · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 8 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-sharp`]

Now the other direction: a note that sits a little over the song’s note.

*The nervous open mic*

> Petra is nervous before her first open mic, and her shoulders are up around her ears. She sings her song, and her friend says it sounded a little off. The next day she ⟦plays the song's first note on a piano app, holds it, and sings hers beside it⟧. ⟦Hers is a little higher, and she has to slide down to meet it⟧.

Petra’s note was close, but high. She was nervous, her shoulders were tight, and tension pushes a note up.

It is the same small miss as the one before, in the other direction. The usual causes are pushing, tension and nerves.

**How to spot it**

1. **Run the check on the line: Petra played the song’s first note on a piano app and sang hers beside it.** It is the same check as before.
2. **See where your note sits and which way you slide: hers sat higher, and she slid down.** A slide down means you were over.
3. **Look for the usual cause: Petra was nervous, and her shoulders were tight.** The cause tells you what to loosen.

This is **Singing sharp**: a small miss, a little over the song’s note, where the one before was a little under.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Drop your shoulders and loosen your jaw.** Tension pushes the note up.
2. **Sing a touch softer, with less air.** Pushing is what lifts the note over.
3. **Run the check again.** It shows whether you moved closer.

You may also hear this called “over the note” or “off pitch on the high side”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Singing sharp**.

## Part 2 of 3: A note that slides in, and a note you hunt for

### 11. Scooping

*Unit Four · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 11 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-scooping`]

The first two were steady notes that sat a little off. This one is a note that moves at the start.

*The swoop*

> Dev loves a slow song, and he sings along with it while he records himself on his phone. On the playback, ⟦every long note starts underneath and slides up into place⟧, the way the singer on the record does it. ⟦He does reach each note, but late⟧.

Dev’s notes are not low. He does get to each one. But every note starts underneath and slides up, so it arrives late and the line swoops.

It is a habit copied from singers on records, and it is easy to pick up without noticing. Dev only heard it on his own recording.

**How to spot it**

1. **Record the line and listen to how each note starts: Dev heard every long note begin underneath.** The start of the note is where this one shows.
2. **Listen for a slide up into the note: Dev’s notes slid up until they reached it.** A steady note that is a little low would not move.
3. **Check whether the note arrives: Dev reached each one, but late.** A note that stays low never gets there, and this one does.

This is **Scooping**. The note is reached, but it arrives late because it starts underneath.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Hear the note in your head before you sing it.** Then you can land on a note you already know.
2. **Start each note exactly where it belongs, or from just above.** Coming from above leaves no slide up from underneath.
3. **Practice the melody on “la” with short, separate notes and no slides.** It trains each note to start where it belongs.
4. **Sound the first note of each phrase on a piano app before you sing it.** Then you start from a note you have just heard.

You may also hear this called “sliding into the note” or “swooping”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Scooping**.

### 14. Guessing the note

*Unit Four · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 14 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-guessing`]

The last one starts before any of this: the note was never in your head.

*The wandering start*

> Mei sings a lullaby to her class of four-year-olds, and she ⟦starts singing before she has the first note in her head⟧. ⟦Her voice wanders up and down until it finds something that sounds right⟧, and by the end of the line it is on a different note than the song's.

Mei did not know the first note when she opened her mouth, so her voice had to look for it. Sometimes it finds the note and sometimes it never does, and the melody wanders.

This is about the start, before any note is sung. It does not mean something is wrong with your ears: nearly everybody sings before they listen.

**How to spot it**

1. **Ask whether you had the note in your head before you sang: Mei did not.** A voice that starts with no note has to hunt for one.
2. **Listen to the start of the line: Mei’s voice moved up and down before it settled.** Hunting sounds like wandering, not like one steady note that is wrong.
3. **Check where the line ends up: Mei finished on a different note than the song’s.** A hunting voice may find the note, or may never find it.

This is **Guessing the note**. The trouble starts before the first note, because no note was in your head.

**What to do when you meet it**

1. **Listen to the line three times, then hum it along with the recording.** The tune gets into your head before it is in your mouth.
2. **Sound the first note of each phrase on a piano app, and hum it before you sing.** Then you start on a note you have just heard.
3. **Learn the melody on “la” before you add the words.** You can listen for the notes alone.
4. **Record yourself and compare it with the song.** You hear for yourself whether you found the note.

You may also hear this called “singing before you hear it” or “not hearing it first”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Guessing the note**.

### 16. When the note ends up low and no note was in her head

### 18. The question to ask about every note

*Unit Four · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 18 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-how`]

Here is the question and its five answers in one place.

**The question:** **“Where does your note land?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other four.

- **“On the song’s note”**
  - Give this answer when the check shows your note on the song’s note, and it only sounded wrong to you.
  - It leads to **On the note**.
- **“A shade under the song’s note”**
  - Give this answer when the check shows your note a little under the song’s note, so you had to slide up to meet it.
  - It leads to **Singing flat**.
- **“A shade over the song’s note”**
  - Give this answer when the check shows your note a little over the song’s note, so you had to slide down to meet it.
  - It leads to **Singing sharp**.
- **“Under it at first, then sliding up into it”**
  - Give this answer when you start under the note and slide up until you reach it, so the note begins with a swoop and arrives late.
  - It leads to **Scooping**.
- **“Wherever your voice happens to start, until you find it”**
  - Give this answer when you start singing with no clear note in your head and move your voice around until it finds the note, or it never does.
  - It leads to **Guessing the note**.

**Why it matters**

Each is fixed differently: a note under the song’s note needs more lift and a brighter sound, a note over it needs less push, a slide needs the note heard before it is sung, hunting needs the melody learned first, and a note that matches needs nothing. Whether a listener liked it does not change the answer; checking your note against the song’s note does.

Run *the note check* on a note that stayed put, and it settles the answer. How the note began settles the rest: a note that started under and slid up into place, or one that started with no note in your head.

**How to answer it**

1. **Ask whether the note was in your head before you sang it.** If it was not, and your voice went looking, it is **Guessing the note**, even when the note also ended up low or high.
2. **Listen to how the note began.** A note that starts under and slides up into place is **Scooping**.
3. **If the note was steady, run *the note check* on it.** Sliding up to meet the song’s note means **Singing flat**, sliding down means **Singing sharp**, and not sliding at all means **On the note**.
4. **Find the exact words that show it: how the note began, or what the check showed.** If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Sometimes two answers both seem to fit. Each pair below has one question that separates it. If a story shows two at once, use the answer named under the pair.

- Singing flat or On the note: Did you have to slide up to reach the song’s note, or did the two already sound the same?
- Singing flat or Singing sharp: Which way did you have to slide to match the song’s note: up or down?
- Scooping or Singing flat: Does the note stay where it is, a little low, or does it start low and move up into place?
- Guessing the note or Singing flat: Was the first note in your head before you opened your mouth, or did your voice go looking for it?
- Guessing the note or Scooping: Before the slide began, did you already hear the note in your head?
- Singing sharp or On the note: Did you have to slide down to reach the song’s note, or did the two already sound the same?
- Guessing the note or Singing sharp: Did you know the note before you sang, or did your voice find its way to it while you sang?

### 21. What to carry away

*Unit Four · rev 1 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 21 of 22*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap`]

You can now tell all five apart on your own.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

Where does your note land?
- On the song’s note → On the note
- A shade under the song’s note → Singing flat
- A shade over the song’s note → Singing sharp
- Under it at first, then sliding up into it → Scooping
- Wherever your voice happens to start, until you find it → Guessing the note

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **On the note**: the song’s note checked against the recording or a piano, and your note matching it.
- **Singing flat**: the song’s note checked against the recording or a piano, your note a little below it, and a slide up to meet it.
- **Singing sharp**: the song’s note checked against the recording or a piano, your note a little above it, and a slide down to meet it.
- **Scooping**: a note started below where it belongs, a slide up until it is reached, and a swoop at the start of the note.
- **Guessing the note**: a start with no clear note in your head, and a voice that moves around until it finds the note, or never does.

**To carry away**

- When a note sounds wrong, run *the note check* before you change anything: play the song’s note, hold it, sing yours beside it. Often the note was fine.
- The slide tells you the way. Up means **Singing flat**, down means **Singing sharp**, and no slide means **On the note**. A listener’s frown and your own feeling do not tell you.
- A note that starts under and slides up is **Scooping**. A note that was never in your head, with a voice going looking for it, is **Guessing the note**, even when it ended low or high.
- Hear the note in your head before it is in your mouth. That one habit helps with every one of these.
