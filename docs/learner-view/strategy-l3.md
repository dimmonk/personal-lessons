# Strategy: Value created, and who keeps it

Revision 1, draft (the screen carries the draft line). Part: Value created, and who keeps it. Where the subject ends up: After this you can look at a business decision (expand, add a product, chase a big customer, answer a rival, invest) and tell whether it is a strategy, say where the business makes its money and why, and pick the move that keeps it different in a way rivals cannot copy, when you face that decision in your own business.

## The why

Every sale creates some value: the gap between what the thing is worth to the customer and what it takes to make it. Price only decides who keeps which part.
Harvard Business School’s strategy course is built on this picture. Here you learn to work out the value of a sale and each side’s share, and to tell a move that makes more value from one that only takes it from someone else, or costs more than it is worth.

## Step 1: Four numbers for one sale

Line up four numbers for one sale, from the top down.
The most a customer would pay: what it is worth to them. The price: what they do pay. The cost: what you pay your suppliers and staff to make it. The least your suppliers and staff would take: below that, they would not sell to you or work for you.
The cost and the least they would take are not the same: a cleaner paid $20 an hour who would have worked for $17 keeps $3 an hour more than she needed.
The value created is the most a customer would pay less the least suppliers and staff would take. It is split three ways. The customer keeps the most they would pay less the price. The business keeps the price less the cost. Suppliers and staff keep the cost less the least they would take.
Why it matters: a business that only fights over the split takes from its customers or its suppliers, and they notice. A business that makes the whole stick longer has more to share, and more to keep.

The value stick, "A birthday cake, one sale": a bar from the least suppliers and staff would take ($10) to the most a customer would pay ($30), cut at the cost ($14) and the price ($24). Value created: $20. Suppliers and staff keep $4; the business keeps $10; the customer keeps $6.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “If I raise my price, I make more value.”
The right line: As long as every customer still buys, a higher price makes you more money only by taking it from what the customer kept. The value is the same; you have moved it to your side. Value grows only when the customer would pay more, by more than it costs to give them that, or suppliers and staff would take less.

## Step 2: A worked example

A plain table, "Dana’s bakery: a birthday cake, one sale":

> For one sale  |  Dollars  (the column heads)
> The most a customer would pay  |  $30
> The price  |  $24
> The cost: what suppliers and staff are paid for it  |  $14
> The least suppliers and staff would take  |  $10

The working, one step at a time:
1. The value created: the most a customer would pay less the least suppliers and staff would take.: $30 − $10 = $20. That is what one cake is worth beyond what it takes to make it.
1. The customer’s share: the most they would pay less the price.: $30 − $24 = $6. The customer gets a cake worth $30 to them for $24.
1. The business’s share: the price less the cost.: $24 − $14 = $10. The other $4 ($14 − $10) is what suppliers and staff keep.

Then the answer: $20 / $6 / $10. One cake creates $20, the $30 a customer would pay less the $10 suppliers and staff would take. The customer keeps $6, Dana $10, and suppliers and staff $4.

## Step 4: Grows the value, or only moves it

To judge a move, see which ends of the stick it moves, and by how much. The customers’ end is the most a customer would pay. The suppliers’ end is the least suppliers and staff would take, and it includes whatever the move adds to the work: more materials, more staff time.
It grows the value when it raises what customers would pay by more than it adds to the work, or when it lowers what suppliers and staff would take.
It shrinks the value when it adds more to the work than customers would pay for it.
If it changes only the price or the cost paid, and both ends stay where they were, it only moves value from one side to another.

| A | B |
|---|---|
| Raise the price of the cake $5. Parents would pay at most what they always would. | Print the child’s photo on the cake. Parents would now pay up to $15 more, and the ink and ten minutes of work add $3. |

Compare: Both can bring more money per cake. Only the second makes the cake worth more to the parent than it adds to the work ($15 against $3), so only it grows the value. The first takes $5 of what the parent kept.
