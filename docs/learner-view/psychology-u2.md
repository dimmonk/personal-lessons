## Part 1 of 3: Excuses, and money already spent

### 1. Are the reasons real?

*Unit Two · rev 6 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 1 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

When someone defends what they did or what they believe, check what their reasoning is actually doing. It is one of five things, and only one of them is working the way reasoning should. That goes for a coworker, a relative, a politician, and you.

You hear these all the time. 'It was only a small one, it doesn't count.' 'We've come too far to stop now.' 'You can't trust that report.' 'I looked into it properly and I was right.' 'I checked, and I was wrong.'

Each could be one of five different things. In four of them, the reasoning bends to protect something the person did, spent or believes. In the fifth, it follows the facts. The sentence alone never tells you which, so this unit shows you what else to look for in the story.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“What are you looking at?”** Its answers:

- **“A choice and its reasons”**: give this answer when one person explains a choice, a view or an act of their own, and nobody else is the target. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“Something done to someone”**: give this answer when one person says or does something to another, about that person or about what happened between them.
- **“A lifelong pattern”**: give this answer when the same behavior shows up for years, in different places and with different people.
- **“Just a one-off”**: give this answer when one person acts a certain way on one occasion or for a short stretch, often after something happened to them, and there is nothing more to it.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. The jolt when what you do does not fit what you believe

*Unit Two · rev 6 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 2 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-cd`]

Start with a feeling you already know. The first of the five things is what people do about it.

*The team dinner*

> Maya has told everyone at work that she is vegan. At a team dinner she learns, halfway through her plate, that the sauce is made with fish stock.

Maya believes something about herself: she is vegan. She has just found out that she is doing something that does not fit it: eating fish stock. For a moment the two sit side by side, and it is uncomfortable. You know the feeling: a small jolt of "this is not like me".

**The word for this.** *Cognitive dissonance*: the discomfort of doing one thing while believing another.

The jolt does not last, because people get rid of it. Two ways are honest. Maya could put down her fork, or she could say, "I am not as strict a vegan as I tell people." Each changes something real.

A third way changes nothing real: make up a reason why it is fine after all. That third way is the first of this unit’s five things.


### 3. Rationalizing

*Unit Two · rev 6 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 3 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-dissonance`]

Back to Maya at the dinner table. Here is the whole evening.

*The fish-stock sauce*

> Maya has told everyone at work that she is vegan. At a team dinner she learned, halfway through her plate, that the sauce was made with fish stock. She finished the plate. On the way home she said to a colleague, '⟦It was only a splash of fish stock. It hardly counts.⟧'

Maya did not stop eating, and she did not take back what she says about herself. Afterward she gave a reason why this plate does not count: "It hardly counts." That is an excuse.

The excuse works. The jolt goes away, and nothing real has changed: she ate the fish stock, and she still calls herself vegan. People reach for it because it costs nothing. Saying one thing and doing another is not enough on its own, and the jolt (*cognitive dissonance*) is only a feeling. What gives it away is the excuse that comes after.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find what they did that does not fit what they say: Maya finished the fish-stock sauce, and she says she is vegan.** Without that, there is nothing to excuse.
2. **Find the reason they gave afterward: "It was only a splash of fish stock. It hardly counts."** The reason comes after the act, and that order gives it away.
3. **Check that nothing real changed: she did not undo it, and she did not say she was wrong.** An excuse makes the jolt smaller and leaves the act and the claim as they were.

This is **Rationalizing**: making up a reason after the act, so that it feels fine.

You may also hear this called “making excuses” or “cognitive dissonance reduction”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Rationalizing**.

### 5. Sunk cost fallacy

*Unit Two · rev 6 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 5 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-sunkcost`]

**Rationalizing** looks back at something already done. The second of the five looks back at something already spent, and uses it as the reason for what to do next.

*The renovation*

> Dan and Aisha have spent two years and $40,000 renovating an old house. A builder tells them that finishing it properly will cost another $30,000, and that the finished house will be worth only about $10,000 more than it is now. '⟦We've put in two years and forty thousand dollars⟧,' Dan says. 'We can't stop now.'

Dan and Aisha cannot get the $40,000 and the two years back, whatever they choose now. Stopping does not lose them a second time, and going on does not bring them back.

So the only thing left to decide is whether another $30,000 is worth what it buys: about $10,000 more on the house. Dan never looks at that. His only reason points backward, at what is already spent.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find what is already spent: two years and $40,000.** It is gone whichever way they choose.
2. **Find the next step still to decide: stop, or spend another $30,000.** A good reason has to be about this step.
3. **Find the reason given for taking it: "We’ve put in two years and forty thousand dollars."** When the reason is what is already spent, it says nothing about the step itself.
4. **Ask what the next step would cost and bring: $30,000 for about $10,000 more.** That is the question the reason skipped.

This is **Sunk cost fallacy**. A "sunk cost" is what you have already spent and cannot get back, and the mistake is letting it decide what you do next. It feels sound because nobody likes waste.

You may also hear this called “throwing good money after bad” or “escalation of commitment”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Sunk cost fallacy**.

### 7. Rationalizing or Sunk cost fallacy: telling them apart

*Unit Two · rev 6 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 7 of 20*

Both look back at something the person already did or spent, so they are easy to mix up. Here they are side by side.

**Story A**

> Rosa paid $80 for a concert ticket. On the night she has a fever and it is snowing. 'I paid eighty dollars for this,' she says, pulling on her coat. 'I'm going.'

**Story B**

> Rosa told her friends for weeks that she would never pay a reseller's price for a concert. Then she paid a reseller $200 for a ticket. 'It's a once-in-a-lifetime show,' she says. 'That makes it different.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about Rosa and a concert ticket. Compare one thing: the reason she gives. Does it say something she did is fine, or does it use money already spent to decide her next step?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Keeps going because of what is already spent”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story A.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the $80 is spent, and Rosa still has to decide whether to go out with a fever. Her reason for going is the $80. That is **Sunk cost fallacy**.

In Story B Rosa did something she said she would never do: she paid a reseller. "It’s a once-in-a-lifetime show" does not decide any next step. It only says the purchase is fine. That is **Rationalizing**.

**How to tell them apart**

Does the reason say that something they did is fine, or does it use what is already spent to decide the next step?

## Part 2 of 3: Evidence, and going where the facts lead

### 8. Confirmation bias

*Unit Two · rev 6 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 8 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-confbias`]

The first two were about a person explaining something already done or spent. The next two are about evidence: what is true, or what to choose.

*The one-way street plan*

> Greg is sure that his town's new one-way street plan has made traffic worse. When a neighbor says her drive to work now takes longer, Greg says, 'Exactly. That proves it.' When the council publishes a count showing that trips are four minutes shorter on average, Greg says, 'Who did the counting? When? I'd want to know how they measured that.' ⟦He asked his neighbor none of those questions⟧.

Greg’s questions are not bad ones: a count can be done badly. But one neighbor’s longer drive is much weaker evidence than a count of many trips, and nobody asked her anything.

That is all it is: a harder test for one side. Evidence for the view walks straight in, and evidence against it has to answer questions first. Someone who keeps doing this can only get more sure, whatever is true.

Notice what Greg is not doing: he is not looking for anything. The neighbor’s remark and the council’s count came to him, and he judged each as it arrived.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the view they already hold: the one-way plan made traffic worse.** Without a view there is nothing to protect.
2. **Find the evidence for it and the evidence against it: the neighbor’s longer drive, and the council’s count.** Both sides have to be in the story.
3. **Compare the questions each side gets: the neighbor gets none, the count gets "Who did the counting? When? How?"** The giveaway is one side questioned harder than the other.
4. **Check that Greg is not running a search: the evidence came to him.** A search with the answer already chosen is a different thing, and it is next.

This is **Confirmation bias**: leaning toward whatever backs up what you already think.

### 10. Motivated reasoning

*Unit Two · rev 6 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 10 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-motivated`]

In **Confirmation bias** nobody sets out to find anything. In the next one the person does: they run a search to settle a choice, and the choice is already made.

*Carol's interviews*

> Carol has to choose a new team leader. ⟦Before the interviews she has already decided⟧ it will be her friend Jas. In each interview she writes down the good points of Jas's answers and the weak points of everyone else's. 'I've been through all four candidates,' she tells her boss, 'and Jas is clearly the strongest.'

Interviews are meant to work one way: you look first, and the answer comes at the end. Carol ran hers backwards. She had the answer first, so all the interviews could do was collect support for it.

Greg also held his view before the evidence came, so having a view first is not the difference. Greg was not looking for anything. Carol set out to look, with the answer already chosen.

A search that cannot change the answer only collects support.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the search: Carol interviews four candidates.** Something is meant to settle a choice or a question.
2. **Find when the answer was picked: before the interviews, she had already decided on Jas.** If the answer comes first, the search cannot change it.
3. **Check what the search collected: the good points of Jas and the weak points of everyone else.** It was never going to turn up anything against the answer.

This is **Motivated reasoning**. "Motivated" because what steers the reasoning is something the person wants, not the evidence.

### 12. Confirmation bias or Motivated reasoning: telling them apart

*Unit Two · rev 6 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 12 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-confbias-motivated`]

This is the hardest pair in the unit. In both, the person goes harder on evidence they do not like, and ends where they started.

**Story A**

> On Friday, Sam decided to hire his cousin's firm to build the extension. On Saturday he 'got quotes': he called two other builders, asked each of them one question, and noted that one sounded rushed and the other was vague about dates. 'I've compared three firms,' he told his wife. 'My cousin's is the best.'

**Story B**

> Sam has believed for years that small local builders do better work than big firms. When a friend's small builder finishes a month late, Sam says every job has delays. When another friend's big firm finishes a month late, Sam says, 'That's big firms for you.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about Sam and builders, and in both he is harder on what goes against him. Compare one thing: is he running a search to settle a choice, and had he picked the answer before it began?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Picks the answer first, then looks for support”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story A.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A Sam is settling a choice: which firm to hire. He decided on his cousin’s firm on Friday and "got quotes" on Saturday, so the search could never change anything. That is **Motivated reasoning**.

In Story B Sam is not choosing anything or looking for anything. He has held a view for years, and evidence comes along. A month late is normal for a small builder, and proof of failure for a big firm. That is **Confirmation bias**.

**How to tell them apart**

Did the person set out to look into something? If so, had they picked the answer before they started?


### 13. Following the facts

*Unit Two · rev 6 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 13 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-fair`]

In the four so far, the facts made no difference. In the fifth, they do. You need it as much as the others: without it, every change of mind looks suspicious and every firm view looks like a fault.

*The night games*

> For years Ben told his soccer club's committee that night games under the lights would bring bigger crowds. The club tried it for a season. Then the attendance figures came in: crowds were smaller at every night game. 'I wanted this to work,' Ben told the committee. '⟦It didn't. I was wrong.⟧'

Ben had every reason to protect his view: it was his idea, and he had pushed for it. Greg, in the same spot, asked who did the counting. Ben read the attendance figures and accepted them. They were bad news for him, and they got the same check that good news would have got.

His view changed because the facts went against it. But the name is not for changing your mind. It is for the fair check, and a fair check can leave a view where it was, or even end on the answer the person hoped for. What separates it from **Motivated reasoning** is the order: the search came first, and it could have gone either way.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the facts: a season of attendance figures.** The view has to meet something real.
2. **Check the facts got the same test good news would have got: Ben did not explain the figures away.** Bad news that gets a harder check than good news is **Confirmation bias**.
3. **See where the view ended up: "It didn’t. I was wrong."** It goes where the facts lead, and that can mean changing it or keeping it.

This is **Following the facts**. It is the only one of the five that is not a fault, so you can say "nothing is wrong here" as exactly as you can name what is.

You may also hear this called “keeping an open mind”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Following the facts**.

### 15. Confirmation bias or Following the facts: telling them apart

*Unit Two · rev 6 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 15 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-confbias-fair`]

These two start the same way: a person with a view meets evidence against it. What happens next is the whole difference.

**Story A**

> Luis thought the new hire, Dana, was not up to the job. At the end of the quarter her sales figures were the best on the team. Luis checked that the figures had been counted the same way for everyone. They had. 'I had her wrong,' he told his manager.

**Story B**

> Luis thought the new hire, Dana, was not up to the job. At the end of the quarter her sales figures were the best on the team. 'One good quarter proves nothing,' Luis said. Two weeks earlier, when Dana missed a single deadline, he had said, 'That tells you all you need to know.'

**What to compare.** The two stories are word for word the same until the figures arrive. Compare one thing: the test Luis puts the figures through, and whether he ever put evidence on his own side through it.

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Picks apart only the evidence against their view”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A Luis checks that the figures were counted the same way for everyone. That is a fair test, the one he would want for any figures, and when they pass, his view goes where they lead. That is **Following the facts**.

In Story B he sets a test too: one quarter proves nothing. But two weeks earlier, one missed deadline was proof enough. How much he needs changes with the side the evidence is on. That is **Confirmation bias**.

So the difference is not whether he questions evidence he dislikes: he does in both. It is whether he asks the same questions of the evidence on his own side.

**How to tell them apart**

Did the evidence against the view get the same questions as the evidence for it?


### 16. A change of view that is not Following the facts

*Unit Two · rev 6 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 16 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `exception`, id `exc-convert`]

A view that changes can look like **Following the facts**: the person used to think one thing and now thinks another. Here is a story where the view changed and the facts had nothing to do with it.

*The convert*

> Until last month Jo said electric cars were overpriced toys. Then, on impulse at a car show, she bought one. Now she tells friends that electric cars are 'obviously the future'. She has read nothing about them that she had not read before.

Jo’s view changed completely, and a changed view is what **Following the facts** often looks like. Yet this is **Rationalizing**.

**You are asked:** This looks like **Following the facts**. Before you read why it is **Rationalizing**, tap the words in the story that settle it.

The pieces you can tap:
1. “Until last month Jo said electric cars were overpriced toys”
2. “on impulse at a car show, she bought one”
3. “electric cars are 'obviously the future'”
4. “She has read nothing about them that she had not read before”

**Shown as soon as you tap.** The words are “She has read nothing about them that she had not read before”.
- If you tapped “Until last month Jo said electric cars were overpriced toys”: That is the old view. It does not tell you what changed it.
- If you tapped “on impulse at a car show, she bought one”: That is what came between the two views. It matters, but on its own it does not rule out new facts arriving as well.
- If you tapped “electric cars are 'obviously the future'”: That is the new view. It does not tell you what changed it.

**Why this is Rationalizing and not Following the facts**

Ask what changed Jo’s view. No new fact arrived. A purchase did: she bought the thing she used to mock, and that does not fit.

The honest way out was "I bought something I think is an overpriced toy." She did not say that. She changed her opinion of electric cars so that the purchase needs no excuse, and the new opinion is the excuse.

So this is **Rationalizing** again. Jo did something that did not match what she said, and then came up with a reason it is fine. Her reason is a whole new opinion, and it does the same job.

**How to tell them apart**

What came between the old view and the new one: a new fact, or only something the person did?

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other four.

- **“Makes excuses for what they already did”**
  - Give this answer when the person did something that goes against what they believe or have said, and afterward finds a reason it is fine, without new facts and without undoing it.
  - It leads to **Rationalizing**.
- **“Keeps going because of what is already spent”**
  - Give this answer when a next step is still to decide, and the reason given for taking it is the money or time already spent, not what the step itself would cost or bring.
  - It leads to **Sunk cost fallacy**.
- **“Picks apart only the evidence against their view”**
  - Give this answer when there is evidence both for and against the person’s view, and they pick apart the evidence against it in a way they never did the evidence for it.
  - It leads to **Confirmation bias**.
- **“Picks the answer first, then looks for support”**
  - Give this answer when the person looks into a question or a choice, the answer was picked before they started, and they only collect what backs it.
  - It leads to **Motivated reasoning**.
- **“Checks every fact the same way, and goes where they lead”**
  - Give this answer when the person checks every fact the same way whichever side it helps, and their view or plan goes where the facts lead, whether it changes or stays the same.
  - It leads to **Following the facts**.

**Why it matters**

Look at what the reasons do. Who gives them, what they are about, and where the person ends up do not change the name.

Two people can reach the same conclusion on the same topic and get different names. For one of them it is **Motivated reasoning**; for the other it is **Following the facts**. The topic, the person and where they end up do not tell them apart. Only what the reasoning did does.

**How to answer it**

1. **Find the words where the person gives their reason, or deals with the evidence.** Everything you need is in that sentence.
2. **Ask what the reasoning is about: something they did or spent, or evidence about what is true.** Something done or spent leads to **Rationalizing**, **Sunk cost fallacy** or **Following the facts**, and evidence leads to **Confirmation bias**, **Motivated reasoning** or **Following the facts**.
3. **Pick the answer that matches those words: the excuse afterward, the money already spent, one side picked apart, the answer chosen first, or every fact checked the same way.** You should be able to say which words show it.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Sometimes two answers both seem to fit. Each pair below has one question that separates it.

- Rationalizing or Sunk cost fallacy: Does the reason say that something they did is fine, or does it use what is already spent to decide the next step?
- Confirmation bias or Motivated reasoning: Did the person set out to look into something? If so, had they picked the answer before they started?
- Rationalizing or Confirmation bias: Is the person explaining something they did, or weighing evidence about what is true?
- Confirmation bias or Following the facts: Did the evidence against the view get the same questions as the evidence for it?
- Sunk cost fallacy or Following the facts: Is the reason for the next step what is already spent, or what the next step would cost and bring?
- Rationalizing or Following the facts: What came between the old view and the new one: a new fact, or only something the person did?
- Motivated reasoning or Following the facts: Could the search have gone the other way, and would the person have gone with it?

What are they doing with their reasons?

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Rationalizing**: something the person did that goes against what they believe or have said, and an excuse they come up with afterward for why it is fine or does not count.
- **Sunk cost fallacy**: money, time or effort already spent that cannot be gotten back, a next step still to decide, and what is already spent given as the reason to take that step.
- **Confirmation bias**: a view the person already holds, evidence for it and against it, and the evidence against it picked apart in a way the evidence for it never was.
- **Motivated reasoning**: a question or a choice the person looks into, an answer they picked before they started, and a search that only collects what backs that answer.
- **Following the facts**: facts each checked the same way whichever side they help, and a view or plan that goes where the facts lead, whether it changes or stays the same.

**To carry away**

- Find the words that show what the reasoning is doing. If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.
- The topic, the person and where they end up never decide it: a view can change without **Following the facts**, and a view can stay put with it.
- One sentence is never enough. "You can’t trust that report" is **Confirmation bias** only if the evidence on the speaker’s own side never got the same questions. "I looked into it properly and I was right" is **Motivated reasoning** only if the answer was picked before the looking. Otherwise it may well be **Following the facts**.
- Try it on yourself first. When you catch yourself saying "it hardly counts" or "we’ve come too far to stop", ask what your reasoning is doing.
