# Basic Math: Proportional or not?

Revision 1, live. Part: Proportional or not?. Where the subject ends up: Work out the number a situation needs (a total, a price per one, a scaled amount, a percent or the price before one, how much paint or soil to buy, how high a ladder reaches, what savings grow to or a loan costs) and tell whether it is sensible, when a receipt, a recipe, a sale tag, a room or an offer hands you some numbers and a question and nobody names the method.

## The why

Twice the miles in a cab does not cost twice as much when there is a charge to start, and two months at a gym do not cost twice one month when you paid to join.
Before you scale, check that the whole thing grows with the amount.

## Step 1: Look for the part paid once

A cab charges $3.50 to start, then $2.50 a mile. 4 miles cost $3.50 + 4 × $2.50 = $13.50. 8 miles is not $27: it is $3.50 + 8 × $2.50 = $23.50. The start is paid once, however far you go.
The rule for every question: look for a part that is paid once, or stays the same, whatever the amount. If there is none, it scales: per one, then times how many. If there is one, scale only the part that grows, then add the fixed part back.
Fixed parts hide in a charge to start, a monthly fee, a joining fee, a charge for the day, or a shipping charge for any size of order.
A price list may not show a fixed part. Then check the earlier cost on it: 4 miles at $2.50 is $10, but your last ride cost $13.50, so $3.50 is paid once.

A table with two rows: Miles: 0, 4, 8 / Fare: $3.50, $13.50, $23.50.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “Twice as much always costs twice as much.”
The right line: Only when nothing is paid once. With a fixed part, twice the amount costs less than twice as much.

## Step 2: A worked example

An offer, "City Cab fares":

> To start the ride  |  $3.50
> Each mile  |  $2.50
> Tips  |  up to you
> Your last ride, 4 miles  |  $13.50

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**Does the cost scale with the miles?**
(the options come in an order that depends on the seed of the question)
- It scales: twice as much, twice the number
- It does not scale: part of it stays the same

The working, one step at a time:
1. Say what is asked and what is given. Asked: the fare for 8 miles. Given: your last ride, 4 miles for $13.50, and the fare card. The other lines do not matter.
1. Look for a part paid once, whatever the amount. The charge to start, $3.50, is paid once whatever the miles, so it does not scale: only the per-mile charge grows.
1. Make a rough answer in your head. About $4.00 once, plus about $2.50 × 8: about $24.00.
1. Name the kind of problem and draw it. Two parts, in a table from 0 up: the charge to start, $3.50, paid once (the cost of 0 miles), and $2.50 for each mile, which grows.
1. Work it out exactly on the calculator, then look back. On the calculator: $2.50 × 8 = $20.00, then + $3.50 = $23.50. Look back: less than double $13.50, because the charge to start is paid once.

Then the answer: It does not scale: part of it stays the same / $24 / $23.50. The charge to start, $3.50, is paid once; only the per-mile charge grows. $2.50 × 8 plus $3.50 is $23.50.
