## Part 1 of 3: Lawmakers voting, and the President or an office

### 1. The news says “the government”. Which one?

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 1 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient-kind`]

When a news story says “the government” changed a rule, find out which government made the final call: lawmakers, the President or an office, a judge, or a state or city. That tells you where to look, who to ask and what can happen next.

“The government has put a new fee on passports.” “The government will not let the building go ahead.” “The government has changed the rules on bringing food into the country.” Each one says “the government”, and none says which one.

There are many: the lawmakers of the whole country, the President and the offices under the President, the judges, your state, your city or county. Each can do different things and has different limits.

Stories also name several at once: a law, the office that applies it, a judge asked to rule on it. If you take the one named first, you can be wrong before you start. So read to the end and find the final call, or the one the story asks for.

Who makes the final call?
- Congress, in the House or the Senate → the lawmakers of the whole country
- The President or a federal agency → the President, and the offices that run the laws of the whole country
- A judge, in any court → a judge deciding a court case
- A state, city or county government → the government of one state, or of a city, town or county in it

The unit has three parts, and you can stop after any of them.

1. Lawmakers voting, and the President or an office
2. A judge, and a state, city or county
3. One whole story, then the drill

Each starts from a real story. After each one you answer a quick question, and the reason is shown right away. Nothing here is graded. A miss only decides what comes back.

### 2. Congress, in the House or the Senate

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 2 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-congress`]

First: lawmakers voting. This is the easiest one to spot, because a vote is something you can see.

*The bicycle-parts tax*

> Bike shops say the tax on imported bicycle parts is too high. ⟦A bill to lower it passed the House of Representatives last month⟧. ⟦On Thursday the Senate voted on the bill too, and it passed⟧.

The bike shops complain, someone writes a bill (a proposed law), and the lawmakers vote on it. Nobody else decides anything, so the final call is theirs. The lawmakers of the whole country are called Congress, and it has two parts: the House of Representatives and the Senate. A bill has to pass both to become a law.

Congress votes on more than laws. It votes on how much money the government can spend, on whether to approve someone the President picked for a top job or a deal the President signed with another country, and on charging an official with wrongdoing. It also counts when the vote has not happened yet: a story that ends with someone asking the Senate to vote is still the Senate’s.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the final decision: the Senate votes on the bill and it passes.** The final call is the one the story ends on, or asks for.
2. **Check who is voting: the House of Representatives and the Senate.** Lawmakers vote. Offices and judges give orders and rulings instead.
3. **Check that nobody else steps in: no office, no judge, no city or state.** If someone else gets the last word, the answer changes.

This is **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**. Congress just means the lawmakers who vote for the whole country.

### 4. Offices that run the laws day to day

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 4 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-agency`]

The next one needs a word, and a story shows what it means before it gets a name.

*Rosa’s application*

> Rosa has applied to become a citizen. Her papers went to the immigration service, an office that checks each application, books the interview and sends a letter with the date. No new law was written for Rosa. The office is applying a law that already exists.

Rosa’s papers did not go to lawmakers, and nobody voted on them. They went to an office whose daily job is applying a law that already exists: checking papers, booking interviews, sending letters.

Offices like this do most of what the government does for you day to day. Others collect taxes, inspect meat plants and write the rules for how strong a seat belt must be. Each one works under a law that lawmakers already passed.

**The word for this.** *Agency*: a government office that runs certain laws day to day, such as the immigration service or the tax office.

Some of these offices belong to the government of the whole country, and some belong to a state, a city or a county. Either way, an office is not the lawmakers and not a judge. It is the part of government that does the daily work of a law.


### 5. The President or a federal agency

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 5 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-president`]

Second: no vote this time. The President, or an *agency*, decides something and announces it.

*The seat belts*

> On Monday ⟦the federal road-safety agency published how strong a seat belt must be in every new car⟧, and the crash test each new car must pass. Its inspectors will start testing new cars next month.

An office decided something and announced it. Nobody voted on the rule and no judge was involved. “Federal” means the office belongs to the government of the whole country, not to a state or a city, so the rule applies everywhere in the country.

That government is not only lawmakers. It also has a President, who leads it, and the offices that run its laws. The President decides things too: orders to the armed forces, deals with other countries, refusing to sign a law Congress passed, forgiving a federal crime. An *agency* does the daily work: writing detailed rules, inspecting, processing applications, collecting.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find who decided: the federal road-safety *agency* announced the rule.** The President or an office acts on its own, with no vote.
2. **Check there was no vote and no judge: there was neither.** A vote would make it the lawmakers’ call, and a ruling would make it a judge’s.
3. **Check whose office it is: “federal” means the whole country’s.** A state has offices too, and they can do the same work, but their calls belong to the state.

This is **“The President or a federal agency”**. Either one counts: the President, or an office of the whole country.

### 7. One law: the vote, then the office that applies it

**Story A**

### 8. A law the President signs

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 3 · Card 8 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `exception`, id `exc-signed`]

A passed law can end on the President’s desk with a signature. That sounds like the President deciding.

*The signed park law*

> In March the House and the Senate both passed a bill that makes the Calder River valley a protected park. On Tuesday the President signed the bill at a ceremony, with the mayors of the valley towns standing behind her.

The story ends with the President’s own act: signing the bill. A decision by the President usually means **“The President or a federal agency”**, yet the answer here is **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**.

**You are asked:** This looks like **“The President or a federal agency”**. Before you read why it is **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**, tap the words in the story that settle it.

The pieces you can tap:
1. “the House and the Senate both passed a bill that makes the Calder River valley a protected park”
2. “On Tuesday the President signed the bill at a ceremony”
3. “the mayors of the valley towns standing behind her”

**Shown as soon as you tap.** The words are “the House and the Senate both passed a bill that makes the Calder River valley a protected park”.
- If you tapped “On Tuesday the President signed the bill at a ceremony”: That is the last thing in the story, and it sounds like a decision. But the bill had already passed, and every word of it was settled by the votes.
- If you tapped “the mayors of the valley towns standing behind her”: The mayors are there to watch. Nothing here says they decide anything.

**Why this is Congress, in the House or the Senate and not The President or a federal agency**

By the time the President signs, every word of the bill was settled by the votes. The signature does not change what the law says, so the decision that the valley becomes a park was made when the House and the Senate voted.

Compare a refusal. If the President refused to sign, that would be the President’s own call: the bill goes back, and does not become a law unless the lawmakers vote for it again.

**How to tell them apart**

Does the story end on a vote, or on something the President or an office decides or does? If a law is in the story, has it already passed?

You will hear it both ways: “Congress passed the park law” and “the President made the valley a park”. For this course a signed law stays with the lawmakers, because the signature never changes the bill.

## Part 2 of 3: A judge, and a state, city or county

### 9. A judge, in any court

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 9 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-courts`]

Third: two sides disagree, and someone else has to settle it.

*The broken heater*

> Hana says her landlord must pay to fix the broken heater. The landlord says that Hana must pay. On Friday ⟦they each told their story to a judge, and the judge decided that the landlord must pay⟧.

Hana and her landlord cannot settle it between them, so a judge hears both sides and decides. A judge does not write laws or run programs. A judge settles a dispute that someone brings.

It does not matter which court it is, or how big the quarrel is. A judge in a federal court and a judge in a state court count the same, and a broken heater counts the same as a famous trial. A story can also end before the ruling, with someone asking a judge to decide. That counts too.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the dispute: Hana says the landlord must pay, and the landlord says Hana must.** Two sides disagree and cannot settle it themselves.
2. **Find the judge: they each told their story to a judge.** A judge decides by hearing both sides.
3. **Find the ruling, or the request for one: the judge decided the landlord must pay.** It counts even when the story ends with someone only asking a judge to decide.

This is **“A judge, in any court”**. “Any court” means any: the highest court in the country, or a state or county court.

### 11. A trial held in the Senate

A story can use courtroom words and still end on a vote by lawmakers. Here is one.

*The department head on trial*

> The head of a federal department is accused of taking money from a company in return for contracts. On Tuesday the House voted to charge him. This week the Senate is holding a trial, and on Friday the senators will vote on whether he is guilty.

This story has a trial, a charge and a man who may be found guilty. Those are courtroom words, and they usually mean a judge. Yet the answer here is **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**.

**You are asked:** This looks like **“A judge, in any court”**. Before you read why it is **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**, tap the words in the story that settle it.

The pieces you can tap:
1. “The head of a federal department is accused of taking money from a company in return for contracts”
2. “On Tuesday the House voted to charge him”
3. “This week the Senate is holding a trial, and on Friday the senators will vote on whether he is guilty”

**Shown as soon as you tap.** The words are “This week the Senate is holding a trial, and on Friday the senators will vote on whether he is guilty”.
- If you tapped “The head of a federal department is accused of taking money from a company in return for contracts”: That is the accusation. It tells you why this is happening, not who decides.
- If you tapped “On Tuesday the House voted to charge him”: That is a vote by lawmakers, so it points the same way. But the courtroom words come after it, and that is the part to read.

Look at who decides. The House votes to charge him, the Senate holds the trial, and at the end the senators vote. No judge decides anything.

Who casts the deciding votes or gives the ruling: lawmakers, or a judge?

### 12. A state, city or county government

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 12 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-states`]

Fourth: a government that covers only one place, such as a state, a city, a town or a county.

*The market square*

> Saturday traffic has made the market square in Marlow hard to cross on foot. On Tuesday ⟦the city council of Marlow voted to ban cars from the square on Saturdays⟧, starting in June.

The council of Marlow voted, and the vote covers one square in one city. A city council decides for its city the way Congress decides for the country: by voting. Nobody from the government of the whole country is in the story, and no judge.

Each state has its own government, with a legislature that makes its laws and a governor who leads it. Cities, towns and counties have their own too, with a council, a board or a mayor. A governor’s or mayor’s order counts as much as a vote. What matters is that the decider belongs to one place, not to the whole country.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find who decided: the city council of Marlow.** A council, a mayor, a governor or a state legislature is a government of one place.
2. **Check the place: one square in one city.** The call covers only that place, not the whole country.
3. **Check it is not a judge: the council voted, and nobody ruled.** A judge in a state court is still a judge, not the state.

This is **“A state, city or county government”**. It covers every level below the whole country: state, city, town and county.

### 16. The question to ask every time

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 3 · Card 16 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-kind`]

Here is the question and its four answers in one place.

**The question:** **“Who makes the final call?”**

**Its answers**

- **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**
  - Give this answer when the final call is a vote in the House or Senate, or someone asks Congress for one, such as on a law or money. A law stays Congress’s call even once the President signs it.
- **“The President or a federal agency”**
  - Give this answer when the final call is the President’s or a federal agency’s, or someone is asking them for one, such as an agency enforcing a law or the President refusing to sign one.
- **“A judge, in any court”**
  - Give this answer when the final call is a judge’s, in any court, federal or state, or someone is asking a judge to decide.
- **“A state, city or county government”**
  - Give this answer when the final call is made by a state’s lawmakers, governor or agencies, or a city, town or county, or someone asks them for it. A state court judge counts as a judge.

**Why it matters**

Each of the four can do different things and has different limits, so what you ask next depends on who decides. Most stories mention more than one of them. Look at the final call, or the one someone is asking for: everything before it is just how it got there.

Get this wrong and you go looking for limits that do not apply, however carefully you look. That is why it comes first.

**How to answer it**

1. **Read to the last sentence before you answer.** The final call, or the request for one, is usually there.
2. **Find what is decided last, or what someone is asked to decide: a vote, an order, a ruling, a refusal, or “asked a judge to settle it”.** Everything before it is only how the matter got there.
3. **Ask who makes that call: “Congress, in the House or the Senate”, “The President or a federal agency”, “A judge, in any court” or “A state, city or county government”.** That is your answer, even when the story names another one first.
4. **Find the exact words that show it.** If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Many stories name two or three of the four: a law and then the office that applies it, a rule and then a judge asked to block it. A story has only one final call, so do not weigh them against each other. Two pairs have no card of their own, and each is easy to mix up: **“The President or a federal agency”** and **“A judge, in any court”**, and **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”** and **“A state, city or county government”**. The test for each pair is below.

- Congress, in the House or the Senate or The President or a federal agency: Does the story end on a vote, or on something the President or an office decides or does? If a law is in the story, has it already passed?
- Congress, in the House or the Senate or A judge, in any court: Who casts the deciding votes or gives the ruling: lawmakers, or a judge?
- The President or a federal agency or A judge, in any court: Where does the story stop: on the President or an office deciding, or on a judge deciding, or on someone asking a judge to?
- A judge, in any court or A state, city or county government: Did a judge make the final call, whatever the court? Or did a state, city, town or county government make it?
- The President or a federal agency or A state, city or county government: Whose government does the office or official belong to: the whole country’s, or one state’s, city’s, town’s or county’s?
- Congress, in the House or the Senate or A state, city or county government: Do the lawmakers make a rule for the whole country, or for one state, city, town or county?

### 17. One whole story, where the start points the wrong way

Watch one story worked through. The first thing you notice is not what decides it, so read to the end.

*The lost-bag fine*

> Last month the House and the Senate passed a law that fines an airline $200 for every bag it loses. This week one airline asked a judge to decide whether the fine applies to bags lost by a partner airline that flew the last part of the trip.

**Question 1 of 1: Who makes the final call?**

> Last month the House and the Senate passed a law that fines an airline $200 for every bag it loses. This week one airline ⟦asked a judge to decide whether the fine applies to bags lost by a partner airline⟧ that flew the last part of the trip.

Answer: **“A judge, in any court”**

It opens with a law the House and the Senate passed. If it stopped there, the final call would be the lawmakers’.

It does not stop there: “asked a judge to decide whether the fine applies to bags lost by a partner airline”. The law is a month old, and now an airline wants a judge to say whether the fine covers bags a partner airline lost. The last thing in the story is a request to a judge.

**You are asked:** The House and the Senate passed the law, so this can look like a vote by lawmakers. What decides it? All of these are true. Choose the one that settles it.

- (a) The House and the Senate passed a law that fines an airline for every bag it loses.
- (b) The story ends with an airline asking a judge whether the fine covers bags a partner airline lost.
- (c) The law sets a fine of $200 for every bag that the airline loses.

**Shown as soon as you choose.** The one that settles it is (b): The story ends with an airline asking a judge whether the fine covers bags a partner airline lost.
- If you chose (a): True, and it is why this looks like **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**. If the story stopped there, that would be the answer.
- If you chose (c): True, and it is part of the law. But the size of a fine does not say whose call it is.

**Why this is A judge, in any court and not Congress, in the House or the Senate**

The first half of the story shows a vote, and the second half shows a request to a judge. The question asks for the final call, or the one the story asks for, and that is the judge’s.

Who casts the deciding votes or gives the ruling: lawmakers, or a judge? The vote is a month old and an airline is now asking a judge, so the answer is **“A judge, in any court”**.

**Does it look like a story you know?**

A second look: does this remind you of a story you know? A new law passed by the House and the Senate may bring back the bicycle-parts tax, which was **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**.

When a likeness and the answer disagree, go back to the words that answer the question: “asked a judge to decide whether the fine applies to bags lost by a partner airline”. The bicycle-parts story has nothing like them, because it ended on the Senate’s vote. The broken heater does, so the answer stands.

### 18. What to carry away

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 3 of 3 · Card 18 of 18*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap-kind`]

You have now answered the first question on your own.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

Who makes the final call?
- Congress, in the House or the Senate
- The President or a federal agency
- A judge, in any court
- A state, city or county government

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**: a vote in the House, the Senate or both, as the final call or the one someone asks for.
- **“The President or a federal agency”**: the President, or a federal agency, making the final call or being asked to make it.
- **“A judge, in any court”**: a judge deciding, or someone asking a judge to decide, as the last thing that happens.
- **“A state, city or county government”**: a state’s lawmakers, governor or agencies, or a city, town or county, making the final call or being asked to make it.

**To carry away**

- When a story says “the government”, ask who made the final call, and find the words that show it. If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet.
- Read to the end. Everything before the final call is only how the matter got there, however loud it is.
- A request counts. A story that ends by asking the Senate to vote, an office to rule or a judge to decide has the answer of whoever is asked.
- A signature on a law that lawmakers passed leaves the story with **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**. A refusal to sign is **“The President or a federal agency”**.
- A trial held by the Senate is **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**. A judge in a state court is **“A judge, in any court”**, not **“A state, city or county government”**.
- The same job can be done by an office of the whole country or by a state’s own. Whose office it is decides.
