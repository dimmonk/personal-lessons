## Part 1 of 2: Workers, one whole people, and old customs

### 1. Before you call a text “socialist” or “fascist”, check whose side it is on

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient-sides`]

Before you call a post “socialist”, a speech “fascist” or a neighbor “reactionary”, check whose side the text is actually on. It is always one of the five answers below, and most name-calling skips this step.

Someone shares a post and a friend says, “That’s just socialist.” A speech comes on the radio and an uncle says, “That’s fascist.” A neighbor defends the church bells and someone mutters, “Typical reactionary.”

Each name skips a simple question: who is the text speaking for? Working people against the people who own the businesses? One whole people, with its country first? Old customs handed down? What every person is owed? Or nobody in particular, because it is only a notice? Skip it and every name you pick after it is wrong too. Four of the five answers lead on to finer names in later units.

Whose side is it on?
- Workers against owners → working people on one side, owners on the other
- The nation, or its ordinary people → one people and its country, first
- The old ways → faith, home life and customs handed down
- Everyone’s rights → what every person is owed
- No side at all → who is in charge, or one practical matter, and no side

The unit has two parts, and you can stop after any of them.

1. Workers, one whole people, and old customs
2. Rights for every person, a text with no side, then the drill

Each starts from a real story. After each one you answer a quick question, and the reason is shown right away. Nothing here is graded. A miss only decides what comes back.

### 2. A word this subject leans on

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-ideology`]

One word comes up in every unit. Start with two neighbors at a bus stop.

> Two neighbors wait at a bus stop and talk about the country. Dolores says, 'A country is for the people who keep it running, so they should have the first say, and the government should answer to them.' Her neighbor Emeka says, 'A country is for what we were handed by the people before us, so we should keep what works and change it slowly.' They do not agree.

Each neighbor answered two questions in a few lines: who is the country for, and how should it be run? That is more than an opinion about one thing, such as where to put a bus stop. It is a few beliefs that fit together, so the first helps explain the rest.

**The word for this.** *Ideology*: a set of beliefs about who a country is for and how it should be run.

Calling a set of beliefs an *ideology* does not say whether they are right. It describes the beliefs a text shows, not the person who wrote it, and one short text shows only a few lines of them.


### 3. Workers against owners

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 3 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-class`]

First: a text that picks the workers’ side against the owners.

*The depot leaflet*

> A leaflet is handed out at the gates of the Dunmere freezer depot. 'The depot's owners took a record profit again this year. ⟦The drivers and loaders who keep it running have been told there is no money for a raise⟧. ⟦The people who own this depot and the people who work in it will not want the same thing, and this leaflet is on the side of the ones who work⟧.'

The leaflet splits the people at the depot in two: the drivers and loaders who are paid to work there, and the owners who take its profit. It says they want different things, and it says which side it is on: the workers.

A mention of wages or a boss is not enough: a notice about when wages are paid takes no side. What counts is two groups set against each other, and the text standing with one. It does not matter whether you agree, or whether the text sounds calm or angry.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the two groups: the drivers and loaders, and the owners.** You need both, because one group alone is not a split.
2. **Check they are set against each other: “will not want the same thing”.** Naming wages or a boss is not the same as setting them against each other.
3. **Find the side the text takes: “on the side of the ones who work”.** A text that only reports the split takes no side.

This is **“Workers against owners”**. Take the owners out of the leaflet and it has nothing left to say.

### 5. The nation, or its ordinary people

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 5 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-nation`]

Second: a text that speaks for one whole people, and puts it first.

*The bridge speech*

> From a speech at the opening of a new bridge on the border road: 'Wherever you were born in this country, whatever your trade or your party, ⟦you are one people with one history and one future⟧. When the nation is slighted, each of us is slighted. ⟦Our first loyalty is to the nation⟧.'

The speech does not split anyone into workers and owners. It says everyone in the country, whatever their trade or party, is one people, and that the nation comes first.

A people can be marked out by its country, as here, or by a shared culture, language or birth. Some texts speak for the whole nation as one. Others speak for the country’s ordinary people against a few at the top, such as politicians, officials or bankers. Both put one people first, and it does not matter whether that people deserves it.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find who “us” is: “you are one people with one history and one future”.** The text must name a people it calls its own.
2. **Check who it is against: no one here.** Some texts set the ordinary people against a few at the top, and that is still this answer.
3. **Find what comes first: “Our first loyalty is to the nation.”** The text must put that people first, not just mention it.

This is **“The nation, or its ordinary people”**. The speech works for everyone in the country at once, whatever their job.

### 7. Workers against owners or The nation, or its ordinary people: telling them apart

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-class-nation`]

These two sound alike: both can be angry about the same closing, and both say “us” against someone else.

**Story A**

> The Harrow cannery closes on Friday and four hundred jobs go with it. The owners are moving the work to a cheaper plant and keeping the profit, while the people who stood at the line for thirty years are left to pay for it. We are on their side.

**Story B**

> The Harrow cannery closes on Friday, and a piece of this country closes with it. We are one people, and when one of our towns is hollowed out the whole nation is smaller. A country that cannot feed itself from its own fields and its own factories is not free, and our first duty is to the nation.

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the closing of the Harrow cannery. Compare one thing: who is “us”, and who is “them”?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“The nation, or its ordinary people”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A, “us” is the people who stood at the line for thirty years, and “them” is the owners who moved the work and kept the profit. Nobody is speaking for the country. That is **“Workers against owners”**.

In Story B, “us” is “one people”, and “the whole nation is smaller”. There are no owners and no workers in it, and its first duty is to the nation. That is **“The nation, or its ordinary people”**.

Same cannery, same anger. What differs is the line the text draws: between workers and owners, or around the whole country.

**How to tell them apart**

Is “us” the people who work for pay, against their owners? Or is “us” one whole people?


### 8. The old ways

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 8 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-tradition`]

Third: a text that holds up what was handed down as the guide.

*The harvest sermon*

> From a sermon read at the harvest service in the village of Elmsley: 'Our grandparents kept the Sabbath, married in this church and raised their children in the faith, and ⟦they handed all of it down to us⟧. ⟦The faith, the family and the old customs are what should guide how this country is run⟧. Where they are set aside, there is nothing left to steer by.'

The sermon names what the grandparents kept (the Sabbath, the church, the faith) and says those customs should guide how the country is run. Other texts hold up a way of home life, or an old order of crown, church and rank. It does not matter whether the faith is true or the custom is good.

What counts is what the text holds up as the guide. Mentioning a church or the past is not enough: a notice that the church bells ring at ten holds nothing up. And speaking of “this country” does not make a text about one people: here the country is only the place the customs should guide.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find what was handed down: “they handed all of it down to us”.** The text must name something from the past, such as a faith, a church or a custom.
2. **Find what the text says it is for: “should guide how this country is run”.** Naming the past is not enough: the text must hold it up as the guide.
3. **Check what comes first: the customs, not the country.** The country is only the place they should guide.

This is **“The old ways”**. Take away what was handed down and the sermon has nothing left to say.

## Part 2 of 2: Rights for every person, a text with no side, then the drill

### 11. Everyone’s rights

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 11 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-rights`]

Fourth: a text that says what every person is owed.

*The open-counter pamphlet*

> From a pamphlet of the Open Counter Society: 'Nobody should need a license to open a stall or a barber's chair. ⟦Each person is owed the freedom to speak, to believe, to own and to trade as they choose⟧, and ⟦that freedom comes before any plan anyone has for the country⟧.'

The pamphlet does not speak for workers, owners, one people or old customs. It speaks for any person at all, and says what each one is owed: the freedom to speak, believe, own and trade.

A right is something you are owed just for being a person. Other texts name other things owed to everyone: a hearing before a decision is made about you, a school, a doctor, or fair treatment whatever your race, sex or income. Texts that disagree about a great deal can all be this answer. Mentioning a school is not enough: a notice that the school opens at nine says nothing is owed to anyone.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find what is owed: “the freedom to speak, to believe, to own and to trade”.** The text must say what a person gets just by being a person.
2. **Check who it is owed to: “each person”.** It is not one group, one people or the workers: it is anyone at all.
3. **Check it comes first: “that freedom comes before any plan anyone has for the country”.** The text must put it first, not just mention it.

This is **“Everyone’s rights”**. It names no group to stand with and none to stand against.

These two are easy to mix up when a text talks about race, because both say people are treated differently by the group they were born into. They point opposite ways.

**Story A**

> From a pamphlet of the Order of the Old Blood: 'Our race is the oldest and the best, and the others were born to serve it. Those of other blood may live among us only if they know their place beneath us.'

**Story B**

> At a housing hearing: 'Nothing in the lending rules mentions race. But applicants of one race are sent to the back of the line year after year, and a rule that treats everyone alike while leaving them there is a rule that has to change. Nobody is above anybody here. Fair treatment is owed to every applicant.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about race. Compare one thing: is one people placed above the others, or is nobody placed above anybody?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Everyone’s rights”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A, the text says its own race is the best and the others were born to serve it. It puts one people first, above the rest. That is **“The nation, or its ordinary people”**.

In Story B, the text says no rule mentions race, yet applicants of one race are left at the back of the line. It says “Nobody is above anybody here” and asks for fair treatment for every applicant. That is **“Everyone’s rights”**.

They share a word and nothing else. The word and the anger do not tell them apart. Whether one people is placed above the others does.

**How to tell them apart**

Is one people put first, maybe above the others? Or is something owed to every person?


### 14. No side at all

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 14 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-none`]

Fifth: a text that stands with nobody. Most of what you read in a day is this.

*The elevator notice*

> A notice on the doors of Birch House: '⟦The elevator will be out of service from Monday, March 3 until Friday, March 14 while the motor is replaced⟧. ⟦Residents who need help with the stairs should call the superintendent⟧ at the number below. Trash is collected on Thursdays as usual.'

The notice does not stand with workers, a people, old customs or what every person is owed. It only says what will happen and what to do about it.

That is very common (a schedule, a sign, a letter about trash pickup), and it is an answer, not a failure to find one: you looked, and there was nothing to name. A text that says who holds power and how they keep it, such as who chairs a council, is this answer too. Even a ruler’s cruel orders name no side, because saying who decides is not saying whom the text speaks for.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find what it says will happen: the elevator is out of service from March 3 to March 14.** A date and a reason are a practical matter, not a side.
2. **Find what it tells you to do: “call the superintendent”.** An instruction is about one practical matter.
3. **Check it stands with nobody: no workers, no people, no customs, nothing owed to everyone.** If it did, it would be one of the first four answers.

This is **“No side at all”**. After this answer nothing more is asked: there is no finer name to give.

### 17. The one question to ask first

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 17 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-sides`]

Here is the question and its five answers in one place.

**The question:** **“Whose side is it on?”**

**Its answers**

- **“Workers against owners”**
  - Give this answer when the text splits people into those who work for a wage and those who own the businesses (or got rich owning them), and takes the workers’ side.
- **“The nation, or its ordinary people”**
  - Give this answer when the text speaks for one people, marked by its country, culture or blood, and puts it first: the whole nation as one, or its ordinary people against a few at the top.
- **“The old ways”**
  - Give this answer when the text holds up ways handed down from the past (a faith, home life, old customs, or an old order of crown, church and rank) as what should guide the country.
- **“Everyone’s rights”**
  - Give this answer when the text puts first what every person is owed: the freedom to speak, believe, own and trade, a fair start in life, or fair treatment whatever their group.
- **“No side at all”**
  - Give this answer when the text only says who holds power and how they keep it, or how to handle one practical matter, and takes none of the other four sides.

**Why it matters**

Each side wants something different, and each has its own names. The next questions ask what that side wants, so this answer decides which questions come next. Something that takes no side has no name to find.

Get this wrong and every question after it goes wrong too. Take an elevator notice for a text on the workers’ side and you go looking for owners who are not there. Take a text about what every person is owed for a text about one people and you change whom it speaks for. Each of the first four answers is where a different *ideology* starts.

**How to answer it**

1. **Read to the end before you answer.** The side a text takes is often in the last line.
2. **First look for workers set against owners: “Workers against owners”.** It wins over every other answer.
3. **Next look for customs held up as the guide: “The old ways”.** It wins over one people put first, and over rights.
4. **Next look for one people put first: “The nation, or its ordinary people”.** It wins over rights.
5. **Next look for something every person is owed: “Everyone’s rights”.** That is what is left when nobody is set against anybody.
6. **None of these? It is “No side at all”.** Then the text is about one practical matter, or about who is in charge.
7. **Find the exact words that show your answer.** If you can’t find them, you don’t have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Some texts show two answers at once, such as a speech that talks about the country and also takes the workers’ side against their bosses. Use the order above. The test for each pair is below.

- Workers against owners or The nation, or its ordinary people: Is “us” the people who work for pay, against their owners? Or is “us” one whole people?

*The living-wage speech*

> From a campaign speech: 'They call it the national living wage, as though the nation had handed it over. The nation gave nothing. **Question 1 of 1: Whose side is it on?**

> From a campaign speech: 'They call it the national living wage, as though the nation had handed it over. The nation gave nothing. ⟦The cleaners and the drivers won it from their employers, in strikes⟧, and every raise since has been won the same way. ⟦A country's wealth is made by the people who work and held by the people who own, and I am with the first⟧.'

Answer: **“Workers against owners”**

The speech opens with “the national living wage” and says “the nation gave nothing”. If you stopped there, you would look for a people put first.

It does not stop there: “The cleaners and the drivers won it from their employers, in strikes” and “A country's wealth is made by the people who work and held by the people who own, and I am with the first”. The cleaners and drivers are on one side, their employers on the other, and the speaker is with the first. The word “national” only says the wage is the same everywhere.

**You are asked:** The speech talks about “the nation” and “a country”, so it can look like **“The nation, or its ordinary people”**. What decides it? All of these are true. Choose the one that settles it.

- (a) The speech says “the national living wage” and “a country’s wealth”.
- (b) The speech says the cleaners and drivers won the wage from their employers, and that the speaker is with the people who work.
- (c) It is a campaign speech.

**Shown as soon as you choose.** The one that settles it is (b): The speech says the cleaners and drivers won the wage from their employers, and that the speaker is with the people who work.
- If you chose (a): True, and it is why this looks like **“The nation, or its ordinary people”**. But those words only say the wage is the same across the country.
- If you chose (c): True, but a campaign speech could be for any side.

**Why this is Workers against owners and not The nation, or its ordinary people**

The speech uses the words “nation” and “country”, but it never speaks for one people. It says the wage was won from employers, and it splits the country into those who work and those who own.

The test for this pair: Is “us” the people who work for pay, against their owners? Or is “us” one whole people? Here the line runs between those who work and those who own, so the answer is **“Workers against owners”**.

**Does it look like a story you know?**

A second look: does this remind you of a story you know? A speech that says “nation” and “country” may bring back the bridge speech, which was **“The nation, or its ordinary people”**.

### 20. What to carry away

*Unit One · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 20 of 20*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap-sides`]

You have now answered the first question on your own.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

Whose side is it on?
- Workers against owners
- The nation, or its ordinary people
- The old ways
- Everyone’s rights
- No side at all

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **“Workers against owners”**: people who work for a wage, people who own the farms, factories, shops or banks, and the text on the workers’ side.
- **“The nation, or its ordinary people”**: a people the text calls its own, marked by its country, its culture or its blood, and that people put first.
- **“The old ways”**: ways handed down from the past (a faith, home life, old customs, or an old order of crown, church and rank), held up as what should guide the country.
- **“Everyone’s rights”**: something every person is owed (the freedom to speak, believe, own and trade, a fair start in life, or fair treatment whatever their group), put first.
- **“No side at all”**: who holds power and how they keep it, or one practical matter, and none of the other four sides taken.

**To carry away**

- Before you name a text, ask whose side it is on, and find the words that show it. If you can’t find them, you don’t have an answer yet.
- There are five answers. Four put someone or something first: **“Workers against owners”**, **“The nation, or its ordinary people”**, **“The old ways”** and **“Everyone’s rights”**. The fifth, **“No side at all”**, is for a text that only says what will happen or who is in charge.
- The answer is not a verdict. A text can be fair or unfair, calm or angry, and still get any of the five.
- A word like “national”, “workers” or “church” is not an answer. The words that decide are the ones that say who or what comes first.
- When a text shows two answers, the first in this order wins: **“Workers against owners”**, then **“The old ways”**, then **“The nation, or its ordinary people”**, then **“Everyone’s rights”**.
- A name thrown at a text is not a description of it. A name has to be earned from what the text says.
