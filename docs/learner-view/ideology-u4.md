## Part 1 of 2: Keep what is left, or ask for it back

### 1. Old ways: keep them, or bring them back?

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 13*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient-ways`]

When someone says a politician wants to “keep our traditions”, or calls someone “reactionary”, you can tell what is really being asked for: to keep what is still there, or to ask for back what was taken.

A neighbor says the village should “keep its traditions”. Someone else says a new law “destroyed our way of life”. A columnist calls one politician “reactionary” and another “just conservative”, and never says what either word means.

Unit One gave both kinds of text the same answer: **“The old ways”**. They are not the same. One asks for what is still there to be kept, and for change to be slow. The other says something was taken away wrongly, and asks for it back. One question tells them apart: **“What does it want done with the old ways?”**

Every text in this unit is invented, and none says what any real person or party believes.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“Whose side is it on?”** Its answers:

- **“Workers against owners”**: give this answer when the text splits people into those who work for a wage and those who own the businesses (or got rich owning them), and takes the workers’ side.
- **“The nation, or its ordinary people”**: give this answer when the text speaks for one people, marked by its country, culture or blood, and puts it first: the whole nation as one, or its ordinary people against a few at the top.
- **“The old ways”**: give this answer when the text holds up ways handed down from the past (a faith, home life, old customs, or an old order of crown, church and rank) as what should guide the country. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“Everyone’s rights”**: give this answer when the text puts first what every person is owed: the freedom to speak, believe, own and trade, a fair start in life, or fair treatment whatever their group.
- **“No side at all”**: give this answer when the text only says who holds power and how they keep it, or how to handle one practical matter, and takes none of the other four sides.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. Conservatism

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 13*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-conserv`]

First: a text that wants what is still there to stay.

*The boundary walk*

> From the Eastby village newsletter: 'On the first Sunday of May we walk the boundary of the village and bless the fields, as our grandparents did, and the children carry the banner. This custom, and the faith behind it, should guide how Eastby plans for the years ahead. ⟦Keep the walk⟧. If the route must change now that the new road cuts across it, ⟦let it change slowly, a step at a time, and ask the old walkers first⟧.'

The walk is still walked, so the text asks for nothing to come back. It asks for the walk to stay, and for any change to come slowly, with the old walkers asked first.

The thinking behind this: what has been handed down has been tested by many years, and anything new is risky until it has been tried. People who disagree say that going slowly can be a way of never changing. No side is taken here. We only read what the text asks for.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the old way the text holds up: the boundary walk.** It is the thing the text wants to protect.
2. **Check it is still there: the village walks it every May, and the children carry the banner.** If it is still there, there is nothing to bring back.
3. **Find what the text asks: “Keep the walk”, and change it slowly if the new road forces it.** Keeping it and going slowly is the whole request.
4. **Check that it asks for nothing lost to come back.** Asking for something lost back is the next name.

This is **Conservatism**, from “conserve”: to keep safe. It says what a text asks for, and is neither praise nor blame.

You may also hear this called “small-c conservatism”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Conservatism**.

### 4. Reactionary conservatism

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 13*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-react`]

Second: a text that says something was taken away, and asks for it back.

*The Church courts of Aldmere*

> From a pamphlet in the kingdom of Aldmere: 'For six hundred years the Church courts of Aldmere judged our marriages and our wills, and the bishops sat on the king's council. Eleven years ago the Assembly abolished the courts and put the bishops out of the council. ⟦That was a wrong, and we do not call it a reform⟧. ⟦We ask that the Church courts sit again, that the bishops return to the king's council⟧, and that Aldmere be ordered as it was.'

In the walk story, the walk was still being walked. Here the Church courts are gone: the Assembly abolished them eleven years ago. The pamphlet calls that a wrong and asks for them back, which is a demand to put something right, not just a wish to keep what is there.

People who think like this hold that a country’s order was built up over centuries, and that nobody had the right to tear it down. People who disagree say the old order was unfair to many, or cannot be brought back, or was not lost wrongly. No side is taken here.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the old order the text names: the Church courts, and the bishops on the king’s council.** It must be something that once stood, not just a feeling about the past.
2. **Check it is gone: the Assembly abolished the courts and put the bishops out.** If it is still there, there is nothing to bring back.
3. **Find the words that call its loss a wrong: “we do not call it a reform”.** Being sad is not enough; the text must say it was wrongly done.
4. **Find the request: “that the Church courts sit again”.** Without the request, it is only sadness.

This is **Reactionary conservatism**: reacting against a change already made, by trying to undo it. It is often thrown around as an insult, but here it only describes what a text asks for.

You may also hear this called “throne and altar” or “traditionalism”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Reactionary conservatism**.

### 6. Conservatism or Reactionary conservatism: telling them apart

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 6 of 13*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-conserv-react`]

These two are the easiest to mix up, because both love what was handed down. Here they are about one school.

**Story A**

> From a parents' letter about Marrow Lane church school: 'Our children are taught the old hymns each Sunday term, as we were, and the pastor still chooses the principal, as he has since our grandparents' time. These ways should guide how the school is run. Keep them. If the school must change, let it be slowly, with the parents asked at every step.'

**Story B**

> From a parents' letter about Marrow Lane church school: 'For two hundred years the school belonged to the church, and the pastor chose the principal. The School Transfer Act took the school from the church. That was a wrong, and it should never have been done. These old ways should guide how the school is run, so undo the Act, give the school back to the church, and let the pastor choose the principal again.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the church school at Marrow Lane, and both want its old ways to guide it. Compare one thing: does the text ask for what is there to stay, or for what has gone to come back?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Bring back what is gone”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the school is still a church school, with its Sunday hymns and the pastor choosing the principal. The text asks to keep them, with slow change. Nothing is gone and nothing is asked back. That is **Conservatism**.

In Story B the school was taken from the church by an act. The text calls that a wrong and asks for the act to be undone and the school given back. That is **Reactionary conservatism**.

How strongly each text feels does not decide it. What it asks for does.

**How to tell them apart**

Is something already gone, and does the text call that a wrong and ask for it back? Or is everything it names still there, with the text asking only to keep it and go slowly?


### 7. The question to ask about old ways

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 13*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-ways`]

Here is the question and its two answers in one place.

**The question:** **“What does it want done with the old ways?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other one.

- **“Keep what is left, and change slowly”**
  - Give this answer when the text wants the faith, home life or customs handed down kept, with any change made slowly, and does not ask to bring back something that is gone.
  - It leads to **Conservatism**.
- **“Bring back what is gone”**
  - Give this answer when the text says an old order of church, crown, rank or custom was wrongly torn down, and asks to bring it back.
  - It leads to **Reactionary conservatism**.

**Why it matters**

Both names value what was handed down. One says the present is a wrong to undo and wants a lost order back; the other accepts the present and asks only that change be slow.

Unit One’s question, **“Whose side is it on?”**, gave both names the same answer: **“The old ways”**. This question is what tells them apart.

How fond a text sounds, how old the thing is and how angry the writer is tell you nothing. Only what the text asks for does.

**How to answer it**

1. **Find the sentence that says what to do with what the text holds up.** That sentence is the request, and the request decides.
2. **Still there? Look for words that ask to keep it and change it slowly: “Keep what is left, and change slowly”.** Nothing has gone, so nothing is asked back.
3. **Gone? Look for all three: what was lost, words calling its loss a wrong, and the request for it back: “Bring back what is gone”.** Miss one and it is not this answer.
4. **Mourning what is gone, but asking only to keep what is left? That is “Keep what is left, and change slowly”.** Sadness is not a request.
5. **Find the exact words that show your answer.** If you can’t find them, you don’t have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

- Conservatism or Reactionary conservatism: Is something already gone, and does the text call that a wrong and ask for it back? Or is everything it names still there, with the text asking only to keep it and go slowly?

### 9. Old ways or one people, at the same festival

*Unit Four · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 9 of 13*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-conserv-nationalism`]

A text can love its country and its customs at once. Here are two about the same harbor festival, and they put different things first.

**Story A**

> From the Port Selby harbor newsletter: 'Each midsummer the crews carry their boats' names to the quay for the old blessing, as their fathers and mothers did before them. That blessing should guide how the harbor festival is planned. Keep it. If the quay must be rebuilt, let the work be slow and done in pieces, so that the blessing is never lost.'

**Story B**

> From a speech at the Port Selby harbor festival: 'Look around you. Fishers and clerks, young and old, left and right, we are one people, and a day like this shows that what divides us counts for less than what holds us together. A country that has such a day has every reason to be proud. Let us go on voting, arguing and disagreeing as we always have, and let us go on being one people when the argument is over.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the harbor festival at Port Selby, and both love it. Compare one thing: what does the text put first, ways handed down, or one people?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“The old ways”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story A.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the blessing of the boats was handed down by the crews before them. The text says it should guide how the festival is planned, and asks to keep it and rebuild the quay slowly. It speaks of no people to be put first. That is **Conservatism**.

In Story B the festival shows that we are one people, and what divides us counts for less than what holds us together. It names no old ways to guide anything, and it leaves voting and disagreement alone. That is **Nationalism**.

Both texts love the same festival. What each puts first is different: ways handed down, or the people who share the festival.

**How to tell them apart**

What does the text put first: one people, marked out by its country, culture or blood? Or ways handed down from the past, which it says should guide?

### 11. Old customs kept, and the owners made to pay

*The quarry gate notice*

- (b) The petition says the House was closed wrongly, and asks for it to be reopened and the sisters brought back.
- (c) Two hundred people in Brenwick Cross signed the petition, so many people agree with it.

**Shown as soon as you choose.** The one that settles it is (b): The petition says the House was closed wrongly, and asks for it to be reopened and the sisters brought back.
- If you chose (a): True, and it is why this looks like **Conservatism**. But how gently a text asks does not say what it asks for.
- If you chose (c): True, but a text for either name could have two hundred signatures.

**Why this is Reactionary conservatism and not Conservatism**

For **Conservatism**, a text needs this: faith, home life or customs handed down from the past, a wish to keep them, any change made slowly, and no call to bring back something that is gone. The petition is patient, but it fails the last part: it asks for something that has gone to be brought back.

Put the question from the two school stories: Is something already gone, and does the text call that a wrong and ask for it back? Or is everything it names still there, with the text asking only to keep it and go slowly? Here something gone is asked back, so the answer to **“What does it want done with the old ways?”** is **“Bring back what is gone”**.

**Does it look like a story you know?**

A second look: does this remind you of a story you know? A text that speaks gently of waiting and going slowly may bring back the boundary walk, which was **Conservatism**. So the likeness and the answer seem to disagree.

When that happens, go back to the words that answer the question: “that was a wrong we will not call anything else” and “We ask only that the House be reopened, the sisters be brought back to it, and the old order of the House be restored”. The boundary walk has nothing like them. The Church courts of Aldmere do: something gone, called a wrong, and asked for back. So the answer stands.

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Conservatism**: faith, home life or customs handed down from the past, a wish to keep them, any change made slowly, and no call to bring back something that is gone.
- **Reactionary conservatism**: an old order of church, crown, rank or custom that the text says was wrongly torn down, and a call to bring it back.

- First check that the text holds up old ways as its guide: **“The old ways”**. Then ask **“What does it want done with the old ways?”**

- Both names describe what a text asks for. They do not say whether the text is right, or what its writer is like.
- Two decisions to remember. A text that speaks for one people and also holds up an old order gets **“The old ways”**, even if it asks for parliament to be closed. A text that holds up old customs and also sets working people against owners gets **“Workers against owners”**.
