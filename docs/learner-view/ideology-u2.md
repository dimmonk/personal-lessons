## Part 1 of 2: Owners keep the businesses, nothing is said, or the government takes over

### 1. Before you call a plan “communism”, check what it asks for

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 28*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

Before you call a plan “communism” or “just socialism”, check what it actually says about the businesses and the government.

A friend says a plan to tax big companies is “communism”. Someone else says the same plan is “just socialism”. Both are on the side of working people against owners, and that is all they share.

Read what each text asks for. One wants the owners to keep their businesses and pay more tax. Another wants the businesses handed to the government. Another wants no government at all. Another only says whose side it is on. Each gets a different name.

Two questions sort them. First: **“What does it say about the businesses?”** Then: **“What does it want done with the government?”** You answer each by finding the words in the text. If the text has no words about it, the answer is that it does not say.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“Whose side is it on?”** Its answers:

- **“Workers against owners”**: give this answer when the text splits people into those who work for a wage and those who own the businesses (or got rich owning them), and takes the workers’ side. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“The nation, or its ordinary people”**: give this answer when the text speaks for one people, marked by its country, culture or blood, and puts it first: the whole nation as one, or its ordinary people against a few at the top.
- **“The old ways”**: give this answer when the text holds up ways handed down from the past (a faith, home life, old customs, or an old order of crown, church and rank) as what should guide the country.
- **“Everyone’s rights”**: give this answer when the text puts first what every person is owed: the freedom to speak, believe, own and trade, a fair start in life, or fair treatment whatever their group.
- **“No side at all”**: give this answer when the text only says who holds power and how they keep it, or how to handle one practical matter, and takes none of the other four sides.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. Social democracy

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 28*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-socdem`]

The most common text on the workers’ side leaves the businesses with their owners and asks for a fairer share.

*The warehouse leaflet*

> From a leaflet by the Lowfield warehouse staff association: 'The people who own the warehouses make their money from our work, and they keep most of it. ⟦We do not ask to take the warehouses from them. We ask for a law that puts a floor under pay, and for a tax on the owners' profits that pays for sick pay and pensions⟧, so that the people who do the work get a fair share.'

The warehouse staff do not want to take the warehouses. They object to how the money is divided, so they ask the government to change the dividing, not the owner.

They ask in two ways, and a text may use either or both. A floor under pay is a minimum wage: the lowest pay the law allows. A tax on the owners’ profits pays for things working people need, such as sick pay, pensions, health care and schools.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check the owners keep the businesses: “We do not ask to take the warehouses from them.”** If the text takes them away, it is a different name.
2. **Find what it asks the government for: a law that puts a floor under pay, and a tax on the owners’ profits.** Both change who gets what, and neither changes who owns what.
3. **Find where the money goes: sick pay and pensions.** The tax pays for things working people need.

This is **Social democracy**: a fairer share, with no change of owner.

You may also hear this called “the welfare state” or “the Nordic model”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Social democracy**.

### 4. Class politics on its own

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 28*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-classonly`]

Many texts on the workers’ side have no plan at all, and that has a name too.

*The laundry notice*

> A notice pinned up at the Brightwell laundry: 'The women who work the presses are paid by the hour, and the owners are paid from what the presses make. We are on the side of the people who work. ⟦Come to the meeting on Thursday and stand with us⟧.'

Most of what is written for working people looks like this. A notice goes up to bring people together and say whose side they are on, not to set out a plan for the whole economy.

So when you ask what the text says about the businesses, there is nothing to find. The marked words are where a plan could have stood, and an invitation to a meeting stands there. The answer is **“The text does not say”**, and it is a real answer. An angry notice and a calm one can both say nothing about the businesses.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the two sides: the women who work the presses, and the owners.** The text is on the workers’ side, so it belongs in this unit.
2. **Look for a plan for the laundry: who should own it, a tax, a law on pay. There is none.** Finding nothing is itself the answer: **“The text does not say”**.
3. **Check what it asks of the reader: “Come to the meeting on Thursday and stand with us.”** An invitation is not a plan.
4. **Check it says nothing about power or the government: it mentions neither.** A text that said who should hold power would get another name.

This is **Class politics on its own**: the text takes a side and stops there.

### 6. Social democracy or Class politics on its own: telling them apart

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 6 of 28*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-socdem-classonly`]

These two are the easiest to mix up. Both are on the workers’ side, and neither takes the businesses from their owners.

**Story A**

> Milkers at the Greenvale dairy have put out a statement: 'The family that owns the dairy took a large profit this year, and we have had no raise in four years. We are on the side of the people who do the milking. We would leave the dairy with its owners, but set a minimum wage that rises with prices, and tax its profits to pay for free training for every worker.'

**Story B**

> Milkers at the Greenvale dairy have put out a statement: 'The family that owns the dairy took a large profit this year, and we have had no raise in four years. We are on the side of the people who do the milking. Come to the gate on Monday and tell the owners we are not alone.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the Greenvale dairy, with the same owners and the same four years without a raise. Compare one thing: does the text go on to say what the government should do about pay, taxes or services?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Owners keep them, but are taxed to pay for services”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story A.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the text goes on to a plan. The owners keep the dairy, and it asks for a minimum wage that rises with prices and a tax on the dairy’s profits to pay for training. The answer is **“Owners keep them, but are taxed to pay for services”**, so this is **Social democracy**.

In Story B the text stops after taking the workers’ side. It says nothing about who owns the dairy, about tax or about services. The answer is **“The text does not say”**, so this is **Class politics on its own**.

The complaint is word for word the same. What comes after it is different: a plan, or nothing.

**How to tell them apart**

Does the text ask the government to do anything about pay, taxes or services, or about who owns the businesses? Or does it only say whose side it is on?


### 7. Democratic socialism

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 28*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-demsoc`]

In the texts so far the owners kept their businesses, or nothing was said about them. This one takes them away.

*The ferry crews*

> From a campaign leaflet by the Westmarch ferry crews: 'The ferry company's owners take the fares, and the crews take the risks. We say ⟦the ferries should belong to the public, run by the government for everyone⟧. We will ask the voters to put a government in place that will do it.'

The ferry crews do not want the owners to keep the ferries. “Belong to the public” means the government runs them for the people who ride, and not for a shareholder’s profit.

A text may want only the biggest businesses handed over, such as the railways or the banks. That is enough. It may also say how, as this one does by asking the voters, or say nothing about how.

**How to spot it**

1. **Check the owners lose the businesses: the ferries “should belong to the public”.** A tax would leave the ferries with their owners.
2. **Find who takes over: the government, “run by the government for everyone”.** The aim is to run them for the people who use them, not for profit.
3. **Look for how: “We will ask the voters”.** Voters can say no, so no party is seizing power.

This is **Democratic socialism**: the businesses, or the biggest of them, pass to the government, and no party seizes power to do it.

### 9. Social democracy or Democratic socialism: telling them apart

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 9 of 28*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-socdem-demsoc`]

Both ask the government to act for the people who work in the businesses. Here is where they part.

**Story A**

> Nurses at the Redmoor private hospitals say: 'The company that owns the hospitals takes its profit, and the nurses take the night shifts. We stand with the nurses against the owners. The hospitals can stay with their owners, as long as the government taxes their profits to pay for nurses' pensions and for training places.'

**Story B**

> Nurses at the Redmoor private hospitals say: 'The company that owns the hospitals takes its profit, and the nurses take the night shifts. We stand with the nurses against the owners. The hospitals should be taken from the company and run by the government for everyone.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the Redmoor private hospitals, and both stand with the nurses. Compare one thing: after the government has acted, who owns the hospitals?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“The government should take them over”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the hospitals stay with their owners, and the government taxes their profits to pay for nurses’ pensions and training places. The owners still own them afterwards. The answer is **“Owners keep them, but are taxed to pay for services”**, so this is **Social democracy**.

In Story B the hospitals are taken from the company and run by the government for everyone. The owners no longer own them. The answer is **“The government should take them over”**, so this is **Democratic socialism**.

A tax changes what the owners keep. A handover changes who the owner is. A text that asks for both is **Democratic socialism**.

**How to tell them apart**

Once the government has acted, who owns the business: the same owners as before, or the government?


### 10. Marxism-Leninism

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 10 of 28*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-ml`]

This text cares as much about who holds power as about the businesses.

*The mill pamphlet*

> From a pamphlet of the Calder Mill workers' party: 'The owners will never give up what they hold, and the courts and police they pay for will protect them. So ⟦the workers, led by our party, must take power and keep it. Once we hold it there will be one party, ours, and no rivals⟧. The mills will belong to the government we form, run for everyone.'

The earlier texts asked the government for things, or promised to win an election and pass a law. This one says the owners are too strong to beat with votes, so the workers must take power through a party and keep it, with no rival allowed.

Here the words about power decide, not the words about the businesses. What matters is that the text never offers to give power up at an election.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find who takes power: “the workers, led by our party”.** A party takes power instead of asking the voters for it.
2. **Check how: they “must take power and keep it”.** Power that is taken, not voted for, is the first half.
3. **Look for rivals: “there will be one party, ours, and no rivals”.** With no rival there is no election it could lose.

This is **Marxism-Leninism**: a party, or the workers, takes power and keeps it. Check the words before you use it, because “communist” is often thrown at plans that say nothing of the kind.

You may also hear this called “communism” or “Leninism” or “Soviet-style communism”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Marxism-Leninism**.

### 12. Democratic socialism or Marxism-Leninism: telling them apart

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 12 of 28*

[reviewers only: card kind `lookalike`, id `look-demsoc-ml`]

These two ask for the same thing to be done with the businesses. The difference is the road to it.

**Story A**

> The Hartfell miners' union says: 'The mines' owners sell the coal, and the miners dig it, and we stand with the miners. The mines should pass to the government, to be run for everyone. We will win a majority in parliament and pass the law.'

**Story B**

> The Hartfell miners' party says: 'The mines' owners sell the coal, and the miners dig it, and we stand with the miners. The mines should pass to the government, to be run for everyone. The party will take power and keep it, and it will allow no rival party to stand.'

**What to compare.** Both stories are about the Hartfell mines and say the same about them. Compare one thing: once the text has what it wants, can the people asking be voted out?

**You are asked:** Which story gives the answer **“Take power for the workers, and allow no other party”**? (Story A / Story B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Story B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Story A the text will win a majority in parliament and pass the law. A majority can be lost at the next election, so the power stays with the voters. This is **Democratic socialism**.

In Story B the party will take power, keep it and allow no rival party. That cannot be lost at an election. The answer is **“Take power for the workers, and allow no other party”**, so this is **Marxism-Leninism**.

The mines, the miners and the handover are the same in both. What differs is whether the people asking can be voted out.

**How to tell them apart**

Does the text say that a party, or the workers, will take power by force or rule as the only party? If it says nothing about power, the answer is no.


### 13. Nothing about the businesses, and a party that rules alone

*The shipyard bulletin*

The pieces you can tap:
1. “The yard's owners are on one side and we, the yard workers, are on the other, and we are on our side”
2. “Hold the line on Thursday”
3. “When the committee has the city, it will rule alone and no rival party will be allowed to stand”

- If you tapped “The yard's owners are on one side and we, the yard workers, are on the other, and we are on our side”: That names the two sides. It says nothing about the yard or about who holds power.
- If you tapped “Hold the line on Thursday”: That is a call to stand together. It says nothing about the yard or about power.

**Why this is Marxism-Leninism and not Class politics on its own**

The question about the businesses does not settle it, because the text says nothing about them. The question about the government does: the committee will rule alone and allow no rival party, so the answer is **“Take power for the workers, and allow no other party”**, and that leaves one name, **Marxism-Leninism**.

Nothing has to be said about the businesses for this name to fit. What matters is who will hold power.

**How to tell them apart**

Does the text say anything about who will hold power, or how? If it says a party will take power and rule alone, the answer is yes.

That is why there are two questions: a text can say nothing about the businesses and a great deal about power.

## Part 2 of 2: Workers own the businesses, an explanation, and the two questions

### 14. Anarchism

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 14 of 28*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-anarch`]

The last name had one party hold power. This text wants nobody to hold power at all.

*The print-works zine*

> From a zine handed out at the Marsh Lane print works: 'The people who own the print works tell us what to do, and the government tells the owners what is allowed. We want neither. The print works should belong to the people who work in it. ⟦The town should be run by open meetings of everyone in it, with no government at all⟧.'

The zine wants no owner and no government giving orders. That does not mean disorder: people who hold this view say workers can run their work and their towns by agreement, in open meetings.

Other names also give the print works to the people who work in it, so the words about the government are what set this one apart. The text must want the government gone now: a text that wants to use it first, and says it will fade away one day, is not asking for that.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find what it says about the government: “with no government at all”.** This is the question that decides it.
2. **Check it wants the government gone now, not used first: the town is to be run by open meetings.** A government used first and dropped later is a different plan.
3. **Look at what takes its place: “open meetings of everyone in it”.** Nobody holds power over the rest, not even a party.
4. **Find the print works: they “should belong to the people who work in it”.** Other names say this too, so it cannot decide on its own.

This is **Anarchism**: no government, and people running things together. The word comes from a Greek word for “without a ruler”, not “without order”.

You may also hear this called “libertarian socialism” or “anarcho-syndicalism”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Anarchism**.

### 20. Marxism

*Unit Two · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 20 of 28*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-marx`]

This text asks for nothing. It explains.

*The weaver’s sums*

> From a pamphlet written for the weavers of Hallam Mill: 'A weaver is paid $60 for a day's work. In that day she makes cloth that sells for $100, once the thread and the running of the loom are taken off. The $40 left over goes to the mill's owner, and the owner and the weavers want opposite things from it. This is not because the owner is cruel. ⟦Every owner has to keep a gap like it, because that is how the arrangement works: owners live from what workers make and are not paid for⟧. We write this for the weavers, and for everyone who works for wages.'

The pamphlet does one thing: it shows how an owner comes to gain from other people’s work. The weaver is paid $60 and makes cloth worth $100, so $40 is the part of her work she is not paid for. And it says this is true of every owner, however kind.

The explanation is the whole text. It does not say what to do about the mill. Someone who holds the explanation may go on to draw a plan from it, and then the text has a plan in it and gets another name.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the sum: $60 paid, $100 made, $40 left over.** The gap between pay and what the work earns is the owner’s gain.
2. **Look for “every owner”: “Every owner has to keep a gap like it”.** It says this is how the system works, not one owner’s greed.
3. **Check it asks for nothing: no tax, no handover and no party.** A plan in the text would give it another name.

This is **Marxism**, after the writer whose books set out the explanation. The text explains how owners gain, and asks for nothing.

You may also hear this called “Marxist theory”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Marxism**.

### 23. The question about the businesses

**The question:** **“What does it say about the businesses?”**

**Its answers**

- **“Owners keep them, but are taxed to pay for services”**
  - Give this answer when the text leaves the businesses with their owners, and asks the government to tax them, set a minimum wage, or pay for health care, schools and pensions.
  - Keeps **Social democracy**. Rules out **Class politics on its own**, **Democratic socialism**, **Marxism-Leninism**, **Anarchism**, **Market socialism** and **Marxism**.

  - Give this answer when the text says the businesses, or the biggest ones, should be owned by the government or by public bodies, for everyone.
  - Keeps **Democratic socialism** and **Marxism-Leninism**. Rules out **Social democracy**, **Class politics on its own**, **Anarchism**, **Market socialism** and **Marxism**.
- **“The workers in each one should own it”**

  - Give this answer when the text explains how the system works: owners keep part of what workers make without paying for it, or the fight between owners and workers drives history.

- **“The text does not say”**
  - Give this answer when the text says nothing about the businesses: no plan for who should own them, no taxes or services, and no explanation of how owners profit.

**Why it matters**

Most of these names differ first on the businesses: who should own them, and whether they compete. Taxes and public services change who gets what, not who owns what, so they are a different answer from handing the businesses over.

That is why **Social democracy** and **Democratic socialism** can both ask for health care and still get different answers: one changes who gets what, the other changes who owns what.

**How to answer it**

1. **Find the sentence about what should happen to the businesses.** If you can’t find the words, you don’t have an answer yet.
2. **A complaint about what the owners did, with no plan, is “The text does not say”.** Saying what is wrong is not saying what to do about the businesses.
3. **A tax or a floor for pay counts only if the owners keep the businesses.** If the text takes the businesses away, it is a handover.
4. **Look for a handover: the text must say a business should pass out of the owners’ hands.** Naming a business does not say who owns it afterwards.
5. **If a text asks for a tax and also a handover, give the handover.** That text is **Democratic socialism**, not **Social democracy**.
6. **If workers own the businesses and the firms also compete, give “The workers in each one should own it, and compete for customers”.** That text is **Market socialism**, not **Democratic socialism**.
7. **If a text explains how owners gain and then asks for a handover, give the handover.** A plan beats an explanation, so that text is **Democratic socialism**, not **Marxism**.
8. **Give “It explains how owners profit from workers” only when the text explains how owners gain and asks for nothing.** Taxing the owners, even heavily, is not an explanation.

- Class politics on its own or Democratic socialism: Does the text say who should own the businesses? Or does it only say whose side it is on?
- Class politics on its own or Market socialism: Does the text say who should own each business, and that the businesses should compete?
- Class politics on its own or Marxism: Is the text about this owner’s choices? Or does it explain why any owner would gain from the work?
- Democratic socialism or Market socialism: After the handover, do the businesses compete with each other for customers, set their own prices and risk failing?
- Democratic socialism or Marxism: Does the text say what should happen to the businesses, or does it only explain how owners gain?

- Anarchism or Market socialism: Once the workers own it, does the business compete with others for customers and risk failing? Or does the text say nothing about that, and want the government gone?

**Why this is Marxism-Leninism and not Marxism**

A text that explains and says nothing about power is **Marxism**. A text that explains and then says a party will take power and keep it is **Marxism-Leninism**.

What the text goes on to say decides it.

**Does it look like a story you know?**

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What does it say about the businesses?
- Owners keep them, but are taxed to pay for services → Social democracy
- The government should take them over → Democratic socialism · Marxism-Leninism
- The workers in each one should own it → Democratic socialism · Marxism-Leninism · Anarchism

What does it want done with the government?
- Take power for the workers, and allow no other party → Marxism-Leninism
- Keep it, and run it by winning elections → Social democracy · Class politics on its own · Democratic socialism · Market socialism · Marxism
- Get rid of it, and run things together → Anarchism
- The text does not say → Social democracy · Class politics on its own · Democratic socialism · Market socialism · Marxism

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Social democracy**: workers set against owners, the owners keeping their businesses, and the government taxing them to pay for schools and health care, or setting a minimum wage.
- **Class politics on its own**: workers set against owners, and nothing more: no plan for the businesses, no taxes, no account of how owners profit, no seizing power, no ending the government.
- **Democratic socialism**: workers set against owners, the businesses handed to the public or their workers by vote or without saying how, and no seizing power or ending the government.
- **Marxism-Leninism**: workers set against owners, and a party, or the workers themselves, taking power by force or ruling as the only party, with no election that could vote them out.
- **Anarchism**: workers set against owners, and the government gotten rid of right away, not used first, with people running their workplaces and towns together without it.
- **Market socialism**: workers set against owners, each business owned by its workers, and those businesses competing for customers, setting their own prices and able to go under.
- **Marxism**: owners and workers, and how the system works, like owners keeping part of what workers make without paying for it, but no plan for the businesses or for taking power.

**To carry away**

- Ask the two questions in order, and find the words for each. If a question has no words, the answer is **“The text does not say”**, and that is a real answer.
- Taking the workers’ side is only the start. Seven names fit it, and the two questions decide which.
- A tax or a floor for pay leaves the owners in place. A handover does not. If a text shows both, the handover decides. If a text explains and also asks for something, what it asks for decides.
- A text that says nothing about the businesses can still be **Marxism-Leninism** or **Anarchism**, by what it says about power. A text that only explains can be **Marxism**, **Marxism-Leninism** or **Anarchism** the same way. A text silent on both is **Class politics on its own**.
- Never fill a silence with a guess. A text that does not say is not a secret plan.
