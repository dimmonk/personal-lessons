# Basic Math: Two steps, mixed, and the test

Revision 1, live. Part: Two steps, mixed, and the test. Where the subject ends up: Work out the number a situation needs (a total, a price per one, a scaled amount, a percent or the price before one, how much paint or soil to buy, how high a ladder reaches, what savings grow to or a loan costs) and tell whether it is sensible, when a receipt, a recipe, a sale tag, a room or an offer hands you some numbers and a question and nobody names the method.

## The why

In real life nobody says which kind of problem it is, and the number you want often needs another number first: the cans before the cost, the gallons before the gas money.
Here you put the whole way of working together, then take the test: fifteen new problems of every kind.

## Step 1: What do you need first?

Every problem, the same five steps: say what is asked and what is given; a rough answer from friendly numbers; name the kind and draw it; work it exactly on the calculator; look back at the rough answer, the unit and the direction.
When the numbers given do not lead straight to the answer, find what you need first. The cost of paint needs the number of cans, and the cans need the area of the walls. Find that first number, then the answer.
Look back at both: the cans are rounded up, and the cost is near cans × price.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “The cost is wanted, so multiply the area by the price.”
The right line: Paint is sold by the can. Find the cans first, then the cost.

## Step 2: A worked example

A plain table, "Painting a room":

> Room, walls 8 ft high  |  14 ft by 10 ft
> Door  |  3 ft by 7 ft
> Coats  |  2
> Paint, a gallon can, covers 350 square feet  |  $37.98
> Sales tax  |  7%

The working, one step at a time:
1. Say what is asked and what is given. Asked: what the paint costs, with tax. Given: the room, the door, two coats, what a can covers and costs, and the tax. That needs the number of cans first.
1. Make a rough answer in your head. Walls about 50 ft around × 8 high, less about 20: 380 square feet; two coats ÷ 350 is about 2.2, so 3 cans. 3 × about $38 = $114; the tax adds a bit, about a tenth: about $125.
1. Find what you need first: the cans. Around the room: 14 + 10 + 14 + 10 = 48 ft, × 8 = 384, − 21 for the door = 363 square feet. Two coats: 363 × 2 ÷ 350 = 2.07, so 3 cans: always round up.
1. Work it out exactly on the calculator, then look back. On the calculator: 3 × 37.98 × 1.07 = shows $121.92. Look back: near the rough $125.

Then the answer: $125 / 3 cans / $121.92. 363 square feet, two coats, is 2.07 cans, so 3; 3 × $37.98 × 1.07 = $121.92.

## The check

15 new questions, no help. No answer is shown until the end. The questions come in mixed order. It comes back once, on new questions, 14 days after the first time.

To pass:
- At least 12 right
- At least 2 right among two steps (right among the three two-step problems)
- None gave an answer more than double or less than half the right one
