# Strategy: Where to play and how to win

Revision 1, draft (the screen carries the draft line). Part: Where to play and how to win. Where the subject ends up: After this you can look at a business decision (expand, add a product, chase a big customer, answer a rival, invest) and tell whether it is a strategy, say where the business makes its money and why, and pick the move that keeps it different in a way rivals cannot copy, when you face that decision in your own business.

## The why

Owners’ plans often fail without a single wrong move in them. They fail because they never choose: every customer, every idea, every fight at once.
Strategists read a plan for two choices made together: where to play, and how to win there. Here you learn to find them, and to name the trap when they are missing.

## Step 1: One choice, in two parts

Where to play: which customers, and where. How to win: why those customers will pick you over the rivals they already have.
The two are one choice. A place to play is good only if you have a way to win there; a way to win is good only for the customers who value it.
A plan that makes no such choice falls into one of six traps.
Do it all: many priorities, none dropped. Head-on: going first at the strongest rival, on the ground where it is strongest. Many fronts: taking on several rivals, in several places, at once.
Something for everyone: every kind of customer at once. A dream: a mission that never says which customers or why they would choose you. Copycat: what every business in the trade already does, done again or done better.
Why it matters: money and hours spent on everything at once are spent on nothing in particular, and the customers you could have won go to a rival who chose them.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “I don’t want to choose yet. Keep every door open.”
The right line: Keeping every door open is the choice not to win anywhere. Pick the customers you can win and how, and say what you will not chase.

## Step 2: Two plans for one shop

| A | B |
|---|---|
| “We play for builders in the county. We win with advice, odd parts the big store does not carry, and doors open at 6 a.m.” | “We aim to be the best hardware store in the state, with great service and great prices for everyone.” |

Compare: Only the first says who and why. The second is a dream: no customer named, no reason to switch.

## Step 3: A worked example

Your own note, written after an argument: "Plan 1 for Grace’s salon", A draft.

> Serve every client in town: walk-in cuts, children, men’s barbering, color, nails and brows.
> Prices from $15 to $200, so nobody is turned away.
> Open seven days, early and late.

Your own note, written after an argument: "Plan 2 for Grace’s salon", A draft.

> We play for women in our part of town who color their hair and plan it months ahead.
> We win with colorists who do nothing but color, and long appointments the chain salons cannot fit in.
> We stop walk-in cuts and nails, which fill the chairs those appointments need.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**Which plan makes a real choice of where to play and how to win?**
- Plan 1
- Plan 2

The working, one step at a time:
1. Find where each plan plays: which customers, where.: Plan 1: every client in town. Plan 2: women nearby who color their hair and plan ahead.
1. Find how each wins there, and whether the two fit.: Plan 1 gives no reason anyone would pick Grace over the salon they use now. Plan 2 wins with color specialists and long appointments, which fit those clients, and drops what gets in the way.
1. Name the trap of the plan that does not choose.: Plan 1 reaches for every kind of client at once: something for everyone.

Then the answer: Plan 2 / Something for everyone: every kind of customer at once. Plan 2 chooses: one kind of client, a way to win them that fits, and what it stops doing. Plan 1 reaches for every kind of client at once, so it never says why any of them would pick Grace: something for everyone.
