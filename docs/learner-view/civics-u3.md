## Part 1 of 2: Laws, and money

### 1. Congress is in the news: what did it do?

*Unit Three · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

After this unit you can read a short news item or an everyday story in which Congress does something, and say which of five things it is: a law on a matter the Constitution gives Congress; a law the Constitution does not let Congress pass; Congress deciding what the government may spend; the Senate approving a person the President chose, or a deal with another country; or charging an official, and trying the charge. You will be able to point to the words that tell you, and to say why it is not one of the other four.

You already read this sort of story every week. “Congress passed a new law on airline tickets.” “Congress cut the money for the program.” “The Senate approved the President’s choice.” “The House voted to charge a judge.” “A court says Congress went too far.” Each says that Congress did something, and each is a different thing.

When the answer to the first question is Congress, one question is left, and this unit teaches it: what does Congress do? It passes laws, but a law that passed every vote is not always one Congress was allowed to pass. The Constitution, the founding set of rules for the country, lists the matters Congress may make laws about, and it protects some rights that no law may take away. Congress also decides how much money the government may spend, the Senate votes on people and agreements the President puts forward, and the two chambers can charge an official with serious misconduct.

Two words are used all the way through. A bill is a proposed law, and it becomes a law when both chambers have passed it and the President has signed it. The chambers are the House of Representatives and the Senate, the two groups of lawmakers that make up Congress.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“Who makes the last decision in the case, or is asked to make it?”** Its answers:

- **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**: give this answer when the last decision in the case is a vote in the House, the Senate or both, or the case ends by asking Congress for one: on a law, on money, on a person the President chose or an agreement the President signed with another country, or on charging or trying an official. A law Congress passed is still Congress’s decision when the case adds that the President signed it. **This unit is about these cases.**
- **“The President or a federal agency”**: give this answer when the last decision in the case is made by the President or a federal agency, or the case ends by asking them for one: an agency writes rules, inspects or enforces, the President gives an order, commands the armed forces, deals with another country, refuses to sign a law or forgives a federal crime.
- **“A judge, in any court”**: give this answer when the last decision in the case is a judge’s, in any court, federal or state, or the case ends with someone asking a judge to decide.
- **“A state, city or county government”**: give this answer when the last decision in the case is made by a state’s lawmakers, its governor or its agencies, or by a city, town or county, or the case ends by asking one of them for it. A judge in a state’s court counts as a judge, not as the state.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a case, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. A law on a matter the Constitution gives Congress

*Unit Three · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-enumerated`]

The answer to the first question is **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**. Now ask what Congress does. Start with the commonest thing: passing a law the Constitution lets it pass.

*The airline-ticket tax*

> Airports across the country need repairs, and the people who fly are a fair group to pay for them. The House passed ⟦a bill that puts a new tax of ten dollars on every airline ticket⟧, and on Wednesday the Senate passed it too.

- Congress passes a law: the House and the Senate have both voted for the bill.
- The law is about a tax: ten dollars on every airline ticket.
- Taxes are one of the matters the Constitution lists for Congress.
- Nothing in the law takes away anyone’s right to speak, to worship, to publish or to gather.
- The need for airport repairs is only the reason the bill exists.

What you are shown is a law, passed by both chambers, about a tax. The airport repairs tell you why the bill exists. What Congress did is pass a law, and what the law is about is a tax. A law is not allowed just because both chambers voted for it. The Constitution lists the matters Congress may make laws about, and what is not on the list is for the states to decide. Taxing is on the list. This is the answer when Congress passes a law on a matter the Constitution lists among its powers (taxes, borrowing, trade between the states or with other countries, the rules for becoming a citizen, money and coins, the mail, war and the armed forces, the federal courts), and the law takes away no right the Constitution protects.

There is a second limit. The Constitution also protects some rights: to speak, to worship, to publish and to gather peacefully. Congress may not pass a law that takes one of them away, even on a listed matter. The tax on tickets takes none of them away, so Congress was allowed to pass it.

**What you must be able to point to.** A law Congress passes, a matter the Constitution lists among Congress’s powers that the law is about, and no right the Constitution protects that the law takes away.

The name for this is **Enumerated power**. “Enumerated” is an old word for “counted off, one by one”: the Constitution counts off Congress’s powers in a list.

You may also hear this called “a listed power” or “an expressed power”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Enumerated power**.

### 4. A law the Constitution does not let Congress pass

*Unit Three · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-beyondcong`]

The second of the five also begins with Congress passing a law, with every vote in order. This time the Constitution does not let Congress pass it.

*The book list*

> Many parents say that students across the country read too little. The House and the Senate passed ⟦a bill that tells every school in every state which ten books its students must read in ninth grade⟧.

- Congress passes a law: the House and the Senate have both voted for the bill.
- The law is about what students read in school: ten named books, in every state.
- What schools teach is not on the Constitution’s list for Congress, so it is for the states to decide.
- No right is involved here: the only problem is the matter.

Nothing about the vote is wrong. If the vote were all that mattered, this law would have the same name as the tax on airline tickets. The difference is the matter. Schooling is not on the Constitution’s list, so each state decides what its own schools teach, and Congress has no power over it, however the votes go.

A law can also fail the other way: it is on a listed matter but takes away a right the Constitution protects, such as the right to speak, to worship, to publish or to gather. Either failure is enough. A law that fails either way can pass both chambers and be signed by the President, and still be one Congress was never allowed to pass.

**What you must be able to point to.** A law Congress passes, and either a matter the Constitution does not list among Congress’s powers, or a right the Constitution protects that the law takes away.

The name for this is **Beyond Congress’s power**. “Beyond” says that the law lies outside the edge of what Congress is allowed to do. It is the opposite of the last name.

You may also hear this called “exceeding Congress’s powers”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Beyond Congress’s power**.

### 6. One matter, the mail: with a right taken away and without

They are easy to mix up, because in both Congress passes a law with every vote in order. Here are two laws about the same matter.

**Case A**

> Rural towns say it costs too much to send a parcel by post. The House and the Senate passed a bill that sets one low price for any parcel under five pounds sent through the post office.

**Case B**

> Some lawmakers dislike a magazine that prints articles against the Senate. The House and the Senate passed a bill that says the post office may not carry any magazine that prints articles against the Senate.

**What to compare.** Both cases are about the post office, and the mail is one of the matters on the Constitution’s list. Compare one thing: whether the law takes away anyone’s right.

**You are asked:** Which case gives the answer **“Passes a law the Constitution does not let it pass”**? (Case A / Case B)

**Shown as soon as you answer.** Case B.

**Why this one and not the other**

In Case A the matter is the mail, which is on the list, and the law only sets a price for sending a parcel. It takes no right away from anyone. The answer is **“Passes a law on a matter the Constitution lists for it”**, and the case is **Enumerated power**.

In Case B the matter is the mail too, but the law bars a magazine from the post because of what the magazine prints, and the Constitution protects the right to publish. The answer is **“Passes a law the Constitution does not let it pass”**, and the case is **Beyond Congress’s power**.

Being on the list is only half of what a law needs: it must also take no right away.

**How to tell them apart**

Is the matter the law is about on the Constitution’s list for Congress? And does the law take away anyone’s right to speak, to worship, to publish or to gather peacefully?

### 7. Congress deciding what the government may spend

*Unit Three · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-purse`]

So far Congress has passed laws. The third thing it does is not about what people may do. It is about what the government may spend.

*The flood barrier*

> A river town floods almost every spring. The President announced a flood barrier for the town, and the federal engineers have their plans ready. But ⟦the spending bill the House and the Senate passed this year has no money for the barrier⟧, so no work can start.

- There is a program on paper: the President announced a flood barrier, and the engineers have their plans.
- There is a decision in Congress about whether the government may spend money on it: the bill that settles this year’s spending.
- The bill leaves the money out.
- Nobody has forbidden the barrier. There is simply no money to build it.

Everyone in this case wants the barrier built, and nobody can build it. What stops it is the bill that decides what the government may spend this year: it has no money for the barrier. The government can spend only what Congress has voted, so Congress did not need to pass a law against the barrier. Leaving the money out was enough.

Voting the money, cutting an amount voted before and leaving it out are three ways of deciding the same thing. This is the answer when Congress decides whether the government may spend money on something: it votes the money, cuts it or leaves it out, and the government can spend only what Congress has voted.

**What you must be able to point to.** A decision by Congress to vote, cut or leave out the money for something, and the government able to spend on it only as Congress decided.

The name for this is **The power of the purse**. A purse is where a person keeps their money, and the name says that Congress holds the government’s: whoever holds the purse decides what can be bought.

You may also hear this called “appropriations” or “the spending bill”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **The power of the purse**.

### 10. A formal agreement between two countries

*Unit Three · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 10 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-treaty`]

The fourth thing Congress does is about a person or an agreement that the President brings to the Senate. The case shows what the agreement is before the word is given.

*The river agreement*

> The United States and the country to its north both draw water from the same river. After two years of talks, the two governments wrote down in one formal document how much water each country may take in a dry summer, and what each will do if the river runs low. The President signed it for the United States, and the other country’s leader signed it for theirs.

The two governments did not leave a possible quarrel over a dry summer to chance, or to what each side remembered later. They wrote down, in one formal document, what each country will do, and each side signed it.

What sets this apart from a deal between two companies is that the two sides are countries, and the people who sign speak for the whole country. Here the President signed for the United States.

**The word for this.** *Treaty*: a formal written agreement between the United States and another country.


### 11. The Senate approving a person the President chose, or a deal with another country

*Unit Three · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 11 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-confirm`]

The fourth thing Congress does starts with the President: the President chooses someone, or signs an agreement, and then the Senate has to say yes. A person comes first here, and an agreement after.

*The head of the parks*

> The President chose a new head for the federal agency that runs the national parks. ⟦The Senate held two days of hearings, and on Thursday it voted 61 to 38 to approve her⟧.

- The President has chosen someone for a top government job: the head of the *agency* that runs the national parks.
- She cannot simply start. The Senate has to vote on her, and it holds hearings first.
- The vote is the Senate’s alone, and more than half of the senators said yes.
- The House has no part in it.

The President’s choice is only a choice until the Senate votes. Until the vote is yes she is only the person the President has proposed, and not yet the head of anything. The jobs this covers are the top ones: a judge, the head of a federal *agency*, someone to represent the country abroad. This is the answer when the Senate votes on someone the President has chosen for a top government job (a judge, the head of a federal agency, someone to represent the country abroad), where more than half the senators must vote yes, or on a treaty the President has signed, where two-thirds of the senators present must vote yes.

The same line covers a *treaty*: the Senate votes on one that the President has signed. The number of yes votes needed is not the same for the two, and the line above says what each is.

**What you must be able to point to.** A person the President has chosen for a top government job, or a treaty the President has signed, and the Senate voting on whether to approve it.

The name for this is **Advice and consent**. It is made from words in the Constitution. Its second word, “consent”, is the one that matters: it means yes, and the President’s choice takes effect only with it.

You may also hear this called “Senate confirmation” or “ratifying a treaty”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Advice and consent**.

### 13. Charging an official, and trying the charge

*Unit Three · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 13 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-impeach`]

The last name was a Senate vote on someone who does not yet have the job. The fifth thing Congress does is also about a person who works for the government of the whole country. But this person already has the job, and is accused of serious misconduct.

*The judge who took money*

> A federal judge is accused of taking money to decide cases in favor of one company. ⟦The House voted, by more than half, to charge the judge with taking the money⟧. ⟦The Senate then held a trial, and 70 of the 100 senators voted to convict⟧. The judge was removed from the job.

- A federal judge, who holds the job, is accused of serious misconduct: taking money to decide cases.
- Two chambers act, in two steps. The House votes first, by more than half, to charge the judge.
- Then the Senate holds a trial, and two-thirds of the senators present must vote to convict.
- Only after the Senate’s vote is the judge removed from the job.

This is a trial, but not in a court. The House brings the charge, as an accuser does in a court, and the Senate holds the trial. A conviction here sends nobody to prison. It only removes the official from the job, and if the official also broke a criminal law, the ordinary courts deal with that separately. This is the answer when a federal official (a judge, the head of a department, even the President) is accused of serious misconduct, and the House votes, by more than half, to charge them, or the Senate holds the trial on that charge, where two-thirds of the senators present must vote to convict before the official is removed. It can happen to a federal judge, to the head of a department, and even to the President.

There are two separate steps. When the House votes to charge an official, people say the official has been “impeached”. That is the first step only, and it does not remove anyone. Only a conviction by the Senate does. The answer covers both steps: the House voting to charge, and the Senate trying the charge.

**What you must be able to point to.** A federal official accused of serious misconduct, and Congress acting to remove them: the House voting to charge them, or the Senate trying the charge.

The name for this is **Impeachment**. It is the word for the whole process of charging an official and trying the charge. The name does not say “removed”: the process can end with the official staying in the job.

You may also hear this called “removal from office”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Impeachment**.

### 16. The question you have been answering all along

*Unit Three · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 16 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-congress`]

This card puts the question and its five answers in one place, and says why it is asked.

**The question:** **“What does Congress do in the case?”**

**What it is for.** Tells apart five things Congress does: a law it has the power to pass, a law it does not, a decision about money, a vote on someone or something the President put forward, and a charge against an official.

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other four.

- **“Passes a law on a matter the Constitution lists for it”**
  - Give this answer when Congress passes a law on a matter the Constitution lists among its powers (taxes, borrowing, trade between the states or with other countries, the rules for becoming a citizen, money and coins, the mail, war and the armed forces, the federal courts), and the law takes away no right the Constitution protects.
  - It leads to **Enumerated power**.
- **“Passes a law the Constitution does not let it pass”**
  - Give this answer when Congress passes a law on a matter the Constitution does not list among its powers (the hours barbers work, who may marry, what schools teach), so the matter is left to the states, or a law that takes away a right the Constitution protects (to speak, to worship, to publish, to gather peacefully).
  - It leads to **Beyond Congress’s power**.
- **“Votes money for something, or refuses it”**
  - Give this answer when Congress decides whether the government may spend money on something: it votes the money, cuts it or leaves it out, and the government can spend only what Congress has voted.
  - It leads to **The power of the purse**.
- **“Votes on a person or a treaty the President put forward”**
  - Give this answer when the Senate votes on someone the President has chosen for a top government job (a judge, the head of a federal agency, someone to represent the country abroad), where more than half the senators must vote yes, or on a treaty the President has signed, where two-thirds of the senators present must vote yes.
  - It leads to **Advice and consent**.
- **“Charges an official with serious misconduct, or tries the charge”**
  - Give this answer when a federal official (a judge, the head of a department, even the President) is accused of serious misconduct, and the House votes, by more than half, to charge them, or the Senate holds the trial on that charge, where two-thirds of the senators present must vote to convict before the official is removed.
  - It leads to **Impeachment**.

**Why it decides**

The five names are defined by what Congress does, and for a law by whether the Constitution lets Congress pass it. Passing a law by the right votes does not make it one the Constitution allows, so a law is never named by the vote alone.

Two cases that sound alike can get different names, and two that sound different can get the same one. A tax on tickets and a one-dollar coin would be the same name. A tax on bottled water and a grant for clinics are different names, although both are about the same clinics. The question is about what Congress does, and nothing about the topic, the vote or the people can stand in for that.

**How to answer it from a case**

Find the sentence that shows what Congress does: the bill that passed, the money voted or left out, the Senate’s vote on a person or an agreement, or the House charging an official. Then ask which of the five answers describes it. You should be able to put your finger on the words.

For a law there are two tests: is the matter on the Constitution’s list, and does the law take a right away? On the list with no right taken away is **“Passes a law on a matter the Constitution lists for it”**; off the list, or a right taken away, is **“Passes a law the Constitution does not let it pass”**. For the other three: money the government may spend is **“Votes money for something, or refuses it”**; a person or a *treaty* the President put forward is **“Votes on a person or a treaty the President put forward”**; a charge against an official, or the trial of the charge, is **“Charges an official with serious misconduct, or tries the charge”**.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Sometimes two answers both seem to fit. Each pair below has been set side by side earlier in this unit, and each has one question that separates it. One more case has a rule of its own: a bill that spends money is also a law, so it can show both a law on a listed matter and a decision about money. When it does, the answer is **“Votes money for something, or refuses it”**.

- Enumerated power or Beyond Congress’s power: Is the matter the law is about on the Constitution’s list for Congress? And does the law take away anyone’s right to speak, to worship, to publish or to gather peacefully?
- Enumerated power or The power of the purse: Is Congress raising money, or setting some other rule? Or is it deciding whether the government may spend money on something: voting it, cutting it or leaving it out? When a case shows both **“Passes a law on a matter the Constitution lists for it”** and a decision about whether the government may spend money on something, the answer is **“Votes money for something, or refuses it”**.
- Advice and consent or Impeachment: Has the person already got the job, and is the vote about something they are accused of doing? Or has the President only just put the person forward for the job?

### 18. A whole case, where the story points the wrong way

*Unit Three · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 18 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `worked`, id `worked-mint`]

Before you run a case yourself, watch one being run from the top, in the order the questions are asked. In this case the most noticeable thing in the story is not the thing that decides it. You are not asked anything until the end.

*The coin presses*

> The mint says its presses are worn out and cannot make enough coins. The House and the Senate passed a bill that gives the mint $120 million to buy new presses. The President is expected to sign it.

**Question 1 of 2: Who makes the last decision in the case, or is asked to make it?**

What it is for: sorts a case by who makes the decision it ends on: the lawmakers of the whole country, the President and the federal agencies, a judge, or the government of a state, city or county. Each of the four may do different things and is held back by different limits, so the question that comes next depends on whose decision it is. Many cases mention more than one of them. What comes before the last decision is how the matter reached it; the last decision, or the one someone asks for, is what the case is about.

> The mint says its presses are worn out and cannot make enough coins. ⟦The House and the Senate passed a bill⟧ that gives the mint $120 million to buy new presses. The President is expected to sign it.

Answer: **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**

The case ends on a vote by lawmakers of the whole country: “The House and the Senate passed a bill”. The President is expected to sign it, but a signature does not change whose decision it was, so the answer to its first question is **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**.

Still possible: all five names this unit teaches.

**Question 2 of 2: What does Congress do in the case?**

What it is for: tells apart five things Congress does: a law it has the power to pass, a law it does not, a decision about money, a vote on someone or something the President put forward, and a charge against an official. The five names are defined by what Congress does, and for a law by whether the Constitution lets Congress pass it. Passing a law by the right votes does not make it one the Constitution allows, so a law is never named by the vote alone.

> The mint says its presses are worn out and cannot make enough coins. The House and the Senate passed a bill that ⟦gives the mint $120 million to buy new presses⟧. The President is expected to sign it.

Answer: **“Votes money for something, or refuses it”**

Congress passed a bill, and what the bill does is give money: “gives the mint $120 million to buy new presses”. The government can spend only what Congress has voted, so Congress is deciding whether the government may spend. The bill is also about coins, and money and coins are on the Constitution’s list, so the case shows both a law on a listed matter and a decision about money. When a case shows both, the answer is **“Votes money for something, or refuses it”**.

Still possible: **The power of the purse**. Ruled out: **Enumerated power**, **Beyond Congress’s power**, **Advice and consent** and **Impeachment**.

**Name it:** **The power of the purse**

**You are asked:** The bill is about coins, which are on the Constitution’s list, so the case can look like **Enumerated power**. Why is this **The power of the purse** and not **Enumerated power**? Every statement below is true of the case. Before you read the reason, choose the one that settles it.

- (a) The bill is about the mint and its coins.
- (b) The bill gives $120 million, and the government can spend only what Congress has voted.
- (c) The President is expected to sign the bill.

**Shown as soon as you choose.** The one that settles it is (b): The bill gives $120 million, and the government can spend only what Congress has voted.
- If you chose (a): True, and it is why the case can look like **Enumerated power**. But the bill’s subject fits both names, so it cannot settle which of the two this is.
- If you chose (c): True, but that is how the matter goes on after Congress has decided. It does not change what Congress did.

**Why this is The power of the purse and not Enumerated power**

A bill about a listed matter is a law on that matter, and on its own that would point to **Enumerated power**. But the case shows more: Congress is deciding whether the government may spend $120 million. A bill that spends is a law, so it always shows both. When a case shows both, the answer is **“Votes money for something, or refuses it”**.

**Enumerated power** is for a law that does something else on a listed matter, such as setting a price or a tax, and spends no money.

**Does it look like a case you know?**

Now the second look: does this case look like one you know? A bill about money and the mint may bring back the airline-ticket tax first, and that case was **Enumerated power**. So here the likeness and the questions seem to disagree.

When that happens, go back to the question and find the words in the case that answer it. They are “gives the mint $120 million to buy new presses”. The ticket tax raised money and spent nothing. This one gives money to buy presses. So the case this one really looks like is the flood barrier: the whole case turns on whether money may be spent, and the answer stands.

### 19. What to carry away

*Unit Three · rev 4 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 19 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap-congress`]

This card puts the unit in one place.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What does Congress do in the case?
- Passes a law on a matter the Constitution lists for it → Enumerated power
- Passes a law the Constitution does not let it pass → Beyond Congress’s power
- Votes money for something, or refuses it → The power of the purse
- Votes on a person or a treaty the President put forward → Advice and consent
- Charges an official with serious misconduct, or tries the charge → Impeachment

**For each name: what you must be able to point to, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Enumerated power**: a law Congress passes, a matter the Constitution lists among Congress’s powers that the law is about, and no right the Constitution protects that the law takes away.
- **Beyond Congress’s power**: a law Congress passes, and either a matter the Constitution does not list among Congress’s powers, or a right the Constitution protects that the law takes away.
- **The power of the purse**: a decision by Congress to vote, cut or leave out the money for something, and the government able to spend on it only as Congress decided.
- **Advice and consent**: a person the President has chosen for a top government job, or a treaty the President has signed, and the Senate voting on whether to approve it.
- **Impeachment**: a federal official accused of serious misconduct, and Congress acting to remove them: the House voting to charge them, or the Senate trying the charge.

**To carry away**

- Say what Congress does, and point to the words in the case that show it. If you cannot point, you do not have an answer yet. The story never decides, and neither does the vote: every law has been through both chambers, and a law can pass every vote and still be **Beyond Congress’s power**.
- For a law there are two tests: is the matter on the Constitution’s list, and does the law take a right away?
- A bill that spends money is a law too. When a case shows both, the answer is the money, **The power of the purse**.
- Two Senate votes can look alike. One is on a person or a *treaty* that the President put forward, **Advice and consent**. The other is on a charge against someone who already holds the job, **Impeachment**. And **Impeachment** covers the charge as well as the trial, so a story that stops at the vote in the House has not told you the official is gone.
