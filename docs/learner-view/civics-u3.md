## Part 1 of 2: Laws, and money

### 1. Congress is in the news: what did it do?

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 1 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `orient`, id `orient`]

When the news says Congress did something, check what it actually did. A new law, a cut to funding, a vote on the President’s pick and a charge against a judge are five different things, and each works by different rules.

You read headlines like these every week. “Congress passed a new tax on airline tickets.” “Congress cut the money for the program.” “The Senate approved the President’s pick.” “The House voted to charge a judge.” Each says Congress did something, and each is a different thing.

The Constitution, the country’s founding rulebook, lists the jobs Congress may make laws about. It also protects some rights that no law may take away. So even a law that passed every vote can be one Congress was never allowed to pass.

Two words come up all through this unit. A bill is a proposed law. It becomes a law when the House and the Senate have both passed it and the President has signed it.

**What Unit One taught, in one place.** The first question is **“Who makes the final call?”** Its answers:

- **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**: give this answer when the final call is a vote in the House or Senate, or someone asks Congress for one, such as on a law or money. A law stays Congress’s call even once the President signs it. **This unit is about these stories.**
- **“The President or a federal agency”**: give this answer when the final call is the President’s or a federal agency’s, or someone is asking them for one, such as an agency enforcing a law or the President refusing to sign one.
- **“A judge, in any court”**: give this answer when the final call is a judge’s, in any court, federal or state, or someone is asking a judge to decide.
- **“A state, city or county government”**: give this answer when the final call is made by a state’s lawmakers, governor or agencies, or a city, town or county, or someone asks them for it. A state court judge counts as a judge.

Two things are marked separately: the name you give a story, and your answers to the questions on the way to it.

### 2. Within Congress’s power

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 2 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-enumerated`]

Start with the most common thing Congress does: passing a law it is allowed to pass.

*The airline-ticket tax*

> Airports across the country need repairs, and the people who fly are a fair group to pay for them. The House passed ⟦a bill that puts a new tax of ten dollars on every airline ticket⟧, and on Wednesday the Senate passed it too.

Congress passed a law, and the law is a tax. Taxes are one of the jobs the Constitution gives Congress, so Congress was allowed to make it. The House and the Senate both voting for it is not what made it allowed.

Two checks decide it. First, is the subject on the Constitution’s list: taxes, borrowing, trade, the mail, money and coins, the armed forces, the rules for becoming a citizen, the federal courts? Second, does the law take away a right the Constitution protects, such as the right to speak, to worship, to publish or to gather peacefully?

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the law itself: a ten-dollar tax on every airline ticket.** The need for airport repairs only explains why the bill exists.
2. **Check the subject is on the Constitution’s list: it is a tax.** Anything off the list is for each state to decide.
3. **Check no right is taken away: nobody loses the right to speak, worship, publish or gather.** A law on a listed subject can still be forbidden if it takes one away.

This is **Within Congress’s power**. It is allowed because of its subject and the rights it leaves alone, not because of the votes.

You may also hear this called “an enumerated power” or “a listed power” or “an expressed power”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Within Congress’s power**.

### 4. Beyond Congress’s power

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 4 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-beyondcong`]

The same start, a different ending: Congress passes a law with every vote in order, and the Constitution still does not allow it.

*The book list*

> Many parents say that students across the country read too little. The House and the Senate passed ⟦a bill that tells every school in every state which ten books its students must read in ninth grade⟧.

Nothing is wrong with the votes. What is wrong is the subject: what schools teach is not on the Constitution’s list, so each state decides for its own schools. Congress has no say, however the votes go.

A law can fail the other way too. Its subject is on the list, but it takes away a right the Constitution protects: to speak, to worship, to publish or to gather peacefully. Either problem is enough, even if the President signs the bill.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the law itself: ten named books for every school in every state.** A law that passed every vote can still be one Congress was not allowed to pass.
2. **Ask what it is about: what schools teach.** That subject is not on the Constitution’s list, so each state decides.
3. **Ask whether it takes away a right: here, nobody’s speech, worship, publishing or gathering is touched.** A law that takes one away fails even on a listed subject.
4. **Stop at the first check that fails: here, the subject.** One failed check is enough.

This is **Beyond Congress’s power**. It passed every vote and was still not allowed.

You may also hear this called “exceeding Congress’s powers”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Beyond Congress’s power**.

### 7. The power of the purse

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 1 of 2 · Card 7 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-purse`]

So far Congress has passed laws. The third thing it does is not about what people may do. It is about what the government may spend.

*The flood barrier*

> A river town floods almost every spring. The President announced a flood barrier for the town, and the federal engineers have their plans ready. But ⟦the spending bill the House and the Senate passed this year has no money for the barrier⟧, so no work can start.

Everyone in this story wants the barrier built, and nobody can build it. The bill that sets this year’s spending has no money for it, and the government can spend only what Congress has voted.

So Congress never had to pass a law against the barrier. Leaving the money out was enough. Voting money, cutting an amount and leaving it out are all the same power: deciding what the government may spend.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the plan that needs money: the flood barrier the President announced.** An announcement pays for nothing.
2. **Find this year’s budget bill, passed by the House and the Senate.** It is where Congress decides what the government may spend.
3. **Check what the bill does about the plan: it leaves the money out.** Voting money, cutting it or leaving it out all settle the question.

This is **The power of the purse**. A purse is where a person keeps their money, and whoever holds it decides what can be bought.

You may also hear this called “appropriations” or “the spending bill”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **The power of the purse**.

## Part 2 of 2: Votes on people, then the drill

### 10. A formal agreement between two countries

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 10 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `term`, id `term-treaty`]

The fourth thing Congress does can involve an agreement with another country. Here is what one looks like, before the word for it.

*The river agreement*

> The United States and the country to its north both draw water from the same river. After two years of talks, the two governments wrote down in one formal document how much water each country may take in a dry summer, and what each will do if the river runs low. The President signed it for the United States, and the other country’s leader signed it for theirs.

They wrote it all down, so that nobody has to trust their memory when the river runs low, and each side signed.

What sets this apart from a deal between two companies is that the two sides are countries. The people who sign speak for the whole country: here the President signed for the United States.

**The word for this.** *Treaty*: a signed, written agreement between the United States and another country.


### 11. Senate approval

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 11 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-confirm`]

The fourth thing Congress does starts with the President. The President picks someone, or signs an agreement, and then the Senate has to say yes.

*The head of the parks*

> The President chose a new head for the federal agency that runs the national parks. ⟦The Senate held two days of hearings, and on Thursday it voted 61 to 38 to approve her⟧.

The President’s pick is only a pick until the Senate votes. Until the vote is yes, she is just the person the President proposed, not the head of anything. The House has no part in it.

This covers the top jobs: a judge, the head of a federal *agency*, an ambassador. It also covers a *treaty* the President has signed. A person needs more than half of the senators to say yes. A *treaty* needs two-thirds of the senators present.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find who made the pick: the President chose the head of the parks *agency*.** The Senate votes only on what the President put forward.
2. **Find the Senate’s vote: two days of hearings, then 61 to 38.** This vote belongs to the Senate alone.
3. **Check she does not hold the job yet: she is “the President’s choice”, not the head.** A vote on someone already in the job is about something else, covered soon.
4. **Look for a *treaty* the same way: the river agreement would need the Senate’s yes too.** It follows the same order, but needs two-thirds of the senators present.

This is **Senate approval**. The Senate’s yes is what lets the President’s pick take effect.

You may also hear this called “advice and consent” or “Senate confirmation” or “ratifying a treaty”. Those words mean the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Senate approval**.

### 13. Impeachment

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 13 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `meet`, id `meet-impeach`]

The last thing Congress does is also about a person who works for the government of the whole country. But this person already has the job, and is accused of wrongdoing.

*The judge who took money*

> A federal judge is accused of taking money to decide court cases in favor of one company. ⟦The House voted, by more than half, to charge the judge with taking the money⟧. ⟦The Senate then held a trial, and 70 of the 100 senators voted to convict⟧. The judge was removed from the job.

This is a trial, but not in a court. The House brings the charge, the way an accuser does, and the Senate holds the trial. A guilty vote sends nobody to prison: it only removes the official from the job. If the official also broke a criminal law, the ordinary courts deal with that separately.

It has two steps. When the House votes to charge someone, people say they have been “impeached”, but nobody is removed yet. Only the Senate’s guilty vote removes them. It can happen to a federal judge, the head of a department, or even the President.

**How to spot it**

1. **Find the accused: a federal judge who already holds the job.** This is about someone in office, not someone being picked.
2. **Find the charge: taking money to decide court cases for one company.** It has to be serious wrongdoing.
3. **Find the House vote: more than half voted to charge the judge.** That step charges the judge, and removes no one.
4. **Find the Senate trial: 70 of 100 senators voted guilty.** Two-thirds of the senators present must vote guilty before anyone is removed.

This is **Impeachment**. It covers the charge and the trial, so a story that stops at the House vote is still this, even if the official stays in the job.

You may also hear this called “removal from office”. That means the same thing here, and from now on this unit uses one name: **Impeachment**.

### 16. The question to ask when Congress is in the news

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 16 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `question`, id `q-congress`]

Here is the question and its five answers in one place.

**The question:** **“What is Congress doing?”**

**Its answers**

Each answer leads to one name, and so rules out the other four.

- **“Passes a law the Constitution allows”**
  - Give this answer when Congress passes a law on something the Constitution lists as its job, like taxes, trade or the armed forces, and it takes away no right the Constitution protects.
  - It leads to **Within Congress’s power**.
- **“Passes a law the Constitution does not allow”**
  - Give this answer when Congress passes a law on something the Constitution leaves to the states, like what schools teach, or one that takes away a protected right, like free speech.
  - It leads to **Beyond Congress’s power**.
- **“Votes money for something, or refuses it”**
  - Give this answer when Congress votes the money for something, cuts it or leaves it out, and the government can spend only what Congress voted.
  - It leads to **The power of the purse**.
- **“Votes on a person or a treaty the President put forward”**
  - Give this answer when the Senate votes on someone the President picked for a top job, like a judge, where more than half must vote yes, or on a treaty, where two-thirds of those present must.
  - It leads to **Senate approval**.
- **“Charges an official with serious wrongdoing, or holds the trial”**
  - Give this answer when a federal official, even the President, is accused of serious wrongdoing, and the House votes to charge them, or the Senate tries them, where two-thirds must vote guilty.
  - It leads to **Impeachment**.

**Why it matters**

Each of the five names is one thing Congress does, and for a law, whether the Constitution lets Congress pass it. A law passed by the right votes can still be one the Constitution does not allow, so never name a law by the vote alone.

Stories that sound alike can get different names, and stories that sound different can get the same one. A tax on tickets and a new one-dollar coin are the same name. A tax on bottled water and a grant for clinics are different names, although both are about the same clinics.

Only what Congress did decides it. The topic, the vote and the people never do.

**How to answer it**

1. **Find the sentence that shows what Congress did.** Look for the bill that passed, the money voted or left out, the Senate’s vote, or the House’s charge.
2. **If it is a law, run two checks: is the subject on the Constitution’s list, and does it take a right away?** On the list with no right taken away is **Within Congress’s power**, and anything else is **Beyond Congress’s power**.
3. **If Congress votes money, cuts it or leaves it out, the answer is The power of the purse.** The government can spend only what Congress has voted.
4. **If the Senate votes on the President’s pick or a *treaty*, the answer is Senate approval.** The Senate’s yes comes before the pick takes effect.
5. **If an official already in the job is charged, or on trial, the answer is Impeachment.** Either step counts: the House’s charge or the Senate’s trial.
6. **Find the exact words that show your answer.** If you can’t find them, you don’t have an answer yet.

**When two answers both seem to fit**

Sometimes two answers both seem to fit. Each pair below has one question that separates it. One pair also has a rule of its own: a bill that spends money is a law too, so when a story shows both, the answer is **“Votes money for something, or refuses it”**.

- Within Congress’s power or Beyond Congress’s power: Is the law about something on the Constitution’s list for Congress? Does it take away anyone’s right to speak, to worship, to publish or to gather peacefully?
- Within Congress’s power or The power of the purse: Does the bill raise money or set some other rule? Or does it decide what the government may spend: voting money, cutting it or leaving it out?

### 18. One whole story, where the start points the wrong way

*The coin presses*

> The mint says its presses are worn out and cannot make enough coins. The House and the Senate passed a bill that gives the mint $120 million to buy new presses. The President is expected to sign it.

**Question 1 of 2: Who makes the final call?**

> The mint says its presses are worn out and cannot make enough coins. ⟦The House and the Senate passed a bill⟧ that gives the mint $120 million to buy new presses. The President is expected to sign it.

Answer: **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**

The story ends on a vote by Congress: “The House and the Senate passed a bill”. The President is expected to sign it, but a signature does not change whose decision it was, so the answer is **“Congress, in the House or the Senate”**.

Still possible: all five names this unit teaches.

**Question 2 of 2: What is Congress doing?**

> The mint says its presses are worn out and cannot make enough coins. The House and the Senate passed a bill that ⟦gives the mint $120 million to buy new presses⟧. The President is expected to sign it.

Answer: **“Votes money for something, or refuses it”**

The bill gives money: “gives the mint $120 million to buy new presses”. The government can spend only what Congress has voted, so Congress is deciding what it may spend.

Coins are on the Constitution’s list too, so the story also looks like a law on a listed subject. When a story shows both, the money wins: **“Votes money for something, or refuses it”**.

Still possible: **The power of the purse**. Ruled out: **Within Congress’s power**, **Beyond Congress’s power**, **Senate approval** and **Impeachment**.

**Name it:** **The power of the purse**

**You are asked:** The bill is about coins, which are on the Constitution’s list, so this can look like **Within Congress’s power**. All of these are true. Choose the one that settles it.

- (a) The bill is about coins, and coins are on the Constitution’s list.
- (b) The bill hands the mint $120 million: Congress is deciding what to spend.
- (c) The President is expected to sign it, once both votes are done.

**Shown as soon as you choose.** The one that settles it is (b): The bill hands the mint $120 million: Congress is deciding what to spend.
- If you chose (a): True, and it is why this looks like **Within Congress’s power**. But coins fit both names, so they cannot settle it.
- If you chose (c): True, but signing comes after Congress has decided. It does not change what Congress did.

**Why this is The power of the purse and not Within Congress’s power**

A bill about a listed subject is a law on that subject, and on its own that would be **Within Congress’s power**. But this bill also decides whether the government may spend $120 million, and a bill that spends is always a law too. When a story shows both, the money wins: **“Votes money for something, or refuses it”**.

**Within Congress’s power** is for a law that sets a rule on a listed subject, like a price or a tax, and spends no money.

**Does it look like a story you know?**

A second look: does this remind you of a story you know? A bill about money and the mint may bring back the airline-ticket tax, which was **Within Congress’s power**.

When a likeness and the answer disagree, go back to the words that answer the question: “gives the mint $120 million to buy new presses”. The ticket tax raised money and spent nothing. This bill gives money to buy presses, like the flood barrier story, where everything turned on whether money could be spent. So the answer stands.

### 19. What to carry away

*Unit Three · rev 5 · Draft: not yet read by a newcomer · Part 2 of 2 · Card 19 of 19*

[reviewers only: card kind `recap`, id `recap-congress`]

This puts the unit in one place.

**This unit’s questions and answers**

What is Congress doing?
- Passes a law the Constitution allows → Within Congress’s power
- Passes a law the Constitution does not allow → Beyond Congress’s power
- Votes money for something, or refuses it → The power of the purse
- Votes on a person or a treaty the President put forward → Senate approval
- Charges an official with serious wrongdoing, or holds the trial → Impeachment

**For each name: what to look for, and the question to ask when you spot it**

- **Within Congress’s power**: a law Congress passes on something the Constitution lists as its job, like taxes or the mail, and no right in the Constitution that the law takes away.
- **Beyond Congress’s power**: a law Congress passes, and either it is about something the Constitution does not list as Congress’s job, or it takes away a right the Constitution protects.
- **The power of the purse**: Congress voting money for something, cutting it or leaving it out, and the government able to spend only what Congress voted.
- **Senate approval**: someone the President picked for a top government job, or a treaty the President signed, and the Senate voting on whether to approve it.
- **Impeachment**: a federal official accused of serious wrongdoing, and Congress acting to remove them: the House voting to charge them, or the Senate holding the trial.

**To carry away**

- Find the words that show what Congress did. If you cannot find them, you do not have an answer yet. The vote never decides it: every law passes the House and the Senate, and a law can pass every vote and still be **Beyond Congress’s power**.
- For a law, run two checks: is the subject on the Constitution’s list, and does the law take a right away?
- A bill that spends money is a law too. When a story shows both, the money wins: **The power of the purse**.
- Two Senate votes can look alike. A vote on the President’s pick or a *treaty* is **Senate approval**. A vote on a charge against someone already in the job is **Impeachment**, and it covers the charge as well as the trial, so a story that stops at the House vote has not told you the official is gone.
- In a headline, “passed a law” needs the two checks, “cut the money” is **The power of the purse**, “approved the President’s pick” is **Senate approval**, and “voted to charge” is **Impeachment**.
