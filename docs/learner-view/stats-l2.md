# Statistical Claims: How big it is: out of how many

Revision 1, live. Part: How big it is: out of how many. Where the subject ends up: When a headline and its article, a chart, an ad or a post on your phone is about to make you believe, share or act on a number, you can say whether the number shows what it is used to claim, point to the line that decides it (or say what is missing), and say how big it really is in plain counts, such as from 3 in 1,000 to 2 in 1,000 over ten years.

## The why

A count means little until you know out of how many. 1,200 sounds like a lot; in a city of 400,000 it is 3 in every 1,000 people.
People who read numbers for a living turn a count into “so many in 1,000” before they decide whether it is big. Here you learn to do that in a few seconds.

## Step 1: Out of how many

To size a count, find how many it is out of, then say it as so many in every 1,000.
First, find the count and the total it is out of: 1,200 people with the flu, in a city of 400,000. Second, divide the count by the total and multiply by 1,000: 1,200 ÷ 400,000 × 1,000 = 3. That is 3 in every 1,000.
It works the same when the total is not whole thousands: 75 loaves returned out of 1,500 baked is 75 ÷ 1,500 × 1,000 = 50 in every 1,000.
Tap the sentence that gives both the count and what it is out of. If no sentence gives both, tap Not given.
Then compare it with the usual, which the text often gives in the same form: “about 6 in every 1,000 in a usual winter”. A claim of more than usual holds up when your number is clearly above the usual (a strong word such as “soar”, “boom” or “surge” needs a big rise, half again or more); a little above the usual is an ordinary year, and the same or below does not show it either. A claim of fewer than usual holds up when your number is clearly below the usual. If the text says in words that it is a usual level, that shows it too, even with no total. If the text gives no total or no usual, you cannot tell.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “A big number means a big problem.”
The right line: 1,200 sounds like a lot until you see it is 3 in every 1,000. A count is big only against how many it is out of.

## Step 2: Percentage points

A rate that goes “up 2 points” or “down 2 percentage points” moved by 2 in every 100 people.
From 8% to 6% is down 2 points. As people, that is from 80 in 1,000 to 60 in 1,000: 20 fewer in every 1,000. Turn percents into people in 1,000 and the change is easy to see.

## Step 3: A sum of money

Size a sum of money, or any amount, the same way: for each person, and for one year.
$6 million over 10 years, in a city of 300,000 people: $6,000,000 ÷ 300,000 = $20 for each person, and $20 ÷ 10 years = $2 a year. A sum that sounds huge can be a few dollars each.

> # Flu hits Millbrook hard this winter
>
> The county health office counted 1,200 flu cases in Millbrook this winter, in a city of about 400,000 people. In a usual winter, Millbrook sees about 6 flu cases in every 1,000 people, the county says.
> Health officer Dr. Lena Ortiz urged residents to get their flu shots.

Before the working, the learner may commit to a choice (not scored, not stored):
**What is this claiming?**
(the options come in an order that depends on the seed of the question)
- More people in Millbrook have the flu than is usual
- More people in Millbrook’s county have the flu than is usual
- Flu shots will stop the flu in Millbrook

The working, one step at a time:
1. Find what the headline claims.: “Hits hard” says more people have the flu than is usual.
1. Find the sentence with the count and what it is out of.: The first sentence has both: 1,200 people with the flu, out of 400,000 people in all.
1. Turn the count into so many in 1,000.: 1,200 ÷ 400,000 × 1,000 = 3, so 3 in every 1,000.
1. Compare it with the usual.: The usual is about 6 in every 1,000. This winter is 3, half the usual, so the headline says more than the numbers show.

Then the answer: More people in Millbrook have the flu than is usual / The county health office counted 1,200 flu cases in Millbrook this winter, in a city of about 400,000 people. / 3 in 1,000 / Does not show it. 1,200 people with the flu sounds like a lot, but in a city of 400,000 it is 3 people in every 1,000. The usual winter is about 6 in every 1,000, so this winter is milder than usual, not harder.
What you would need to see: You would need a count above the usual for a city this size: more than 2,400.
