# Basic Math: Rates and scaling

Revision 1, live. Part: Rates and scaling. Where the subject ends up: Work out the number a situation needs (a total, a price per one, a scaled amount, a percent or the price before one, how much paint or soil to buy, how high a ladder reaches, what savings grow to or a loan costs) and tell whether it is sensible, when a receipt, a recipe, a sale tag, a room or an offer hands you some numbers and a question and nobody names the method.

## The why

A deal says 4 for $5 and you want 7. A recipe serves 4 and you are cooking for 7. A price is in euros. Each time you know one pair of numbers and need another.
One move solves all of them: find the amount for one, then scale.

## Step 1: Per one, then scale

Cans are 4 for $10, and you want 7. Write the two rows: how many, and dollars. One can costs $10 ÷ 4 = $2.50. Seven cost 7 × $2.50 = $17.50.
Check which way the rate goes: dollars for one can is dollars ÷ cans. Cans for one dollar would be the other way up, and that is not what you need.
The same move changes a recipe (cups for one person), money, a map (miles for one inch) and units. A rate for money or units is written with a 1 on one side: 1 US dollar = 0.92 euros; 1 mile = 1.609 kilometers.
The rule for which way: going toward the side with the 1 (euros to dollars, kilometers to miles), divide by the number. Going away from it (dollars to euros, miles to kilometers), multiply. Then use the test that always works: should the answer be bigger or smaller? 0.92 euros make a dollar, so a price in euros is a bit more in dollars.

A table with two rows: Cans: 4, 1, 7 / Dollars: $10.00, $2.50, ?.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “7 cans is 3 more than 4, so it costs $3 more: $13.”
The right line: Adding the difference ignores what one can costs. Prices like these scale: find the price of one, then multiply.

## Step 2: A worked example

A recipe, "Pancakes":

> ¾ cup  |  flour
> 2  |  eggs
> 1 teaspoon  |  salt
> (Serves 4)
> (Bake or cook 25 minutes)

The working, one step at a time:
1. Say what is asked and what is given. Asked: the flour for 7 people. Given: ¾ cup of flour serves 4 (¾ cup is 0.75 on the calculator). The eggs, the salt and the time do not matter here.
1. Make a rough answer in your head. 7 people is about 2 times 4, so about 2 × 0.75 = 1.5 cups.
1. Name the kind of problem and draw it. Per one, in a table of two rows: 4 people take 0.75 cups, so 1 person takes 0.75 ÷ 4, about 0.188 cups, and 7 people take 7 times that.
1. Work it out exactly on the calculator, then look back. On the calculator: 0.75 ÷ 4 × 7 = 1.31 cups. Look back: near the rough 1.5; more people than 4, so more than ¾ cup.

Then the answer: 1.50 cups / 1.31 cups. One person takes 0.75 ÷ 4 cups, so 7 people take 1.31 cups. A recipe scales by the number of people.

## Step 3: A worked example

A plain table, "Trip budget":

> A hotel room for one night  |  165 euros
> Exchange rate  |  1 US dollar = 0.91 euros
> Card fee abroad  |  none

The working, one step at a time:
1. Say what is asked and what is given. Asked: what 165 euros is in US dollars. Given: 1 US dollar buys 0.91 euros. The card fee does not matter.
1. Make a rough answer in your head. Call the rate 1 to 1: about $165. Each dollar buys less than one euro, so nudge it up, about a tenth more. About $182.
1. Name the kind of problem and draw it. Per one, in a table of two rows: 1 US dollar = 0.91 euros. From euros to dollars, the side with the 1, divide by 0.91.
1. Work it out exactly on the calculator, then look back. On the calculator: 165 ÷ 0.91 = $181.32. Look back: near the rough $182; more dollars than euros, since each dollar buys less than one euro.

Then the answer: $182 / $181.32. Each dollar buys 0.91 euros, so 165 euros is 165 ÷ 0.91 = $181.32.
