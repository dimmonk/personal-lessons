# Wealth Preservation: Your biggest single bet

Revision 1, live. Part: Your biggest single bet. Where the subject ends up: Run a planner's yearly check on your own US accounts: list everything, see what money you need soon is exposed and what single loss could ruin you, find the certain leaks (yearly costs in dollars, a missed match, tax in the wrong place, foreign filings) and the risks you did not choose (one big bet, a drifted mix), check who inherits and who can act, and change only what the check calls for.

## The why

Owen holds his employer’s stock in his 401(k) and more shares from work in his brokerage account. Each looks small on its own statement.
Added up, they are a quarter of everything he has, riding on the company that also pays him. Here you find your biggest single bet.

## Step 1: The rule

Add up one company’s stock in every account: its stock fund in a 401(k), shares from work, shares you bought. Divide by the total of all your accounts: the whole list (not your home).
Over about 10%, a common rule and not a law: sell down to it. The money from the sale stays on your list, so the total does not change: sell the stock minus 10% of the total. A fund that holds hundreds or thousands of companies is not one bet.
Sell first inside a 401(k) or an IRA, where a sale is not taxed; shares in a brokerage account may owe tax on their gain. One exception: if your employer’s stock in the 401(k) has grown a lot since it was bought, it has a tax rule of its own when it leaves the plan, so check with a tax preparer before selling it there. Otherwise, sell there first.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “I work there, so I know the company is solid.”
The right line: More than half of single US stocks have done worse over their life than safe Treasury bills. And if your employer falls, your pay and your savings fall together.

## Step 2: A worked example

A statement, "Owen’s 401(k)" (a tab named "401(k)", one of a household's papers: one open at a time, the first at the start, and the one with a marked line after the answer):

> Fund  |  Value  (the column heads)
> Acme Corp Stock Fund  |  $14,000
> Large Company Fund (500 companies)  |  $52,000

A statement, "Owen’s brokerage account" (a tab named "Brokerage", one of a household's papers: one open at a time, the first at the start, and the one with a marked line after the answer):

> Holding  |  Value  (the column heads)
> Acme Corp shares, from work  |  $16,000
> US Stock Market Fund (about 3,500 companies)  |  $18,000

A statement, "Owen’s bank" (a tab named "Bank", one of a household's papers: one open at a time, the first at the start, and the one with a marked line after the answer):

> Account  |  Balance  (the column heads)
> Savings  |  $15,000

The working, one step at a time:
1. Add up the company’s stock in every account. $14,000 + $16,000 = $30,000.
1. Divide by the total of all the accounts. $30,000 ÷ $115,000 = 26.1%.
1. Hold it against 10%. A fund of hundreds of companies is not one bet. 26.1% is over 10%.
1. Work out what to sell. 10% of $115,000 is $11,500. $30,000 − $11,500 = $18,500.
1. Sell first where a sale is not taxed. All $14,000 in the 401(k) first: no tax on a sale there. The other $4,500 from the shares in the brokerage account, which may owe tax on their gain.

Then the answer: 26.1% / Change it / $18,500 / Acme Corp Stock Fund, in the 401(k). Acme Corp is $30,000 of $115,000, 26.1% across the 401(k) and the brokerage account, over 10%. Selling about $18,500, the 401(k)’s $14,000 first, brings it down to 10%.
