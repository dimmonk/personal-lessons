# Wealth Preservation: Foreign holdings under US rules

Revision 1, live. Part: Foreign holdings under US rules. Where the subject ends up: Run a planner's yearly check on your own US accounts: list everything, see what money you need soon is exposed and what single loss could ruin you, find the certain leaks (yearly costs in dollars, a missed match, tax in the wrong place, foreign filings) and the risks you did not choose (one big bet, a drifted mix), check who inherits and who can act, and change only what the check calls for.

## The why

Priya moved from Toronto for a job. She kept her checking account, her RRSP and her TFSA in Canada, and filed her first US tax return without a word about them.
Living in the US, you report accounts abroad to the US, and some of them are taxed in ways Canada does not warn you about. Here you raise the flags; a specialist does the filing.

## Step 1: The reports

Add up each account’s highest balance in the year, in dollars. Over $10,000 together, at any time: file an FBAR (FinCEN Form 114: FinCEN is the Treasury office that takes it), online, not with your tax return, by April 15 (October 15 at the latest).
Form 8938 goes with your tax return when either of two totals passes its line: the December 31 total, or the highest total. Check both; one over is enough.

A plain table, "Form 8938, living in the US: over either line":

> Filing status  |  December 31  |  At any time  (the column heads)
> Filing alone  |  $50,000  |  $75,000
> Married filing jointly  |  $100,000  |  $150,000

A value looked up, never memorized: "Accounts outside the US that together go over this at any time in the year need a report of foreign accounts (FBAR)": $10,000. It prints "Looked up 2026-10-22" and "Source: IRS, Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (irs.gov)", says to check the source, and after a year says to look it up again.

A value looked up, never memorized: "Form 8938, living in the US. Filing alone: over the first on December 31, or over the second at any time in the year. Married filing jointly: over the third on December 31, or over the fourth at any time": $50,000; $75,000; $100,000; $150,000. It prints "Looked up 2026-10-22" and "Source: IRS, Comparison of Form 8938 and FBAR Requirements (irs.gov)", says to check the source, and after a year says to look it up again.

## Step 2: The flags

An RRSP or RRIF: federal US tax on its growth waits until money comes out, with nothing to file for that (some states do not follow). It still counts in both totals. Money taken out has Canadian tax withheld: check with the preparer first.
A TFSA: Canada does not tax what it earns, but the US may, every year. Money added while you live outside Canada is taxed by Canada at 1% a month.
A Canadian mutual fund or ETF outside an RRSP: usually a passive foreign investment company under US tax: its gains can be taxed at the top rate, plus interest.
Anything due or flagged: book a preparer who works on both sides of the border. Your job is to raise it, not to file it.

A wrong idea, marked wrong: “My Canadian accounts are Canada’s business. The US only cares about US accounts.”
The right line: Living in the US, you report accounts abroad, and missing the FBAR can bring penalties.
