All four were present and read in full; nothing here was filled in from memory. Revised 2026-10-04 after two adversarial reviews (`docs/learning-science/review-citations.md` and `docs/learning-science/review-claims.md`); every change, and every review point that was rejected, is listed in the Revision log at the end. ## 1. How to use this document

This is the single source of truth for lesson design in Fieldcraft. Every rule about how a lesson, a key question, a drill item, a piece of feedback or a verdict is built must trace to a numbered principle (P1, P2, ...) below, and nothing else may govern lesson design. Taste, habit, a screen-length instinct or "it reads cleaner" are not grounds. If a rule cannot cite a principle, either the rule is wrong or this document is missing a principle.

- `docs/lesson-standard.md` operationalises this document. It turns principles into concrete card formats, templates and build-time checks, and it must cite principle ids (for example "per P5") on every rule. A rule in the standard that cites no principle has no authority.
- To change how lessons are built, change this document first: add or revise the principle and its evidence, then change the standard, then change the lessons. Never the other way round. The standard may not contradict a principle's REQUIRES or FORBIDS lines.
- Principle ids are stable. Do not renumber; retire a principle by marking it RETIRED with a reason, and add new ones at the end of their section with the next free number. P28 and P29 were added in the 2026-10-04 revision this way, and P30 in the Fable 5.1 pass: they sit with the sections they belong to but carry the next free numbers, so ids are not in numeric order inside every section.
- REQUIRES and FORBIDS lines are normative: a lesson either meets them or it does not. Lines marked **(judgement call)** are design choices made where the evidence is silent or split; they bind the standard until this document changes, but they are not research findings and should be tuned on real learner data.
- The owner's standing instruction applies to everything here: there is no limit on the size of the content. An idea that needs two paragraphs gets two paragraphs. Nothing in this document caps the size of a unit, a card or an explanation. When an explanation outgrows a phone screen, the card scrolls or the explanation continues on the next card (P7 requires 3); content is never cut to make it fit. Section 5 answers this directly from the evidence.

### Evidence conventions

Every source below was checked online by the researcher who wrote the note it came from. This synthesis inherits those verification statuses; it did not re-verify them, except that every DOI link in the references was resolved against the Crossref registry at the end of writing (see section 8) and that the 2026-10-04 revision re-checked online every source the two reviews questioned (the Revision log says what was found). The seven sources added in the Fable 5.1 pass were each resolved against Crossref and their abstracts read during that pass. The four sample sizes the citation review found in no abstract (Britton & Gülgöz 1991; Harp & Mayer 1998; Sadoski, Goetz & Fritz 1993; Paas 1992) were dropped rather than kept on a weaker record. Tags:

- `full`: VERIFIED-ONLINE, full text (or author manuscript) read.
- `abs`: VERIFIED-ONLINE, citation matched in Crossref and the abstract read.
- `sec`: VERIFIED-ONLINE for the citation only; the findings were read in a secondary report (search excerpt, institutional summary, review of the paper). One notch weaker. A principle that leans on a `sec` source for a number says so.
- `rec`: record only (existence and title confirmed); no finding is relied on.
- `mem`: UNVERIFIED-FROM-MEMORY. No source in this document carries this tag. A few leads that the notes could not confirm were dropped and are listed in section 7.

Strength labels: **strong** = several independent meta-analyses or large replicated experiments agree in direction; **moderate** = a meta-analysis or several experiments agree, with caveats about population, material or research group; **limited** = few studies, indirect evidence, or findings read only in secondary reports; **contested** = credible evidence in both directions. Effect sizes are as the authors report them (d or g, Cohen-style) and most laboratory studies measure learning minutes to a few days later, not months. Study types are named because most of this literature studied school children or undergraduates.

## 2. The learner and the task

**The learner.** An adult studying alone, mostly on a phone, a novice in the subject (each of the seven subjects is new to them), in short self-paced sessions with no teacher, no exam and no external stakes. They arrive with different amounts of prior knowledge, and from the second unit on they are no longer a beginner in the subject: they have studied the earlier units of the same subject (each has 5 to 7 units). Nothing holds them there, so they can stop at any moment; how readily they do is not known, and P28 requires it to be measured, not assumed.

**The task.** Diagnostic classification: read an unlabelled real-world case, answer two or three questions that narrow a list of outcomes, name the outcome, and score name and route separately (a right name reached by the wrong route is a miss). The point of the skill is to apply it to cases in the learner's own life, so the transfer target is concrete, messy and arrives without labels. The cards are the only teaching; everything the learner must later recognise (key wording, option text, outcome names, verdict text) has to have been taught in the cards.

**Words this document uses.** Each has one meaning here and is used the same way throughout (P5 applies to this document too).

- **Case**: a short real-world situation, message or claim that the learner has to judge.
- **Outcome**: one of the named answers a case can turn out to be (Psychology has sixteen).
- **Key**: the subject's fixed set of questions that narrows the outcomes down to one. The key belongs to the subject, not to a unit: each unit teaches part of it.
- **Key question**: one question in the key. The **gate question** is the first one; its answer decides which branch of the key applies.
- **Route**: the answers given to the key questions on the way to an outcome. "Running the route" means answering them in order for one case. Inside a unit, "the route" means the part of the key taught so far.
- **Card**: one screen of teaching inside a unit.
- **Check**: one question between cards about what was just taught.
- **Drill**: the set of practice questions that ends a unit.
- **Specimen** and **determination**: a specimen is an unlabelled case; the determination is the exercise in which the learner runs the whole key on a specimen with no help, and name and route are scored separately.

**Three kinds of unit.** The task above describes most of the app, not all of it. A unit is one of three kinds:

- **Classification units (C)** teach a key and its outcomes, as described above.
- **Fact units (F)** teach a list the learner must simply know. The comprehension audit found US Civics Units 2, 4 (mostly) and 5 are exam-fact lists (documents, amendment numbers, dates, symbols) that the key cannot classify (`docs/comprehension-audit/civics.md`, K8).
- **Procedure units (P)** teach a calculation or routine. Basic Math contains 27 procedures or formulas, and the audit found a worked numeric example for only 5 of them and none for 15 (`docs/comprehension-audit/math.md`).

The "Applies to" column in the principle table says which kinds each principle governs. Where a requirement names the key, the route or an outcome, it binds classification units; for the other two kinds read "step" or "item". P14 is about outcomes and is for classification units only. P12, P13 and P22 govern classification units and procedure units: in a procedure unit the thing to tell apart is the type of problem, and so which procedure applies, and that is where the evidence for those three principles is strongest (comparing worked solutions, varied worked examples, mixed mathematics practice). P9 also covers the steps of a procedure. Fact units and procedure units have their own added requirements (P3 requires 5, P24 requires 7, P10 requires 6, P22 requires 8), because the evidence that matters for them differs: spaced retrieval for facts, worked examples and mixed problem types for procedures. Section 4.3 gives a sequence for each kind.

**Which bodies of evidence transfer.**

| Evidence | Why it transfers | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Retrieval practice, spacing, feedback | Hundreds of experiments and many meta-analyses, in labs and classrooms, including some adult professionals; the mechanisms do not depend on the subject | Mostly undergraduates; short delays; fact-like material. Whether it holds for a multi-step route is argued (P16) |
| Worked examples, expertise reversal, case comparison, self-explanation | Include adult novices and, for worked examples and comparison, categorisation and recognition tasks (statistics problem types, designers' styles, negotiation cases, ECG and radiology diagnosis) | Dominated by mathematics and science problems; recognition studies are few |
| Interleaving, contrasting categories | The nearest match to the task: "which of these confusable categories is this new case?" Strongest for pictures and for choosing the method for a maths problem; includes ECG and chest-radiograph diagnosis in medical students | Reversed for studies using words, not reliable for expository text; one study of statistical concepts (Sana, Yan & Kim 2017) is the nearest positive result, and nothing tests everyday verbal categories (ideologies, scam types, fallacies), which is most of what this app teaches |
| Segmenting, signalling, pre-training, contiguity, seductive details | Meta-analyses agree on direction | Mostly narrated animations and short expository passages, not text cards; applying them to cards is by analogy |
| Pretesting, productive failure, concreteness fading, if-then plans, debiasing and fraud education | Real effects under the studied conditions | Fact retention (pretesting); teacher-led STEM classrooms (productive failure); mathematics and children (fading); behaviour change for held goals (plans); specific biases or investment fraud (debiasing, fraud education) |
| Phone delivery, adults with no stakes, the key as a format, scoring name and route separately | Almost no direct evidence | Treated as gaps in section 7. Where this document requires something here it says it is a judgement call |

Two limits shape the whole document. First, nothing found studies the app's exact task, so every principle is an inference from neighbouring tasks; the strength labels say how far each inference is from its evidence. Second, the strongest results for several techniques (interleaved mathematics, productive failure, perceptual learning modules, debiasing games, pre-training) come from research groups that champion the technique, so they are discounted accordingly.

## 3. Principles

The principles run along the learning sequence: before the unit (P1-P2), presenting new material (P3-P8), teaching the route and the examples (P9-P14), first practice (P15-P18), feedback (P19-P21), later practice (P22-P23), across units and days (P24), transfer (P25-P26), and checking the lesson works (P27). P29 (the wrong ideas learners arrive with) sits with the examples, P28 (staying with it) with the days-and-units section, and P30 (showing structure as a map or a table) with presenting new material. Each line under "Source notes" names the findings in the four research notes (I = instruction, P = practice, D = discrimination-transfer, T = text-feedback) so a maintainer can trace a claim back. There are 30 principles, more than the usual range, because ideas with different evidence or different checkable requirements were kept apart instead of being merged (where two principles carry the same rule, one of them points to the other so the build has one check, not two). P28 and P29 were added after the claims review found that nothing covered persistence and nothing covered misconceptions; P30 was added in the Fable 5.1 pass because pictures and diagrams, one of the best-replicated findings about learning from text, had been left as a gap.

### Before the unit

### P1. Before teaching, tell the learner in plain words what they will be able to do and how the parts fit together.
- Evidence:
  - Advance organisers: Luiten, Ames & Ackerson (1980), meta-analysis of 135 studies, facilitative for learning and retention, abstract gives no effect size, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312017002211), `abs`. Mayer (1979), review answering the earlier conclusion that organisers do not work, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543049002371), `abs`. Hartley & Davies (1976), review of pretests, objectives, overviews and organisers, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543046002239), `abs`. Dochy, Segers & Buehl (1999), review: prior knowledge generally raises performance, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543069002145), `abs`.
  - Objectives and preview questions: Sana et al. (2020), 3 experiments, stating objectives helped retention a little and turning them into multiple-choice pretest questions helped more, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.19-11-0257), `abs`. Pan & Sana (2021), 5 experiments, N = 1,573, pretests helped memory at 5 minutes and 48 hours with or without feedback, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000345), `abs`. Richland, Kornell & Kao (2009), 5 experiments, failed pretest attempts still helped later learning, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016496), `abs`. Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009), 6 experiments, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015729), `abs`.
  - Classroom limits: Carpenter, Rahman & Perkins (2018), randomised classroom study, prequestions helped only the asked-about material and added little a week later beyond end-of-class retrieval, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000145), `abs`. Soderstrom & Bjork (2023), classroom study reporting benefit for related non-pretested material, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09805-6), `sec` (wording from a search-returned abstract). Toftness et al. (2018), two experiments, in 20-minute lecture videos the benefit stayed with the prequestioned content, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jarmac.2018.06.003), `abs`.
  - Checklist, not proof: Merrill (2002), theoretical synthesis of five conditions (real-world problem, activate knowledge, demonstrate, apply, integrate), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02505024), `abs`. Frick et al. (2010), correlational study of 464 students, student-rated, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11423-009-9131-z), `abs`.
- Strength: limited to moderate for an organiser (old meta-analysis, no effect size in the abstract, a history of inconsistent findings, so expect a modest benefit); limited for stating objectives; moderate that a pretest helps what it asks about; limited for any claim that it helps everything or adds to later retrieval practice.
- Boundary conditions: Every study here measures retention of facts from expository text or lectures, not classification. Pretest gains are specific to what is asked. The classroom studies disagree on whether prequestions add anything once later retrieval practice exists. The two pretest papers disagree on feedback: Sana et al. found feedback on pretest answers lowered final performance, Pan & Sana found the advantage held with or without it, and the failed-test literature found the benefit with immediate feedback (P19); this is unresolved, and the rule below sidesteps it by always explaining at once. The claim that organisers help low-prior-knowledge learners most is unverified (`mem`) and is not relied on. Whether the learner has met the everyday situation the organiser starts from is an assumption the lesson should not make without a check.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. Every unit opens with an orienting card that says, in plain words, what the learner will be able to do after it, stated as a task on a real case ("tell X from Y in a real message"); names the ideas in the order the cards teach them and how they connect, with the key shown as a map (P30); and ties the unit to a situation the learner has met in everyday life, not to the subject's vocabulary.
  2. The orienting card is more general than the details that follow and uses the final terms only after giving them in plain words (P4).
  3. **(judgement call)** Preview questions are optional and at most three: ungraded, drawn from the unit's real key questions or outcomes, each followed at once by the explanation (P19, P20), never scored against the learner and never gating progress. Because nothing has been taught yet, that explanation is written in ordinary words, uses no term the learner has not met, and may say that the unit teaches the full reasoning (P3, P4). They are the weakest-supported element in this document and may be dropped without breaking anything else.
- FORBIDS:
  - Opening a unit with a bare definition or the subject's vocabulary.
  - Treating the orienting card as a substitute for explanation.
  - Preview questions about incidental detail, preview questions with no explanation after them, or any score attached to them.
- Source notes: I F22, F23, F9; P F14.

### P2. Write for a true beginner by default, and let support shrink as the learner shows competence.
- Evidence:
  - Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003), review: techniques that help novices lose their benefit or backfire for experienced learners, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4), `abs`. Kalyuga (2007), review, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-007-9054-3), `abs`. Chen, Kalyuga & Sweller (2017), theoretical review linking it to element interactivity, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-016-9359-1), `abs`.
  - Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters & Brod (2025), meta-analysis of 60 experimental studies, 176 effect sizes, about 5,900 participants: low prior knowledge learned more from high-assistance instruction (d about 0.51), high prior knowledge from low-assistance (d about -0.43), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142), `sec` (existence confirmed; the numbers come from two independent secondary reports that agree).
  - Feedback version: Fyfe & Rittle-Johnson (2016), two experiments with school children, feedback helped children without a correct strategy and hurt those who had one, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000053), `abs`; a companion study found immediate feedback also helped mastery for children with high prior knowledge, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2016.03.009), `abs`. Shute (2008), review, immediate directive feedback for low achievers, delayed facilitative feedback for high achievers, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654307313795), `full`.
  - Text version: McNamara, Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch (1996), two experiments, low-knowledge readers did better with coherent text and high-knowledge readers with minimally coherent text, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1401_1), `abs` (see P3).
- Strength: strong for the general pattern (a meta-analysis plus decades of experiments), though the pooled techniques are heterogeneous and "prior knowledge" is measured in many ways.
- Boundary conditions: It is an interaction, so it matters only when learners differ in knowledge; for a true beginner audience it argues for more support, not less. Learners stop being true beginners as units pass, and the same evidence then argues for thinning the explanation of links they have already learned and answered correctly, not of anything new. How far that goes is limited: a learner in Unit 5 of a phone course is nowhere near the experienced learners of the reversal studies, and has had days in which to forget, so the evidence permits a shorter restatement of an earlier link and does not justify forbidding a fuller one. The two meta-analytic magnitudes are about equal in opposite directions, so the novice default rests on who the learners are, not on redundancy being cheap. Using it requires knowing who knows something, which needs a test. Segmenting (P7) did not follow the usual story: high-knowledge learners gained more in retention, so "small chunks are for novices" is not a clean finding. Expertise changes within a session and the review does not say where to switch. A fixed lesson cannot adapt fading to each learner, which the fading authors say it should.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. The default path of the first unit of a subject is the full-beginner version: it assumes nothing about the subject beyond everyday life. Every later unit assumes everyday life plus the earlier units of the same subject: it may use their terms and routes without re-teaching them (it restates a definition in one line, per P3 requires 2, and does not re-explain it), and everything that is new to the learner is written out in full.
  2. Shortcuts for competent readers are opt-in and never remove teaching from the default path. **(judgement call)** The mechanism is a short "check what you already know" at the start of a unit whose success lets the learner skip the worked cases.
  3. Support shrinks by design, never by thinning the explanation of anything new. Inside a unit: full worked case, then a completion item, then cold (P17). Across units: a link that an earlier unit of the same subject taught in full may be restated in one line, with the full explanation one tap away (requires 4); a link that is new to the learner is always written out in full. A lesson's text is fixed, so this permission rests on what the earlier units taught, not on a live reading of one learner's record; the learner who has forgotten the link is served by the tap and by the returns of P24.
  4. First encounters with an item get the full explanation (P20); later encounters with the same material (Mixed drill, repeated specimens, spaced returns) may carry shorter feedback that points back to the card's wording, with the full reasoning one tap away.
- FORBIDS:
  - Trimming the beginner cards because experts would find them redundant.
  - A sparse default that hopes beginners cope.
  - Hiding the full explanation behind a tap the first time an item is met.
  - Using the one-line restatement for anything the earlier units did not teach in full, or offering it with no way to reach the full explanation. (A later unit may always re-explain an earlier link at greater length when the new material leans on it heavily; the one-line form is a permission, not a limit.)
- Source notes: I F4, F16, F21; T F9, F21; P F16.

### Presenting new material

### P3. Spell out every connection in the explanation, so a reader with no background never has to fill a gap.
- Evidence:
  - McNamara, Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch (1996), two experiments with junior-high students on science text, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1401_1), `abs`. McNamara & Kintsch (1996), two experiments, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/01638539609544975), `abs`. McNamara (2001), experiment on reading order, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0087352), `abs`.
  - Outside that lab group: Britton & Gülgöz (1991), two experiments, repairing the places where text called for an unstated inference improved conveying the author's intent in adult samples, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.83.3.329), `abs`. Beck, McKeown, Sinatra & Loxterman (1991), revising fifth-grade social-studies text raised recall and question scores, [doi](https://doi.org/10.2307/747763), `abs`. McKeown et al. (1992), [doi](https://doi.org/10.2307/747834), `abs`. Linderholm et al. (2000), causal repairs helped more- and less-skilled readers on a difficult text but not an easy one, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1804_4), `abs`.
  - Strohmaier, Ehmke, Härtig & Leiß (2023), meta-analysis of 45 studies, 6,477 learners, text rewrites: small average effect (g = 0.15), clarity and elaboration helped, reducing complexity and adding cohesion cues did not, low-knowledge learners gained more, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2023.100533), `sec` (numbers from an institutional abstract and a search summary). McNamara, Ozuru & Floyd (2011), fourth graders, adding cohesion cues without explanatory content is not enough, [link](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1070456.pdf), `abs`.
  - Wittwer & Renkl (2010), meta-analysis of 21 experiments, instructional explanations added little per se on average and helped conceptual more than procedural learning, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-010-9136-5), `abs`. Wittwer & Renkl (2008), framework for why explanations often fail, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520701756420), `abs`.
- Strength: moderate. The direction is consistent across experiments over three decades (low-knowledge readers gain from explicit, coherent text, in different texts and populations), but the one meta-analysis found only a small average effect (g about 0.15) and no benefit from cohesion cues alone, the experiments come largely from one lab group, and the "every connection" form of the rule is an extrapolation from them. Moderate for the knowledge-by-coherence interaction.
- Boundary conditions: The "gaps help" result is narrow. It appeared only for readers with high knowledge (and in one study only those with weaker comprehension skill), only on deep-understanding measures, and only after the reader fills the gap successfully; skilled high-knowledge readers did better with the cohesive text (Ozuru et al. 2009, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2008.04.003), `abs`; O'Reilly & McNamara 2007, n = 143, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/01638530709336895), `abs`). Repairs did nothing for an easy text. Strohmaier's null for "cohesion" sits beside the benefits above; the likeliest reconciliation (the notes' reading, not tested) is that cheap surface cues do nothing and repair of a missing explanatory link works. Wittwer & Renkl are a caution that explanation does not work by being present: it has to be aimed at the confusion and followed by the learner doing something (P15, P16). Almost all studies are short expository passages, school students or undergraduates, read once. P3 and P6 are one relevance rule in two halves (section 4.1): every sentence has a job, and every missing link is written in; the build checks them together.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. Each card states its relations in words: why this idea follows the previous card (one sentence linking back), what the idea is, what it decides in the key (what it rules in and out), and what a result means ("so you can rule out X").
  2. Each "this" and "it" has one unmistakable referent. Each term is defined where first used, or the card says which earlier card defined it and restates the definition in one line (P8).
  3. No card depends on a link the learner would have to supply from knowledge of the subject. Inference practice lives in checks and drills, where the app can check the answer (P16), not as gaps in card prose.
  4. Repairs are of content: the missing link is written in. Adding connective words over unchanged content does not count.
  5. **(judgement call)** **Fact units:** facts are chunked and grouped under the concept they serve, and each states how it relates to that concept (what it is evidence of or part of); a bare list is not a card. How the facts are retrieved is P24 requires 7. The grouping rests on the organiser evidence of P1, which is limited.
  6. Cards run in the order of dependence. A card uses only everyday knowledge and what earlier cards of the same subject have taught, and nothing a learner needs now is deferred to a later card or unit. This is requires 3 applied to the order of the cards: a forward pointer is a gap the learner cannot fill. (The comprehension audit found feedback that said "watch for this to resurface ... in Lesson 2" and cards that told the learner to "run D2", a step that exists nowhere in the key.)
- FORBIDS:
  - Leaving a gap for the learner to bridge in card text. (A link that an earlier unit taught in full is not a gap: P2 requires 3 lets it be restated in one line, with the full explanation one tap away.)
  - "As we saw" with no restatement.
  - Pointing forward ("you will meet this in Unit 4") for something the learner needs in order to understand the present card or answer the present item.
  - Stacking facts without saying how they relate.
  - Trimming connective reasoning to save words.
- Source notes: I F16, F17; T F1, F2.

### P4. Say what an idea means in ordinary words first, then attach the term, and define each term the learner must use where it is first needed.
- Evidence:
  - Ginns, Martin & Marsh (2013), meta-analysis of rewrites into a conversational style (second person, visible author): retention d about 0.30, transfer d about 0.54, effects small and unreliable in studies longer than about 35 minutes, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-013-9228-0), `abs`.
  - Pre-training: Mayer (2009), chapter summary, support in 5 of 5 tests of problem-solving transfer (median d = 0.85), and Mayer & Pilegard (2014), chapter summary, support in 13 of 16 tests (median d = 0.75), for teaching the names and characteristics of the main components before the main lesson, [Mayer 2009](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/multimedia-learning/pretraining-principle/01791D57F5D4164251269E6DF56A8BF1), [Mayer & Pilegard 2014](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-managing-essential-processing-in-multimedia-learning-segmenting-pretraining-and-modality-principles/DD24C2F48B9B1277CE59F78276110258), `abs` (chapter summaries, by the originator). Mayer, Mathias & Wetzell (2002), experiments, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898X.8.3.147), `rec` (not relied on). The same order tested outside that lab: Pollock, Chandler & Sweller (2002), four experiments, presenting complex material first as isolated elements that can be taken in one at a time, and only then with all the elements interacting, gave better learning "for certain groups of learners" than presenting everything together in both phases, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0959-4752(01)00016-0), `abs`.
  - Vocabulary: Stahl & Fairbanks (1986), meta-analysis (children): large effect on comprehension of passages containing the taught words (0.97), small on global comprehension (0.30); best methods combined definition with context, deeper processing and more than one or two exposures, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543056001072), `abs`. Shulman et al. (2020), N = 650 adults: jargon disrupted processing fluency, and lowered self-reported interest and perceived understanding, even when definitions of the jargon terms were provided, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0261927x20902177), `abs`. Bullock et al. (2019), also N = 650 (the two abstracts appear to describe the same experiment): jargon impaired processing, which led to more resistance to persuasion, higher risk perception and lower support for the technology; its abstract says nothing about definitions or perceived understanding, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963662519865687), `abs`.
  - Readability: Davison & Kantor (1982), case study, adaptations driven by readability formulas were not always successful and some made text harder, [doi](https://doi.org/10.2307/747483), `abs`. McNamara et al. (2010), Coh-Metrix validation, Coh-Metrix cohesion indices distinguished high- from low-cohesion versions of texts, while common readability indexes such as Flesch-Kincaid inappropriately distinguished them (they scored the versions differently in a way that did not track cohesion), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/01638530902959943), `abs`. Graesser, McNamara & Kulikowich (2011), five factors carry most text difficulty (word concreteness, syntactic simplicity, referential cohesion, causal cohesion, narrativity), [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189x11413260), `abs`. Sayfi et al. (2024), randomised trial, 488 adults, a plain-language redesign raised correct answers by about 20 percentage points for one of two recommendations, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2023.11.009), `abs`; Elliott et al. (2023), 295 parents, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2023.06.018), `abs`.
- Strength: limited to moderate. No study found tests the order this principle asks for (ordinary words first, then the term); it combines the conversational-style, pre-training, vocabulary and jargon evidence. Pre-training is moderate to limited (large effects, but from the originator's lab, narrated animations, college students, no independent meta-analysis; one independent set of experiments on the parts-then-whole order); the jargon-fluency result is limited (online adult samples, outcomes were engagement and perceived understanding, not learning).
- Boundary conditions: In this app the technical terms are the learning target and cannot be removed, which is not the situation in the jargon experiments. The pre-training studies taught what each part of a system is and does before a narrated animation that ran at its own speed; none tested a list of definitions placed before a text the reader paces. What carries over to a card lesson is the order (each part taught on its own before the card that combines the parts), not a glossary: a card of definitions with no case is the bare-definition opening that P11 forbids, and the vocabulary evidence favours definition together with context over definition alone. Defining a word does not by itself make a passage easier (global effect 0.30). No experiment isolates sentence length for adult novices; "keep sentences under N words" is a rule of thumb, not a finding. The plain-language trial was a whole redesign made with readers, not word substitution. Conversational voice that adds chatter belongs under P6.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. Every idea is first said in ordinary words, then given its term, then the term is used from then on. This holds for headings too: on the card that introduces a term, the heading says the idea in ordinary words (the term may follow the ordinary words in that heading and never stands there alone); from the next card on, headings use the fixed term (P7 requires 5). Address the learner as "you". **(judgement call)** The order "ordinary words, then term" is a design choice that combines the evidence above; it is not itself a tested finding.
  2. Each subject keeps two vocabulary lists. Target terms (outcome names, diagnostic vocabulary) are defined in everyday words at first need, with a concrete context, and reused in later cards, the key and the drills (at least two exposures). Incidental terms (anything the classification does not need) are replaced with ordinary words or cut, and abbreviations are spelled out.
  3. Parts before the whole (the pre-training form). Every term that a key question, an option or a worked case uses is taught before it is needed there, never inside the card that combines it with other terms. A term that is an idea in its own right (every outcome is one) gets cards of its own: a case, then ordinary words, then the term (P11, section 4.3 step 1). A single word that a key question or option leans on is taught at first use, in ordinary words with a concrete context (requires 2). The orienting card (P1) may list the unit's target terms with a few plain words each, as a preview of what is coming; the preview is never the teaching. **(judgement call)** No study compares this with a card of definitions placed before the first teaching card. The choice follows P11 (a case before a definition), P7 (one new point per card) and the vocabulary evidence (definition with context), as the boundary conditions explain.
  4. The quality gate for a card is: every technical word is defined or avoided, every inferential step is written down, and a real novice can restate the card (P27). A grade-level score or a sentence-length count is not a gate.
- FORBIDS:
  - A bare definition in subject jargon as the first thing a learner reads.
  - A glossary card (a list of terms and definitions with no case) as the way a unit teaches its terms.
  - A heading made of a term the learner has not yet been taught.
  - Defining a term with another undefined technical term.
  - Splitting a because-chain into short sentences with the "because" deleted.
  - Chatty filler that adds no information.
- Source notes: I F11, F14; T F3, F5.

### P5. Use one name for one concept everywhere (lesson, key, options, drills, feedback, verdict); where real life uses another word, teach it once and say which name it means.
- Evidence (indirect; see the flag below):
  - Response congruency: Pan & Rickard (2018), meta-analysis of 192 transfer effect sizes from 122 experiments, 10,382 participants: testing transferred (d = 0.40), and transfer was predicted by response congruency (the correct answers at practice and at use being the same or overlapping), elaborated retrieval practice and high initial performance; with none of the three the estimate was near zero (d = -0.05), with all three d = 0.90 (0.58 bias-adjusted), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151), `abs` (published abstract) and `full` (author-accepted manuscript for the figures).
  - Encoding matches retrieval: Tulving & Thomson (1973), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0020071), `sec` (citation plus a secondary summary of the abstract). Morris, Bransford & Franks (1977), transfer-appropriate processing, word-recognition experiments, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(77)80016-9), `abs`.
  - Synonyms versus repetition: Oversteegen & van Wijk (2003), replacing repeated words with synonyms made text more attractive but less comprehensible and slower to read, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1075/dd.4.2.05ove), `sec` (search summary only). Kolers & Gonzalez (1980), a same-language synonym was a weaker memory aid than exact repetition, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.6.1.53), `abs`. Graesser et al. (2011), referential cohesion is one of five main factors of text difficulty, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189x11413260), `abs`.
  - Why novices are hurt by variation: Chi, Feltovich & Glaser (1981), novices represent problems by literal surface features, experts by principle, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0502_2), `abs`.
- FLAG: this principle rests on indirect evidence. No source found tests terminology consistency across a lesson, a key and a quiz (a practitioner write-up that states the gap: Pierrot 2017, [link](https://idratherbewriting.com/2017/03/10/effects-of-terminology-consistency-guest-post/), a blog, not research). The rule is adopted because it is low-risk (consistency costs nothing), the indirect evidence points one way, and it is exactly the failure the owner reported.
- Strength: limited as direct evidence; moderate as indirect evidence (the congruency moderator is a cross-study contrast).
- Boundary conditions: Synonym variation made text more attractive, so there is a style cost to consistency, which does not matter for teaching. Learners often understand paraphrases, and understanding an idea is different from matching a cue. The real world uses many words for one thing, so the end goal is that the learner recognises the variants, which means variants must be taught, not left to chance. Yue, Bjork & Bjork (2013), where a small mismatch between narration and on-screen text helped, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031971), `abs`, concerns simultaneous narration and text in multimedia and is not a basis for varying concept names.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. Each subject has one fixed label for every outcome, every diagnostic question and every option, used verbatim in: the card where it is introduced, the key question and option text, the drill stems and options, the verdict text, and every piece of feedback (why, fals, w).
  2. The learner never meets, in the key or in a drill, an outcome name, question or option that has not already appeared in the same words in a card of that subject. The reverse also holds: every taught target term is used in the key or a drill. A build-time check can enforce both directions (an implementation idea, not a research finding).
  3. Where real life uses another word for the same thing, a card says "also called ..." once, and the feedback at the point of use maps it back ("the case says X; that is the lesson's Y"). After the mapping, one term is used for everything that follows. This is the owner's own alternative: either stay consistent, or match all the ways of saying it in one place.
  4. Case and specimen text may use varied wording on purpose, because transfer needs it (P13); feedback always maps it back to the fixed term. Variation lives in the cases, never in the labels.
  5. **(judgement call)** Each subject has one source for its wording, and it is the key: cards, drills, feedback and verdicts take their labels from it. The key's wording has to earn that place first. It must pass P4 (ordinary words, every technical word taught) and the cold read of P27 like any card. Where the key's wording is the technical or confusing one, the key is rewritten and everything else follows it; where a card and a sound key name the same idea differently, the card changes.
- FORBIDS:
  - Renaming a concept for style or variety.
  - Different wording for the same concept in the lesson, the key, a drill or the verdict.
  - Two terms for one idea without a stated mapping.
  - Analogies or metaphors inside key questions or options, where precise wording matters (P11).
- Source notes: I F24, F11, F16; P F4; T F4, F5.

### P6. Cut what does not help the learner reach the answer, and never cut what does.
- Evidence:
  - Rey (2012), review and meta-analysis of 39 experimental effects: removing "seductive details" (interesting but irrelevant text, pictures, anecdotes) gave small to medium gains in retention and medium gains in transfer, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2012.05.003), `abs`. Sundararajan & Adesope (2020), meta-analysis, including seductive details can hinder learning, moderated by image type, format and pacing, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4), `abs` for direction; the figures (58 studies, more than 7,500 students, about g = -0.33) are `sec` (university release and search summary).
  - Harp & Mayer (1998), 4 experiments with students reading expository passages, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.90.3.414), `abs`. Garner, Gillingham & White (1989), adults and children, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0601_2), `abs`. Bender, Renkl & Eitel (2021), 194 students in a longer session, harm for those not told the details were irrelevant, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcal.12560), `abs`.
  - "Less is more", narrowly: Mayer, Bove, Bryman, Mars & Tapangco (1996), 3 experiments, a summary of captions with simple illustrations of the steps of a process matched or beat the full textbook text; removing the illustrations or the captions removed the advantage and adding text to the summary reduced it, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.88.1.64), `abs`.
  - Elaboration depends on relevance: Reder, Charney & Morgan (1986), author-written and learner-generated elaborations each improved skill learning from instructional text, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03209230), `sec`. Reder & Anderson (1980, 1982), summaries versus text, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0022-5371(80)90122-x), [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/bf03209210), `sec`. Stein, Morris & Bransford (1978), precise relevant elaborations help, irrelevant ones hurt, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0022-5371(78)90423-1), `sec`.
- Strength: strong that interesting-but-irrelevant material harms recall and transfer (two meta-analyses and many experiments; the Sundararajan figure is `sec`). Contested as a general "less is more" claim, which is narrow and old.
- Boundary conditions: This is a rule about relevance to the learning goal, not about length. The "less is more" result compares a built-around-the-steps summary with a long, diffuse textbook passage, tested soon after reading in college students; it is not a comparison against a thin explanation. A worked case, a definition or a near-miss that serves the goal is not a seductive detail. The studies measure recall and transfer, not whether people keep studying, so an engaging aside might keep someone going. The 2021 study suggests telling learners an aside is irrelevant changes its effect, but its abstract does not say the informed group is unharmed. Rey's review calls into question each of the four proposed explanations.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. Every sentence has a job: define, explain why or how, connect to an earlier card or to a key question, illustrate, or separate from the nearest neighbour. A sentence with none of those jobs is cut or marked optional.
  2. Cut: anecdotes, trivia, history tangents, name-dropping, jokes, decorative images, and vivid example detail that competes with the decisive feature, regardless of how few words that saves.
  3. Keep: every step of the diagnostic reasoning, every definition a later question or option uses, the near-miss and the exception.
  4. An aside kept for interest is visibly marked optional and contains nothing a later item tests.
  5. **(judgement call)** An end-of-unit recap, written in the key's wording ("what to carry into the drill"), may be added. It is an addition to the explanation, never a replacement for it; the recap idea is an inference from the summary results and no study tested it in a classification course.
- FORBIDS:
  - Cutting a connective step, definition, example or near-miss because a card is "long".
  - Citing "less is more" as a mandate to shorten.
  - Keeping vivid detail because it is interesting.
  - Any rule of the form "a card must be under N words".
- Source notes: I F13; T F7, F8.

### P7. Put one idea on each card, let the learner set the pace, and add as many cards as the explanation needs.
- Evidence:
  - Rey, Beege, Nebel, Wirzberger, Schmitt & Schneider (2019), meta-analysis of 56 investigations, 88 comparisons: learner-paced meaningful segments gave small-to-medium gains in retention and transfer, lowered cognitive load and increased learning time; learners with high prior knowledge gained more in retention, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4), `abs`.
  - Rosenshine (2012), narrative synthesis of observational teacher studies, cognitive science and cognitive-support research: present new material in small amounts with practice after each step, spend more time presenting and explaining, give many examples, [link](https://www.aft.org/ae/spring2012/rosenshine), `full` (no DOI).
  - Scrolling and screens: Sanchez & Wiley (2009), 2 experiments, scrolling reduced understanding of complex text, especially for readers with lower working-memory capacity, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720809352788), `abs`. Two meta-analyses of reading from paper versus screens found a screen disadvantage for comprehension, concentrated in informational or expository text (g = -0.32 in the second) and larger under time pressure: Delgado et al. (2018), 54 studies, 171,055 participants, g = -0.21, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2018.09.003), `abs`; Clinton (2019), 33 studies, g = -0.25, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9817.12269), `abs` (the notes do not say which paper reported which moderator).
  - Delivery claims: Monib, Qazi & Apong (2025), systematic review of 40 microlearning articles, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.heliyon.2024.e41413), `sec`. Sung, Chang & Liu (2016), meta-analysis of mobile devices in education (moderate average effect for the use of devices, not for text length), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2015.11.008), `abs`.
- Strength: moderate for segmenting (a meta-analysis, but mostly narrated multimedia and small to medium effects); limited for any phone-specific claim.
- Boundary conditions: Segmenting increases time on task and does not rescue a learner who lacks the background to interpret the pieces (P2, P4, P10 supply that). Rosenshine's "small steps" means the amount of new material per step, not how much explanation each step receives, and his figures come from observational studies of school teachers. The screen-reading penalty is smaller when reading is self-paced, and none of these are phone studies; Sanchez & Wiley used desktop web pages, and their abstract reports only that a scrolling format reduced understanding of complex topics, most for readers with lower working-memory capacity. It gives no page length, and nothing in it speaks to a card that runs a little past one phone screen. Microlearning reviews pool many definitions of "micro" and test no length. No source gives a word count per card.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. One idea per card, cut at idea boundaries. "One idea" here means one new point: one thing the learner did not have before the card (one distinction, one step, one worked case, one contrast). A whole outcome or a whole key question is not one point; it takes several cards (section 4.3). The card carries everything needed to understand its point, including the case that shows it (P8 requires 4, P10). A worked case that cannot fit on one screen runs over consecutive cards in step order (P10 requires 2); each of those cards restates the case text, or its decisive part, and the rule at its head (P8), so every card is still self-contained.
  2. No cap on the number of cards in a unit and no word budget per card. Nothing is cut or compressed to make a card fit a screen.
  3. **(judgement call, no validated threshold)** Screen fit is a check, never a limit. When a card runs past one phone screen, ask whether it makes two points. If it does, split it at the boundary between them. If it makes one point that needs the room, keep the content and do one of two things: continue on the next card at a natural joint (for example the case on one card and what it decides on the next), carrying the heading over and opening with one line that restates what the previous card established, as a worked-case sequence does (requires 1); or, where there is no clean joint, let the card scroll. The preference for a second card over a scroll comes from one desktop study; the rule against cutting comes from P3 and P6; and the contiguity evidence (P8) is against separating a rule from its case in order to save a screen.
  4. Learner-paced only: no timers and no auto-advance, because the screen penalty grows under time pressure.
  5. Each card heading names the card's point: in the fixed term once the term has been taught (P5), and in ordinary words on the card that introduces the term (P4 requires 1). P8 requires 2 is this same rule, checked once.
- FORBIDS:
  - Auto-advancing cards or timed reading.
  - Cutting or compressing an explanation so that a card fits one screen, or treating "it scrolls" as proof that a card is too long.
  - Deciding card count or card length in advance of what the explanation needs.
  - Squeezing two ideas onto one card to save screens.
  - Defending thin explanation by citing "microlearning" or "bite-sized".
- Source notes: I F8, F10; T F10, F22.

### P8. Show the learner where to look, and keep what belongs together on the same card.
- Evidence:
  - Signalling: Schneider, Beege, Nebel & Rey (2018), meta-analysis of 103 studies, 12,201 participants, highlights, bolding, colour, organisers and pointers improved retention (g about 0.53) and transfer (g about 0.33) and lowered load, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2017.11.001), `sec` (existence confirmed; numbers from a German-language clearinghouse review and a search summary). Alpizar, Adesope & Wong (2020), meta-analysis of 29 experiments (44 effect sizes), d = 0.38, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11423-020-09748-7), `abs` (one note recorded existence only).
  - Headings: Lorch & Lorch (1996), 3 experiments, headings and overviews changed recall only for complex topic structures, and when only half the topics were signalled the unsignalled topics were recalled worse than with no signals at all, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.88.1.38), `abs`. Lorch et al. (2001), headings and summaries, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1006/ceps.1999.1037), `abs`.
  - Contiguity: Ginns (2006), meta-analysis of 50 independent studies, reducing the spatial or temporal separation of related information gave gains, particularly for complex material and novices, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2006.10.001), `abs`.
  - Author versus learner emphasis: Ponce, Mayer & Méndez (2022), meta-analysis of highlighting, instructor-provided highlighting improved memory and comprehension (0.44), learner-generated highlighting improved memory (0.36) but not comprehension (0.20), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-021-09654-1), `sec` (effect sizes from a search-returned summary).
- Strength: moderate. Signalling is moderate to strong for diagrams and multimedia; for text-only cards the signalling and contiguity effects are applied by analogy and are limited.
- Boundary conditions: Signals help only if they point at what matters; a page with everything highlighted signals nothing, and signalling only some of what is taught hurts the rest. The contiguity studies mostly use diagrams and their labels; for cards the "labels" are the rule, the example and the question text, and the analogy is how far apart they sit. On a phone only one card is visible, so integration must happen inside each card. Findings on whether prior knowledge moderates signalling are inconsistent.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. In every worked case, mark the phrase in the case text that decides each answer in the key, in one consistent visual style across all seven subjects, and say in words why it decides.
  2. Every card has a heading that names its point (P7 requires 5: the fixed term once it has been taught, ordinary words on the card that introduces it) and the same layout as the other cards; the unit's structure (the ideas and how they connect) is visible at its start (P1, P30).
  3. Every idea gets the same heading and structural signal (requires 2); inside a card only the one deciding cue per worked case is highlighted, and the same style is used for it everywhere. No partial signalling: an idea is never left without the heading and place in the structure that the others have.
  4. Rule, case and reasoning sit on the same card, or on consecutive cards that carry the heading and a one-line restatement forward (P7 requires 3). When a later card relies on an earlier definition, it repeats the definition in a line rather than expecting recall or a swipe back.
  5. Emphasis is supplied by the author. Asking the learner to highlight is not a learning mechanism here (section 6). **(judgement call)** Nor is asking the learner to take notes: Dunlosky et al. (2013) did not rate note-taking, so this is a design choice, not a finding.
- FORBIDS:
  - Decorative emphasis, or bolding many things on one card.
  - Emphasis on the story rather than the structural cue.
  - Splitting a rule from its case across cards without restating the case text and the rule at the head of each following card in the sequence (P7 requires 1, P10 requires 2).
  - "See card 3" as the only definition of a term.
- Source notes: I F12, F15; T F11; P F19.

### P30. Draw the structure: show the key as a map, and put look-alike outcomes side by side in a table.
- Evidence:
  - Graphics with text: Guo, Zhang, Wright & McTigue (2020), meta-analysis of 39 experimental studies published 1985 to 2018: adding graphics to text had a moderate positive effect on reading comprehension (g = 0.39) regardless of grade level, with no significant difference among pictures, pictorial diagrams and flow diagrams, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/2332858420901696), `abs`.
  - Organisers that show relations: Robinson & Kiewra (1995), two experiments with college students: given enough time, students who studied graphic organisers learned more hierarchical and coordinate relations than students who studied outlines or the text alone, and were better at applying that knowledge and writing integrated essays, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.87.3.455), `abs`. Schroeder, Nesbit, Anguiano & Adesope (2018), meta-analysis of 142 independent effect sizes (n = 11,814): learning with concept and knowledge maps g = 0.58; studying a provided map g = 0.43 and constructing one g = 0.72, each against its comparison condition; effective in STEM and non-STEM domains, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-017-9403-9), `abs` (ERIC record).
  - What must not be added: decorative or merely interesting images, see P6 (Rey 2012; Sundararajan & Adesope 2020, where the harm depended on image type). Organisers as signals and keeping related things together, see P8 (Schneider et al. 2018; Ginns 2006).
- Strength: moderate that a graphic which shows the relations in a text helps comprehension (two meta-analyses and experiments; school and college students; printed or desktop material); limited for this app (no study of a diagnostic key shown as a map, and none of maps or tables on a phone screen).
- Boundary conditions: The benefit belongs to graphics that carry the structure of the content; the same literature finds harm from decoration (P6). The organiser experiments found the benefit when students had enough time, which self-paced cards allow. Constructing a map did better than studying one, but drawing is impractical on a phone, and studying a provided map still helped. A key is a branching structure, and neighbouring outcomes differ on the same few questions, which are the hierarchical and coordinate relations the organiser experiments tested; applying them to a key is an inference. The existing lessons show how a table fails: the comprehension audit found a card whose table was five abstract phrases with no example (Psychology 1.3) and found that the learner never sees the list of what they are learning (Psychology W7). A table summarises teaching; it cannot stand in for it.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. The subject's key is shown as a map: the questions in order, and under each answer the outcomes it keeps. At the start of the subject, and on each unit's orienting card with the part this unit teaches marked, the map is a preview: every question and outcome carries a few plain words beside its fixed label, because nothing on it has been taught yet (P1 requires 2, P4 requires 3). Wherever the key is run, the map appears in the fixed wording alone (P5). Where the whole map is not legible on a phone, it is shown one branch at a time, always in the same layout.
  2. Once two or more neighbouring outcomes have each been taught in prose with a case, they are set side by side in a table whose rows are the questions the key asks about them, in the key's order and wording, so the learner can see the one row on which they differ. In procedure units the table sets problem types side by side with the feature that tells which procedure applies; in fact units it is the grouping of P3 requires 5.
  3. A map or table never replaces the explanation. A comparison table comes after the outcomes in it have been taught; every word in it has already been taught on a card (P5 requires 2), and each cell can be understood without the others.
  4. **(judgement call)** The learner is not asked to draw a map. Constructing did better than studying in the meta-analysis, but drawing is impractical on a phone; the checks and drills supply the active part (P15, P16).
- FORBIDS:
  - A table or diagram as the only teaching of an idea, or a table of abstract phrases with no case behind it.
  - Decorative images (P6).
  - A comparison table, or the map shown where the key is run, that uses wording the cards have not taught. (The preview map on an orienting card is the one exception, and it puts plain words beside every label.)
- Source notes: none; added in the Fable 5.1 pass, with the three sources checked online (abstracts read).

### Teaching the route and the examples

### P9. Teach why each question in the key is asked, in the key's own words.
- Evidence:
  - Eiriksdottir & Catrambone (2011), literature review: specific step-by-step instructions help initial performance, while explaining the principles behind the steps helps learning and transfer (a usability versus learnability trade-off); both can be met by fading and by combining instruction types, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720811419154), `abs`.
  - Catrambone (1998), four experiments, grouping steps under labels helped learners form subgoals and solve novel problems, abstract labels tied less to surface features, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.127.4.355), `abs` in one note, existence and title only in another (flagged, section 7). Margulieux, Catrambone & Guzdial (2016), three experiments, subgoal labels helped undergraduates and in-service K-12 teachers (adults) learning programming online, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/08993408.2016.1144429), `abs`. Atkinson, Derry, Renkl & Wortham (2000), review of worked-example principles, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543070002181), `abs`.
  - Schworm & Renkl (2007), experiment with 71 student teachers, studying examples taught knowledge about argumentation, and prompts directing attention to the underlying principles were needed to build the skill itself, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.2.285), `abs`.
  - Counterweight: Carroll, Smith-Kerker, Ford & Mazur-Rimetz (1987), two experiments with adults, a briefer task-first software manual beat a conventional one, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327051hci0302_2), `abs`. van der Meij & Lazonder (1993), minimal-manual users completed about 50% more tasks, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/0953-5438(93)90001-a), `abs`.
- Strength: moderate for step-versus-principle (a review plus experiments largely from one researcher, on procedures and problem solving); limited for subgoal labels in a non-procedural classification task (none found).
- Boundary conditions: If the only aim were to get someone through a procedure today, the rationale would be dead weight, and the minimalist manuals show that. But their brevity came with real tasks from the start and explicit help recovering from errors, not with thin explanation, and the goal was operating a system, not classifying new cases. Learners prefer low effort, so a rationale that no later task asks for will be skipped. The subgoal experiments used example solutions in problem-solving tasks, not classification.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. Every question in a subject's key (the gate question and each follow-up) has a card, or a labelled part of one, that says what the question separates, which outcomes each answer keeps and rules out, and why that distinction decides between them, in the key's own wording.
  2. **(judgement call)** Each group of questions has a purpose label that says what it sorts, tied to the outcome definitions, not what the example case happens to mention. The subgoal-label evidence comes from procedural problem-solving tasks, not classification.
  3. Worked cases name each step with the key's own words for that question (P10).
  4. A later task uses the rationale (a check or drill item such as "which question separates these two outcomes?" or "which answer to this question does the case support?"), so it is not decoration the learner can skip.
  5. Rationale fades: full in early specimens, shorter later (P2, P17).
- FORBIDS:
  - Presenting the key as steps without their rationale.
  - Labelling a question group by the example's topic.
  - Reducing the rationale to a phrase.
  - Using "minimalism" to justify cutting the reasoning behind the key.
- Source notes: T F23, F24; I F20, F2.

### P10. Show a complete worked case before asking the learner to run the route alone.
- Evidence:
  - Barbieri, Miller-Cotto, Clerjuste & Chawla (2023), meta-analysis of 55 studies (181 effect sizes), worked examples beat unaided problem solving for maths performance, g about 0.48, participants from elementary school to adulthood, mostly middle-school algebra and geometry; correct examples beat incorrect ones; studies that added self-explanation prompts showed a smaller advantage, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1), `sec` (citation confirmed; numbers from two independent secondary reports because the publisher page was blocked). van Gog, Kester & Paas (2011), experiment with secondary-school novices, worked examples and example-problem pairs gave lower load and better learning than problem solving alone, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2010.10.004), `abs`. Sweller & Cooper (1985), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0201_3), `rec` (existence and title only). Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas (1998), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1022193728205), and its 2019 update, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5), `abs` (the update).
  - Recognition and classification: Rourke & Sweller (2009), two experiments, higher-education novices at recognising designers' styles did better after worked examples than after problem solving, for learners with moderate visual-literacy skills, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2008.03.006), `abs`. Quilici & Mayer (1996), three experiments, college students, studying worked statistics problems grouped by solution type led to sorting by structure rather than cover story, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.88.1.144), `abs`. Schworm & Renkl (2007), see P9.
  - Guidance beats discovery: Alfieri, Brooks, Aldrich & Tenenbaum (2011), meta-analysis of 164 studies, explicit instruction beat unassisted discovery (d about -0.38 across 580 comparisons) and enhanced, assisted discovery beat other instruction (d about 0.30 across 360), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021017), `full`. Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1), `abs`; Hmelo-Silver, Duncan & Chinn (2007), the critical response, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520701263368), `abs`. Lazonder & Harmsen (2016), meta-analysis of 72 inquiry studies, adding guidance helped (d about 0.50), [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654315627366), `abs`. Stockard et al. (2018), meta-analysis of Direct Instruction programmes (teacher-delivered), [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654317751919), `abs`. Pashler et al. (2007), IES practice guide, alternating worked-out examples with problems beat problems alone (moderate evidence), [link](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/20072004.pdf), `full`. Rosenshine (2012), see P7.
  - Problem-first challengers, for the boundary: Sinha & Kapur (2021), meta-analysis of 53 studies, solving first beat instruction first on average (g about 0.36) with reversals for grades 2-5 and domain-general skills, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105), `abs`. Loibl, Roll & Rummel (2017), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-016-9379-x), `abs`. Kapur (2008), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/07370000802212669), `abs`. Schwartz et al. (2011), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025140), `abs`. Weaver et al. (2018), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2017.12.003), `sec`. Against: Fyfe, DeCaro & Rittle-Johnson (2014), 122 second and third graders, instruction first gave better knowledge, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjep.12035), `abs`; Matlen & Klahr (2013), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-012-9248-z), `abs`. No difference: Likourezos & Kalyuga (2017), delayed transfer did not differ between fully guided worked-example, partly guided and unguided starts before instruction (the worked-example start lowered cognitive load), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-016-9399-4), `abs`.
- Strength: strong for novices in well-structured domains; moderate for classification (few recognition experiments); contested for problem-first designs.
- Boundary conditions: The advantage shrinks and reverses as learners become competent (P2). The evidence is dominated by mathematics and science problems, school-age learners and immediate or short-delay tests. The recognition result applied to learners with moderate baseline skill, so complete beginners may need more. Showing instances without pointing at the principle teaches facts about the examples, not the skill (P9, P15). Problem-first designs needed learners able to generate attempts, a teacher or peers to consolidate, explicit comparison with the canonical method, and mostly STEM classes; none was tested on adults learning alone on a phone. A fixed lesson cannot adapt its fading.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. Before the first time a learner is asked to run the key unaided in a unit, the unit contains at least one complete worked case: the unlabelled case, the first question and why it is asked, the answer and what it rules out, each following step, the name reached and the route, and why the nearest alternative fails, all in the key's wording, with the deciding cue marked (P8). In a unit that teaches only part of the key, "complete" means every key question the learner has been taught so far, in the key's order; a question that belongs to a later unit is not run.
  2. The worked case is presented as a worked case ("watch the route first"), with the rule beside the case on the same card or on consecutive cards. A worked case may run over consecutive cards in step order; each card restates the case text (or its decisive part) and the rule at its head (P7 requires 1, P8), so the rule is never separated from its case.
  3. Each step names the principle behind it (the key question's purpose) in the same words as the outcome definitions (P9).
  4. Worked cases are correct. No incorrect worked solutions for a beginner (P18).
  5. The order is: teaching cards, each followed by a short check on the one point it taught (P16 requires 2); then a complete worked case; then questions that ask the learner to run the route; then the drill. A check on a single point may come before the worked case; a question that asks for the route may not. A unit never goes from a short definition straight to a drill.
  6. **Procedure units:** the worked-example ramp is the primary design. A worked example with real numbers, step by step, appears in a card before the learner must use the procedure (the Basic Math audit found none for 15 of its 27 procedures), then a completion item, then cold (P17).
- FORBIDS:
  - Asking for an unaided determination on first exposure. (The one exception is the ungraded preview question of P1 requires 3: asked before the teaching, answered and explained at once, never scored.)
  - "Work out the key from these cases" with no guidance.
  - An example shown without its reasoning, or a worked case that skips the step ("the answer is X because it is clearly X").
- Source notes: I F1, F2, F3, F5-F7, F8; P F16; D F8.

### P11. Introduce an idea through a concrete case, then name the decisive feature in general words, then state the rule.
- Evidence:
  - Concreteness fading: Fyfe, McNeil, Son & Goldstone (2014), systematic review of mathematics and science instruction, beginning concrete and explicitly fading to the abstract, partly theoretical, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-014-9249-3), `abs`. Goldstone & Son (2005), two experiments, transfer was better when appearance switched and best when concrete elements became idealised, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327809jls1401_4), `abs`. Fyfe, McNeil & Borjas (2015), children's mathematics, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2014.10.004), `sec`.
  - Counter-evidence: Jaakkola & Veermans (2018), elementary science, concrete throughout took less time or produced better learning than fading, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-017-9428-y), `sec`. Kokkonen & Schalk (2021), conceptual analysis, fading may not be as generalisable as suggested, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09581-7), `abs`. Kaminski, Sloutsky & Heckler (2008), undergraduates learned maths better from one abstract representation, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1154659), `sec` (one-sentence summary only); De Bock et al. (2011), replication, results held but what was learned was questioned, [doi](https://doi.org/10.5951/jresematheduc.42.2.0109), `abs`; Kaminski et al. (2009), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1750-8606.2009.00096.x), `abs`.
  - Definitions need examples: Tennyson & Park (1980), review, define by critical attributes, choose examples by critical and variable attributes, arrange in sets, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543050001055), `abs`. Wissman, Zamary, Rawson & Dunlosky (2023), two experiments, application to new scenarios was better after practising examples than definitions, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000432), `abs`. Zamary & Rawson (2018), provided examples beat learner-generated ones, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-016-9396-9), `sec`. Sadoski, Goetz & Fritz (1993), four experiments, concreteness was most strongly related to comprehensibility and recall, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.85.2.291), `abs`; Sadoski, Goetz & Rodriguez (2000), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.92.1.85), `abs`.
  - Analogies: Donnelly & McDaniel (1993), four experiments, basic-level questions were answered best after literal presentation, inferential questions after analogical, strongest in novices, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.19.4.975), `abs`. Richland & Simms (2015), review, analogies need explicit support, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1002/wcs.1336), `abs`. Spiro et al. (1989), theoretical chapter, a single analogy can later become an obstacle, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1017/cbo9780511529863.023), `sec`.
- Strength: moderate that an idea needs both concrete cases and a general statement of the decisive feature (the fading experiments and the examples-versus-definitions experiments, the latter on textbook concepts of the kind this app teaches, such as positive reinforcement in psychology); limited for the case-first order itself (tested mainly in children's mathematics, and a judgement call in requires 1); contested for concrete versus abstract in general; moderate to limited for definitions plus examples; moderate for the analogy trade-off, limited for the misconception risk.
- Boundary conditions: Nearly all of this is mathematics or science representations and school-age learners; no study tested fading with real-world conceptual cases such as ideologies or scams. What was learned in an abstract form transferred to a similar abstract form, and concrete to concrete, which is a statement about matching learning to use, not about abstract being better. This app's transfer target is concrete (messy real cases), which is why concrete cases are required and the decisive feature is also stated generically so it can travel. The generated-example result concerns learning quality and efficiency; it does not show whether a personal example helps motivation or relevance (P26). Analogy results are undergraduates, science concepts and immediate tests.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. A new idea is introduced in this order: (a) a concrete, memorable real case; (b) the same case with its story stripped back to the decisive features; (c) the rule in plain words, stated as provisional until step (d); (d) a new case in a different story for the learner to classify, as a check (P16), after which the rule is confirmed as general because the same decisive feature has now been seen in two differently-storied cases (P12, P13). The card opens with the one line that links back (P3 requires 1), and the term is attached after step (c), once the learner has the idea in ordinary words (P4 requires 1). Step (d) asks about this one feature, never about the whole route (P10 requires 5). **(judgement call)** The stripped-case step (b) and the exact four-step order are design choices: concreteness fading is tested mainly in children's mathematics and is contested in general.
  2. A definition is never the only treatment of an outcome. Each outcome has at least one worked example, and the learner's practice centres on applying the idea to a new case, not repeating the definition.
  3. The decisive feature is stated generically and shown concretely in at least two different cases (P13).
  4. An analogy, where one is used, gives the shape of a mechanism and then the literal rule is stated, with an explicit mapping (what corresponds to what) and an explicit statement of where it breaks. It never replaces the literal definition.
- FORBIDS:
  - A bare definition as the first thing on a card.
  - Abstract-only teaching.
  - An analogy standing in for a definition, or appearing in a key question or option.
  - Replacing provided examples with learner-invented ones.
- Source notes: D F13, F14, F27; T F12, F13.

### P12. Teach by contrast: cases in pairs, with the learner told what to compare.
- Evidence:
  - Alfieri, Nokes-Malach & Schunn (2013), meta-analysis of 57 experiments (336 tests), case comparison beat sequential study, single cases, non-analogous cases and traditional instruction (d about 0.50, 95% CI 0.44 to 0.56); four moderators of fifteen mattered (objective, whether a principle was given, content, test delay); asking learners to find similarities, giving the principle after the comparison, perceptual content and immediate testing went with larger effects, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2013.775712), `abs`.
  - Adult novices: Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson (2003), three studies, comparing two cases beat studying them separately and raised later use of the strategy in live negotiation, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.95.2.393), `abs`. Loewenstein, Thompson & Gentner (1999), graduate management students who drew an analogy between two cases were nearly three times more likely to use the strategy, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03212967), `abs`. Gick & Holyoak (1983), a principle or diagram alone did not produce the analogy from one story but did with two, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(83)90002-6), `abs`.
  - What to compare: Rittle-Johnson & Star (2009), 162 seventh and eighth graders, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0014224), `abs`; Rittle-Johnson, Star & Durkin (2009), 236 seventh and eighth graders, students who did not attempt algebraic methods at pretest benefited most from studying examples sequentially or from comparing problem types, rather than from comparing solution methods (for true novices, comparing problem types did not beat sequential study), while students who did attempt them learned more from comparing solution methods, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016026), `abs`. Kok et al. (2015), randomised experiment with 84 medical students, comparing same-disease and different-disease cases both gave efficient learning, yet eye tracking showed students compared normal anatomy in 45.8% of trials and few abnormalities, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.acra.2015.04.012), `abs`.
  - Mechanism: Kang & Pashler (2012), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1801), `abs`; Birnbaum, Kornell, Bjork & Bjork (2013), [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-012-0272-7), `abs`; Carvalho & Goldstone (2017), attention goes to what differs between successive items when interleaved and to what they share when blocked, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/xlm0000406), `abs`. Quilici & Mayer (1996, 2002), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.88.1.144), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.796), `abs`.
- Strength: moderate for comparison in general (one meta-analysis and independent experiments including adult novices agree in direction, with weaker effects on delayed tests; not several independent meta-analyses); moderate for structure-versus-surface; limited for the order of pairs.
- Boundary conditions: Effects were larger with immediate than delayed tests, so durability is the weaker part. The moderators are cross-study contrasts and can be confounded. "Find the similarities" (comparison literature) and "see the differences between categories" (interleaving literature) may not conflict when applied to different pairings: similarity-seeking across two different-looking cases of one outcome builds the schema, difference-seeking across look-alikes from neighbouring outcomes builds the boundary. This is the notes' reading and is untested. The comparison meta-analysis found larger effects when learners were asked to find similarities, so the difference-finding instruction in REQUIRES 1(b) rests on the interleaving mechanism (Carvalho & Goldstone 2017; Birnbaum et al. 2013), not on that meta-analysis. Putting the same-outcome pair before the look-alike pair for a beginner is by analogy to the equation-solving result, not tested on classification. "Principle after comparison" is a within-comparison moderator, not a head-to-head test against explaining first for complete novices (see section 4). Most adult studies are negotiation, problem solving, school mathematics and radiology, not classifying political, psychological or fraud cases.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. For each outcome the unit gives (a) a pair of different-looking cases that belong to the same outcome and (b) a pair of look-alike cases that belong to different outcomes, the second being its closest neighbour in the key. The look-alike pair is shown only when both of its outcomes have been introduced on their own (P22 requires 1); for the first outcome a unit teaches, it waits until the neighbour has been taught. In procedure units read "type of problem" for "outcome": two different-looking problems that call for the same procedure, and two look-alike problems that call for different ones. **(judgement call for (b))** The look-alike pair asks for difference-finding, which the comparison meta-analysis does not directly support (see boundary conditions).
  2. Each pair carries an explicit instruction naming the feature to compare (the one the key tests) and one question about it ("which answer to the key's question does each case give?"). The difference or sameness is then stated in plain words, in the key's words, after the comparison (P15).
  3. **(judgement call)** For a true beginner the same-outcome pair comes before the look-alike pair.
  4. Nothing unrelated is inserted between the two cases of a pair.
- FORBIDS:
  - A heading like "compare these two" with no instruction.
  - A principle presented as general after a single case, with no second, differently-storied case. (The rule may be stated provisionally after the first case, as P11 requires 1 allows; it is confirmed as general only after the second.)
  - Pairs that differ on several features at once without saying which one decides.
- Source notes: D F6, F7, F2; I F19; T F12.

### P13. Vary the story while the decisive feature stays fixed, and move from clean cases to messy ones.
- Evidence:
  - Structure versus surface: Chi, Feltovich & Glaser (1981), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0502_2), `abs`. Quilici & Mayer (1996), three experiments, structure-emphasising examples led to more correct choices of the statistical test, an effect strong for lower-ability and not for higher-ability students, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.88.1.144), `abs`; Quilici & Mayer (2002), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.796), `abs`.
  - Variety needs structure first: Braithwaite & Goldstone (2015), two experiments, learners with less prior knowledge did better with similar examples, those with more with varied ones, and brief training on the underlying relations removed the reversal, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/07370008.2015.1067215), `abs`. Raviv, Lupyan & Green (2022), review, variability makes early learning harder but eventually gives more general performance, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2022.03.007), `abs`. Paas & van Merriënboer (1994), four computer-based training strategies for geometrical problem solving (low or high variability, each with conventional practice or with worked examples): students who studied worked examples gained most from high-variability examples, invested less time and mental effort in practice and reached better, less effortful transfer than students who first attempted conventional problems, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.86.1.122), `abs`. Pashler et al. (2007), see P10, `full`.
  - Clean cases: Hornsby & Love (2014), idealised training on unambiguous mammograms beat a representative mix on a range of test cases but not on cases very unlike training, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jarmac.2014.04.009), `abs`. Kulatunga-Moruzi, Brooks & Norman (2001), 12 preclinical students, similarity to past cases helped atypical cases, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15328015tlm1302_6), `abs`.
  - Classification practice with new exemplars: Jacoby, Wahlheim & Coane (2010), three experiments, bird families, testing improved classification of new exemplars, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0020636), `abs`. Wahlheim, Dunlosky & Jacoby (2011), spacing helped more when birds were shown in pairs that highlight differences, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-010-0063-y), `abs`.
  - Pan & Rickard (2018), see P5, `abs`/`full`.
- Strength: moderate for structure-versus-surface and for variety after structure; limited for the clean-to-messy order (one binary mammogram experiment and one 12-student study).
- Boundary conditions: Idealised training did not remove the need to meet messy cases. Sorting is a proxy for classifying, not naming a case on the spot. Nobody gives a schedule for how fast to move from clean to varied to misleading. Paas & van Merriënboer (1994) supports varied examples inside worked-example study; the variability benefit was reported for the worked-example groups, so variety is a feature of study cases and not a reason to put hard problems first.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. The lesson teaches, in words, what counts as the structural feature and what counts as surface in this subject (in Statistical Claims, what is being compared with what, as against whether the claim is about health or money), and says which dimension stays constant and which is meant to change. In procedure units the story is the wording and setting of the problem, and the structural feature is whatever tells which procedure applies.
  2. Inside a unit the cases run: first a clean case with a simple story where the decisive feature is clear; then the same feature in a very different story; only later cases where the story misleads. Do not open with the widest spread. **(judgement call)** The clean-to-messy order rests on one binary mammogram experiment and one 12-student study; tune it on first-attempt accuracy for near-transfer items.
  3. Practice and specimen cases differ from the lesson's own examples in setting and surface detail. The "why" text names the structural feature, not a retelling of the story.
  4. Messy cases are taught as named, remembered cases ("this looks like Y but is X, because ...") so the learner has something specific to be reminded of.
  5. Case banks are large enough that a learner who returns across days meets few repeats, because repeated specimens train the memory of the story. How many cases per outcome are enough is not answered by the evidence.
- FORBIDS:
  - Opening a unit with the widest possible spread of cases, or stopping at the tidy case.
  - "Why" text that retells the case.
  - Reusing a lesson example as the drill's case.
- Source notes: D F9, F10, F12; P F22, F4.

### P14. Teach what each outcome is like, including its exceptions, and teach the learner to check the key's answer against their overall impression of the case.
- Evidence:
  - The classification task narrows attention: Markman & Ross (2003), review, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.129.4.592), `abs`. Hoffman & Rehder (2010), two experiments with eye tracking, classification learners focused on diagnostic features and were worse at drawing a distinction outside training, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019042), `abs`. Carvalho & Goldstone (2017), see P12.
  - Explaining flattens exceptions: Williams & Lombrozo (2010), three experiments, explaining why items belonged to categories made learners more likely to find the broad regularity, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1551-6709.2010.01113.x), `abs`. Williams, Lombrozo & Rehder (2013), two experiments, the same tendency made learners overgeneralise when the pattern had exceptions, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030996), `abs`.
  - Two checks beat one: Ark, Brooks & Eva (2007), 48 novices learning ECGs, contrastive instruction and combined feature-check-plus-familiarity instruction both beat no instructions and held after a week, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2929.2007.02688.x), `abs`. Eva, Hatala, LeBlanc & Brooks (2007), two studies, combined instruction helped when cases were made misleading, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2923.2007.02923.x), `abs`. Kulatunga-Moruzi et al. (2001), see P13. Allen & Brooks (1991), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.120.1.3), `sec` (opening of the abstract only).
  - "Be careful" does not work: Sherbino et al. (2014), controlled trial, 191 medical students, cognitive forcing strategies changed nothing, [doi](https://doi.org/10.2310/8000.2013.130860), `abs`. Norman et al. (2014), residents told to be quick or careful scored 44.5% versus 45.0%, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1097/ACM.0000000000000105), `abs` (two cohorts, not one randomised experiment). Norman et al. (2017), narrative review, bias-recognition teaching has not reduced errors while knowledge-reorganising strategies have small consistent benefits, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1097/ACM.0000000000001421), `abs`. Lambe et al. (2016), systematic review of 28 studies, guided reflection was the most consistently successful, cognitive forcing helped in some, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2015-004417), `abs`.
- Strength: moderate for describing each outcome beyond the features that separate it, and for teaching exceptions on purpose (laboratory experiments, mostly with artificial categories); limited for the two checks (several experiments from one tradition, ECGs and medical trainees, no replication outside medicine, applied here to text cases by analogy).
- Boundary conditions: The studies used a visual task where a feature check and an overall impression are both available; for text cases the overall impression may be less reliable. The best mix depends on expertise (novices did better with resemblance to past cases, residents with an analytic strategy). The narrowing results use artificial categories; in everyday domains with rich prior knowledge the narrowing may be milder, but no test was found. Lambe's positive results and the null trials reconcile as structured steps (guided reflection) versus generic reminders.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. Each outcome gets a general description with its contrasts: what it is, its typical features and logic, and what it looks like in the wild, not only the features the key uses to separate it from neighbours.
  2. Some tasks run the other way: given the outcome, what would you expect to see, or which detail does not fit?
  3. Each outcome with known exceptions, hybrids or near-misses has them taught explicitly and separately, with a prompt to explain why this case is X even though it has the feature that usually means Y. The caveats are taught, not appended. Where the aim is to remember details (an outcome's typical claims), ask the learner to describe or list rather than explain.
  4. **(judgement call)** The lesson teaches both checks and says so: run the key's questions on the features, and notice whether the case resembles ones you have seen; when they disagree, recheck the feature that decided it. The route is the feature check; the name is the overall judgement; the overall impression is a signal to check, neither ignored nor trusted blindly. A beginner's overall impression can only draw on the cases the lessons have shown, so the feature check leads, and the resemblance check grows with the named cases of P13 requires 4.
- FORBIDS:
  - A card whose teaching is "slow down" or "beware confirmation bias".
  - Describing an outcome only by the key's separating features.
  - Appending exceptions as an afterthought.
- Source notes: D F11, F17, F19, F21; P F22.

### P29. Name the wrong idea the learner is likely to bring, mark it wrong, and give the correct reasoning.
- Evidence:
  - Schroeder & Kucera (2022), meta-analysis of 44 independent comparisons (n = 3,869): refutation text, which states a common misconception, says it is wrong and gives the correct explanation, went with a positive, moderate effect (g = 0.41) compared with other learning conditions, which the authors call consistent and robust across a wide variety of contexts, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-021-09656-z), `abs`.
  - Wrong statements need safeguards: Roediger & Marsh (2005) and Butler & Roediger (2008), see P18, wrong statements read without feedback can be learned as false facts, and feedback reduces that. Metcalfe (2017), see P18, the corrective feedback has to analyse the reasoning behind the mistake.
- Strength: moderate that refutation text helps (one meta-analysis, framed by its authors around scientific misconceptions); limited for this app's subjects.
- Boundary conditions: Nothing found tests refutation text on political, financial, psychological-folk or scam beliefs, which is where adults hold strong wrong everyday ideas ("correlation shows cause", "this scam only fools gullible people", "the president can simply decide this"). The learner is not a blank slate in these subjects: everyday life is where the wrong ideas come from, so a principle written only for a beginner who "assumes nothing" (P2) would miss them. Which wrong ideas learners hold in this app's subjects is not known from the literature; the author has to find them, and the app's own error data and the cold readers of P27 are the source. A refutation card repeats a wrong statement, which is why P18 requires 3 applies to it.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. For each outcome or key question whose wrong everyday reading is known, one card states the tempting idea as a learner would put it, marks it clearly as wrong, and then gives the correct reasoning in the key's words (P3, P5), in that order and in the same card, with the correction as the last thing read (P18 requires 3).
  2. A later check or drill item tests that exact contrast, the tempting idea against the correct one, answered from memory and explained at once (P16, P19).
  3. Wrong everyday ideas are found, not guessed. Before the app has learner data, the sources are published accounts of common misunderstandings in the subject (looked up and named in the unit's build notes, not recalled from memory) and what the cold readers get wrong (P27). Once it has data, the source is the wrong options learners choose most often per item (P17 requires 4).
- FORBIDS:
  - A wrong idea stated without being marked wrong in the same card, or left as the last thing read (P18).
  - Opening a unit for a beginner with the wrong idea (P18 requires 5).
  - A refutation card that no later item tests.
- Source notes: none; added in the 2026-10-04 revision from problem 6 of `docs/learning-science/review-claims.md`, with the evidence checked online.

### First practice

### P15. Make the learner commit to a reason before showing them the model reason (the lesson's own worked-out reason).
- Evidence:
  - Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi & Winne (2018), meta-analysis of 64 reports (69 effect sizes), prompting self-explanation, g about 0.55 across solving, studying worked problems and studying text; by level g about 0.48 (elementary), 0.43 (high school), 0.61 (undergraduate), 0.68 (professional programmes); better than receiving an instructor's explanation by about g = 0.35; conceptual prompts worked, metacognitive prompts (about the learner's own planning or performance) did not; the two studies with multiple-choice prompts averaged g = 0.24, not reliably above zero; publication bias judged not a plausible threat, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x), `full`.
  - Chi, Bassok, Lewis, Reimann & Glaser (1989), observational comparison of good and poor students (correlational), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1302_1), `abs`. Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu & LaVancher (1994), experiment with eighth graders, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1803_3), `abs`. Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill (2003), two experiments, prompts to name the principle behind each step plus fading gave medium to large effects on near and far transfer without extra time, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.95.4.774), `abs`. Schworm & Renkl (2007), see P9.
  - Constraints: Rittle-Johnson & Loehr (2017), review, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-016-1079-5), `abs`. McEldoon, Durkin & Rittle-Johnson (2013), 69 children, modest and patchy gains over extra practice problems, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8279.2012.02083.x), `abs`. Chamberland et al. (2015), randomised experiment, 54 third-year medical students, explaining cases themselves and then listening to residents' explanations with specific prompts did better a week later than a control group (and, on different cases, than the same design without prompts); the abstract reports no benefit for the no-prompt group, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/medu.12623), `abs`. Dunlosky et al. (2013), self-explanation and elaborative interrogation rated moderate utility, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266), `full`.
  - Generation: Slamecka & Graf (1978), five experiments, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.4.6.592), `abs`. Bertsch et al. (2007), meta-analysis of 86 studies (445 effect sizes), mean benefit 0.40, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03193441), `abs`. McCurdy et al. (2020), meta-analysis of 126 articles, the effect depends on how tightly the response is cued, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-020-01762-3), `abs` (read via the authors' preprint).
- Strength: strong for the average effect on learning; moderate for how to prompt; limited for tap-to-choose replies (two studies with multiple-choice prompts, g = 0.24, not reliably above zero), which is the format a phone favours (requires 4).
- Boundary conditions: Barbieri et al. found the worked-example advantage smaller when prompts were added; the literature does not resolve why (time or load are candidates). Prompts about the learner's own feelings or confidence do not help; prompts about the content do. Availability of an on-demand explanation reduced the effort put into explaining. Self-explanation takes longer. No study tested typing an explanation on a phone. Explaining pushes toward the general rule and flattens exceptions (P14). The generation effect is a laboratory finding on words and simple material, and a generated wrong answer with no feedback is not covered; it needs a tightly cued prompt answerable from what was just taught.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. After a worked case, and after a contrasting pair, the learner is asked which detail decided a given answer and why, in the "why this and not that" form, with the same wording every time. The prompt targets the content (the cue, the key question, the nearest neighbour), never the learner's confidence or feelings.
  2. The prompt is tightly cued and answerable from what was just taught.
  3. The learner commits before the model reason appears. The model reason is never an optional hint opened first; it appears immediately after the learner's reply (P19).
  4. **(judgement call)** Format. A tap on the deciding phrase or a "because" choice is cheap on a phone, but the evidence for multiple-choice prompts is weak (two studies, g = 0.24, not reliably above zero); a one-sentence free reply is better supported and costs typing. Either is permitted; whichever is used, the model explanation must follow at once, and a free reply should be one sentence.
  5. The prompt sits on the step that carries the idea, not at the end of every card.
- FORBIDS:
  - Showing the model reason first, or making it available as a hint beforehand.
  - Prompts about self-assessment ("how well do you understand this?").
  - Open prompts with no stated target and no explanation after.
- Source notes: I F18; D F15, F16, F18; P F15.

### P16. Follow teaching with answering from memory: short checks between cards and a real drill after.
- Evidence:
  - Retrieval versus restudy: Roediger & Karpicke (2006), two experiments, restudy won at 5 minutes but testing won at 2 days and 1 week, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x), `abs`. Rowland (2014), meta-analysis, g = 0.50 (159 effect sizes), g = 0.41 when the final test came within a day and 0.69 when a day or more later, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559), `full`. Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017), meta-analysis, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306), `abs` (the quoted figures 118 articles, 272 effect sizes, g = 0.61 are `sec`). Yang et al. (2021), classroom meta-analysis of 222 studies (48,478 students), g = 0.499, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000309), `abs`. Agarwal, Nunes & Blunt (2021), systematic review of classrooms, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-021-09595-9), `abs`. Dunlosky et al. (2013), practice testing rated high utility, `full`. Pashler et al. (2007), quizzes to re-expose students to key content got the top evidence rating, `full`.
  - Complex material: van Gog & Sweller (2015), the testing effect decreases or disappears as element interactivity rises, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9310-x), `abs`. Replies: Karpicke & Aue (2015) argue that van Gog and Sweller's review omitted studies showing retrieval-practice effects with complex materials, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9309-3), `sec` (search-returned summary); Rawson (2015) argues that the conclusion that the testing effect fails for complex materials is not warranted by the outcomes reported or by prior evidence, while calling for more work on when and why the effect is limited, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9308-4), `sec` (search-returned abstract). Karpicke & Blunt (2011), two experiments with 80 and 120 undergraduates, free recall beat concept mapping a week later (d = 1.50 and 1.07), students predicted the opposite, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199327), `full`.
  - Interim checks: Szpunar, McDermott & Roediger (2008), four experiments, testing during study insulates against proactive interference, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0013082), `abs`. Chan, Meissner & Davis (2018), meta-analysis, testing potentiates later learning of new material, with conditions under which it impairs, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000166) and correction [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000312), `abs`. Yang & Shanks (2018), inductive learning of painting styles, interim classification tests nearly doubled accuracy on new paintings, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/xlm0000449), `abs`. Yang, Potts & Shanks (2018), review, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-018-0024-y), `abs`. Rosenshine (2012), check understanding often, `full`.
- Strength: strong that answering beats rereading for durable learning; moderate for interim checks (a meta-analysis and several experiments, mostly lists and text, one inductive category study); contested whether it holds for complex multi-step material.
- Boundary conditions: The advantage can reverse at very short delays. Heterogeneity is large and the control condition changes the size. Samples are overwhelmingly college students. Retrieval needs initial success or feedback (P17, P19): without feedback, at 50% correct or less there was no reliable effect (g = 0.03). Pan & Rickard (2018), see P5, found transfer of testing weakest to problems involving worked examples and among the greatest to problems involving medical diagnoses (their abstract names both). Their problem-solving category is split: the author manuscript, as read by the claims review and not re-read here, gives d about 0.05 for worked-example circuit troubleshooting and about 0.59 for medical-diagnosis scenarios, a significant difference. The diagnostic result is the nearer match to this app's task and favours retrieval practice on whole cases, so that source does not support practising the route in pieces; the case for pieces before the whole rests on van Gog & Sweller (2015) and the element-interactivity argument, whose dispute is unresolved (section 4.1). Testing can impair new learning under conditions the note did not read. No evidence says how often interim checks are best.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. Every unit ends with a drill in which the learner answers before seeing the answer, with the cards out of view. Review of older material is the same kind of event (answer, then see), never "show the card again".
  2. Between cards there are short checks on what was just taught: a single key question, or a single feature, on a new case, answerable from the cards just read and followed at once by its explanation (P19, P20). A check asks about one point, never about the whole route (P10 requires 5). Where the checks sit is fixed by section 4.3: one after each outcome has been introduced and one after each key question has been taught, so the learner never reads through more than one step of a unit without using it. **(judgement call)** Whether to add more than that is not settled by the evidence; keep them light enough that they do not feel like a test on every screen.
  3. The answer to a check never sits in the first line of the next card, which would turn it back into rereading.
  4. The drill's share of the learner's time is substantial. **(judgement call)** A unit with full cards and a token drill has too little practice, and the fix is more practice, never shorter cards; no ratio is supported.
  5. Drill items first cover the pieces (one key question at a time, one outcome's features at a time), then the whole route; the full determination on specimens comes last (P17). The support for pieces before the whole is van Gog & Sweller (2015) alone, and that argument is disputed.
- FORBIDS:
  - Review by re-showing cards.
  - Highlighting or summarising as a learning mechanism (Dunlosky et al. 2013 rate both low utility). **(judgement call)** The app does not ask the learner to take notes either: Dunlosky et al. did not rate note-taking, so that is a design choice, not a finding.
  - A drill item shown before an attempt, or a check whose answer is on screen.
  - A check or preview question the learner cannot answer from what was taught. (The one exception is the ungraded preview question of P1 requires 3, which is answered and explained at once.)
- Source notes: P F1, F5, F15, F21; I F8.

### P17. Build each drill as a ramp: first the route is shown and the learner finishes it, last they run it alone; pieces come before the whole, and most first attempts should succeed.
- Evidence:
  - Fading: Paas (1992), worked-out and completion problems both beat conventional problems for transfer, the worked strategy most clearly, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.84.4.429), `abs`. van Gog, Kester & Paas (2011), see P10. Renkl, Atkinson, Maier & Staley (2002), three experiments, smooth transitions from example to problem, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/00220970209599510), `abs`. Atkinson, Renkl & Merrill (2003), see P15. Renkl & Atkinson (2003), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep3801_3), `abs`. Pashler et al. (2007), fading examples into problems step by step helps, `full`.
  - Success and feedback: Rowland (2014), see P16: with feedback the testing effect was g = 0.73, without g = 0.39; without feedback it depended on initial success (50% or less, g = 0.03; 51 to 75%, g = 0.29; above 75%, g = 0.56); with feedback, low initial success went with larger effects (g = 0.99 under 50%, 0.68 for 51 to 75%, 0.40 above 75%; a small subset, and the author reads it as retrieval effort), `full`. Pan & Rickard (2018), see P5: transfer to new problems was predicted by initial test accuracy (together with response congruency and elaborated practice), `abs`. Rosenshine (2012), a success rate of roughly 80 percent appeared optimal (82 versus 73 percent in one fourth-grade study), `full`.
  - Mastery and targets: Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990), meta-analysis of 108 mastery-learning evaluations: mastery programmes had positive effects on examination performance in colleges, high schools and upper elementary grades (average effect size about 0.52 in secondary summaries; stronger for weaker students), and self-paced mastery programmes often reduced completion rates in college classes, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543060002265), `abs` for the direction, `sec` for the 0.52. Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia & Cohen (2019), the "85 percent rule", a mathematical derivation for neural-network learners with no human education data, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-12552-4), `abs`.
  - Parts and whole: Wickens, Hutchins, Carolan & Cumming (2013), meta-analyses of 37 transfer studies of training: practising a task in parts generally gave negative transfer when the parts are performed at the same time in the whole task, but not when they are performed in sequence; raising difficulty helped when the increases were adapted to the learner and not when they came in fixed steps; experienced learners benefited less from both, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720812451994), `abs`. Pollock, Chandler & Sweller (2002), see P4, `abs`.
  - Difficulty framework: Schmidt & Bjork (1992), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1992.tb00029.x), `abs`. Soderstrom & Bjork (2015), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615569000), `abs`.
- Strength: moderate for fading the support (a worked case, then a case with part left out, then a case alone: several experiments, mostly one research group, no meta-analysis of fading); limited for pieces before the whole (it rests on the disputed complex-material argument of P16, with indirect support from parts-then-whole study in Pollock et al. 2002 and from the finding that part practice does no harm when the parts are done in sequence, as a key's questions are); limited for any exact accuracy number.
- Boundary conditions: Far transfer needed the principle-naming prompts (P15). The 80% figure is an association from a few elementary maths studies, and 85% is a model of idealised learners, a hypothesis for human classification learning, not a finding. The success bands in Rowland are between-study comparisons, and the 50% line is a warning for practice without feedback; this app always gives feedback, so low accuracy is a teaching-defect signal rather than a statistical cliff. There is "no validated target accuracy" for this app. Overlearning inside one sitting is inefficient (P24). The training meta-analysis (Wickens et al. 2013; mostly skilled-performance tasks, not classification) found no benefit from raising difficulty in fixed steps, so the ramp is justified by fading support for a beginner, which is the worked-example evidence, not by a general rule that easy-before-hard helps; that is one more reason to tune it on the app's own data (requires 4).
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. Inside a unit, drill items rise in difficulty: (a) the route is shown and the learner supplies the name or one answer; (b) part of the route is left out; (c) the whole route, cold, on a new case.
  2. Because the app scores name and route separately, "route given, name asked" is the easiest rung and "route and name both asked" the hardest.
  3. A worked case precedes the first rung (P10).
  4. First-attempt accuracy is recorded per item. **(judgement call)** A unit whose early-rung items sit at or below about half is treated as under-taught, and the fix is clearer teaching or an earlier worked example, not more drill; aim for most early-rung items answered correctly on the first try. The numbers are starting values to tune on real learner data. Mixed items (P22) will score lower and are not failures. The evidence points two ways: with feedback, low initial accuracy went with larger retention effects (Rowland 2014, a small subset), while transfer to new problems rose with initial accuracy (Pan & Rickard 2018). Because the target is transfer (P25), aim for most early-rung first attempts correct; low accuracy is a flag to investigate, not a proven harm, and the risk it guards against (dropout, P28) is untested.
  5. **(judgement call)** No hard gate by default. Mastery learning is the better-supported design for learning itself (Kulik et al. 1990: positive effects on examination performance, larger for weaker students), but self-paced mastery programmes often lowered completion in college classes, and no study of gating the next unit in a self-paced, no-stakes app was found. The reason for no hard gate is the completion risk, not a finding against mastery. When first-attempt accuracy on a prerequisite unit's items is low, the app shows a soft prompt ("review these first"), schedules those items (P24), and lets the learner continue. Tune on completion data (P28).
- FORBIDS:
  - A flat drill of equal-difficulty items, or asking for the whole route cold before the pieces.
  - More repetitions as the response to a unit that is hard to answer because it was hard to understand.
  - Citing 85 percent, or any single accuracy number, as established.
- Source notes: I F3, F8; P F2, F5, F16, F17, F18.

### P18. Use wrong options and faulty claims only with feedback and only after the right reasoning is in place.
- Evidence:
  - Multiple choice: Roediger & Marsh (2005), a multiple-choice test helps later performance but students also pick up false facts from lures, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.31.5.1155), `abs`. Marsh, Roediger, Bjork & Bjork (2007), [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03194051), `abs`. Butler & Roediger (2008), feedback raised correct answers and reduced lure intrusions, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/MC.36.3.604), `abs`. Little, Bjork, Bjork & Angello (2012), two experiments, with plausible alternatives multiple-choice tests fostered retention of information about the wrong alternatives too, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797612443370), `abs`. Pashler et al. (2007), the IES panel prefers recall formats to multiple choice, `full`.
  - Error examples: Booth, Lange, Koedinger & Newton (2013), two classroom experiments in algebra tutoring, explaining incorrect examples may have been especially beneficial for conceptual understanding, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2012.11.002), `abs`. Große & Renkl (2007), two experiments on probability, mixed correct and incorrect solutions improved far transfer only for learners with favourable prior knowledge, poor-knowledge learners did better with correct solutions only, and incorrect solutions reduced principle-based self-explanation, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2007.09.008), `abs`. Barbieri et al. (2023), correct examples outperformed incorrect, see P10, `sec`. Metcalfe (2017), review, corrective feedback including analysis of the reasoning that led to the mistake is what makes errorful learning beneficial, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-010416-044022), `abs`.
- Strength: strong that multiple choice with plausible alternatives and feedback works; contested for error examples with novices: two studies from different domains and age groups suggest that prior knowledge decides (consistent with P2, but a reading of two studies, not a settled result), so the app places faulty-claim items after correct reasoning and a drill (requires 4).
- Boundary conditions: The lure harm was measured on general-knowledge facts after a delay with no feedback; the Little et al. benefit depends on the alternatives being plausible. Whether the same applies when every option is a fixed list of outcome names (this app's shared option list) was not tested. The IES panel prefers recall formats and one secondary description of Adesope et al. says multiple choice gave larger effects, which conflicts; the format question is open (section 7), and typing is costly on a phone. Error examples can crowd out explanation of the principle. The Booth and Große results disagree on novices.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. Multiple-choice options in classification drills are the real, named outcomes the learner may confuse (the shared option list), never a false statement dressed as a fact. Every wrong option is a plausible competitor.
  2. The explanation after an item names why the right option is right and why the most tempting wrong option fails, in the lesson's words (P20).
  3. A wrong statement appears in a card only if it is clearly marked wrong and the correction follows in the same card. Teaching a likely wrong everyday idea on purpose is P29, which follows this rule.
  4. The faulty-claims drill (errDrill) comes after the learner has met the correct reasoning and has done at least one drill. It asks the learner to commit to a diagnosis first (for example, choose which diagnostic question the claim ignores) before the fault is revealed, and its feedback shows the fault and the corrected claim together, with the corrected claim last.
  5. No unit opens for a beginner with wrong examples.
- FORBIDS:
  - A distractor that is a false statement.
  - A "show the fault" control the learner can press without attempting.
  - Leaving the faulty wording as the last thing read.
- Source notes: P F6; I F21; T F20.

### Feedback

### P19. Give every answer an immediate explanation, right or wrong.
- Evidence:
  - Feedback carries much of the learning from a practice item: Rowland (2014), see P16, feedback g = 0.73 versus 0.39 without, `full`. Butler & Roediger (2008), see P18. Butler, Karpicke & Roediger (2008), two experiments, feedback on correct answers roughly doubled retention of answers given with low confidence, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.34.4.918), `abs`. Butler, Godbole & Marsh (2013), two experiments on prose passages, feedback explaining why the answer was correct gave equal performance on repeated questions and better performance on new inference questions than the answer alone, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031026), `abs`.
  - Meta-analyses: Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020), 435 studies, over 61,000 learners, d = 0.48 with large heterogeneity; by type, reinforcement or punishment d = 0.24, corrective feedback d = 0.46, high-information feedback d = 0.99, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087), `abs` (effect sizes by type read in the open-access full text). Van der Kleij, Feskens & Eggen (2015), 40 studies (70 effect sizes) of computer-based feedback: elaborated feedback 0.49, knowledge of the correct response 0.32, correct/incorrect only 0.05, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314564881), `abs`. Bangert-Drowns et al. (1991), [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543061002213), `abs`. Hattie & Timperley (2007), [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/003465430298487), `abs`. Shute (2008), see P2, `full`.
  - Timing: Kulik & Kulik (1988), meta-analysis of 53 studies, applied classroom studies mostly favoured immediate feedback, laboratory studies of test content mostly favoured delay, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543058001079), `abs`. Butler, Karpicke & Roediger (2007), two experiments, delayed feedback gave better final performance on multiple-choice material (attributed to spacing), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898X.13.4.273), `abs`. Hays, Kornell & Bjork (2013), three experiments, after a failed test immediate feedback produced the benefit and delayed feedback did not, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0028468), `abs`. Mullet et al. (2014), two experiments in an upper-level college course, feedback a week late gave better exam performance on new problems though students preferred immediate feedback, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jarmac.2014.05.001), `abs`. Attali & van der Kleij (2017), randomised experiment with 2,445 participants, immediate feedback followed by a delayed review gave higher performance than delayed feedback after incorrect first responses but lower performance after correct first responses (immediate feedback without the review was lower in both cases), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2017.03.012), `sec` (repository abstract). Rowland (2014) found delayed larger than immediate (g = 1.38, k = 6 versus 0.66, k = 46) but flagged it as thin and confounded with retention interval.
- Strength: strong that feedback matters and that explanation beats a bare verdict; contested on immediate versus delayed.
- Boundary conditions: Timing results disagree across studies. The delay advantage is plausibly a spacing effect, not a feedback effect, and the delay studies mostly used undergraduates and delays of a day to a week. The immediate-feedback advantage is strongest for difficult new material and weaker learners (Shute). Students prefer immediate feedback and judge it more helpful even when delay gives better transfer, so preference is a poor guide. Feedback can hurt competent learners (P2). Many of the studies are prose passages, general-knowledge facts or school tasks, not classification routes.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. No item (drill item, in-card check, specimen step, determination, faulty-claim item, preview question) is ever scored without an explanation shown immediately after it, for right answers as well as wrong ones. Never end at "Incorrect".
  2. In the unit drills the first explanation is immediate. The benefit of delay is obtained by bringing the item back later (P24), not by withholding the first explanation. This is the compromise the evidence best supports for new difficult material (immediate feedback plus a later review), but it rests on one large study that found it better than delayed feedback after incorrect first responses and worse after correct first responses (Attali & van der Kleij 2017); that is one more reason a correct answer gets only the short recap of P21 requires 1.
  3. The stored explanation text (the specimen why and fals text, the drill w text, the errDrill fault text) is the feedback, and it is written as reasoning (P20), not as a verdict label.
- FORBIDS:
  - Verdict-only feedback.
  - Withholding the explanation to "make the learner think".
  - Delayed-only feedback for first-time items.
- Source notes: P F2, F3; T F14, F17.

### P20. Write feedback as information about the reasoning, decisive sentence first, never as praise and never as a bare "right" or "wrong".
- Evidence:
  - Task over person: Kluger & DeNisi (1996), meta-analysis of 607 effect sizes (23,663 observations), feedback improved performance on average (d = 0.41) but more than a third of interventions decreased it, and effectiveness fell as feedback moved attention toward the self, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254), `abs`. Wisniewski et al. (2020), see P19, weaker effects on motivational than on cognitive outcomes (d = 0.33 against 0.51). Hattie & Timperley (2007), levels of feedback (task, process, self-regulation, self), the first three being most beneficial, `sec` for the level descriptions (read in educational-authority summaries). Shute (2008), guidelines: focus on the task, use praise sparingly if at all, avoid comparisons with others and overall grades, `full`.
  - Manageable pieces: Shute (2008) calls the findings on feedback complexity inconsistent. She reports a meta-analysis of 15 experimental studies of computer-based and programmed instruction (Schimmel 1983, a conference paper) that found the amount of information not significantly related to feedback effects, and one study of college undergraduates (Kulhavy, White, Topp, Chan & Adams 1985) in which more complex feedback went with less error correction and lower efficiency than simple correct-answer feedback; she advises presenting elaborated feedback in manageable units, `full` (the two studies are known only as reported in the review). Timmers & Veldkamp (2011), three studies, about half of respondents attended to feedback on incorrect answers only and about a quarter paid no attention to feedback, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2010.11.007), `abs`. Van der Kleij, Eggen, Timmers & Veldkamp (2012), higher-education experiment, no significant difference between feedback conditions, more attention to immediate feedback, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2011.07.020), `abs`. Attali & van der Kleij (2017), adding an explanation raised later performance after incorrect first responses but not after correct ones, see P19, `sec`.
  - Explanatory content: Butler, Godbole & Marsh (2013), see P19. Wittwer & Renkl (2008), see P3.
- Strength: strong that task-focused feedback beats person-focused feedback; moderate for manageable pieces, which reconcile findings that elaboration helps and that some learners skip feedback; limited for the decisive-sentence-first ordering, which is a judgement call.
- Boundary conditions: No cap on how much explanation a wrong answer may need; the evidence is about efficiency and attention, and it does not fix a length. Much of the evidence is from workplaces, laboratories and school tasks. Feedback is less effective for motivational outcomes, so affirming messages may have a role in keeping people studying that these learning-outcome studies cannot see (untested here). The attention results come from assessments in higher education and an online maths site.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. Order: line one gives the verdict and the right answer; line two gives the reason tied to the cue in the case text (point to or quote it); then, in short separate pieces, why the tempting alternative fails. (An expand control is permitted only for later encounters of an item, per P2 requires 4, never the first time an item is met.) **(judgement call)** The decisive sentence goes first because some learners skip feedback entirely (Timmers & Veldkamp 2011: about half attended only to feedback on incorrect answers and about a quarter to none) and the research on the amount of feedback is mixed (Shute 2008). How far into a piece of feedback phone readers actually read has not been measured, so the app logs how often the learner moves on before the later pieces have been on screen, to test this. There is no cap on how much a wrong answer may need.
  2. Feedback is information ("Right: the case shows X, which only Y has"), not evaluation ("Great job!", "Not quite"). Status marks are neutral. No comparison with other learners and no overall grade.
  3. The feedback addresses the route: for the key it says which answer to which question the case actually supports and points to the cue. After a wrong answer it shows the reasoning that leads from the learner's choice to the right answer.
  4. Feedback uses the key's own words (P5) and maps varied case wording back to the fixed term.
  5. Specimen feedback names the structural feature, not the story (P13).
- FORBIDS:
  - Praise as feedback, or "Incorrect" alone.
  - Feedback that retells the case or pads with restatement.
  - Comparing the learner with others.
- Source notes: T F14, F15, F16; P F3; D F2, F18.

### P21. Treat a right answer as unproven until the route is checked, and bring wrong answers back, above all the confident ones.
- Evidence:
  - Hidden errors: Butler, Karpicke & Roediger (2008), see P19, feedback on correct answers doubled retention of low-confidence correct answers, `abs`. Attali & van der Kleij (2017) and Timmers & Veldkamp (2011), see P20: adding an explanation after correct answers added little, and learners often skip feedback on correct answers, `sec` and `abs`.
  - Confident errors: Butterfield & Metcalfe (2001), errors made with high confidence are hypercorrected, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.27.6.1491), `abs`. Butler, Fazio & Marsh (2011), the effect persisted over a week but high-confidence errors returned, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-011-0173-y), `abs`. Metcalfe (2017), review, corrective feedback that analyses the reasoning behind the mistake is what makes errorful learning beneficial, see P18.
  - Self-assessment: Rhodes & Tauber (2011), meta-analysis of 112 effect sizes (4,554 participants), delaying a judgement of learning markedly improved its accuracy (g = 0.93) with a small gain in memory itself (g = 0.08), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021705), `abs`. Pashler et al. (2007), the panel rated delayed judgements and quizzes-to-identify-gaps as low-evidence recommendations, `full`. Dunlosky & Rawson (2012), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2011.08.003), `rec` (not relied on).
- Strength: moderate for the low-confidence result (clean, but general-knowledge facts) and for hypercorrection; moderate for lasting correction. No source found tests feedback that corrects the route when the name was right, so the application to the route is an inference from related findings.
- Boundary conditions: The low-confidence result concerned simple confirmation of a correct answer, not an explanation, and the explanation-after-correct result is for maths problems; the two are in tension, hence "one short reason" below. The persistence of old errors after a week shows one corrective message is not the end of it. Delayed judgements were tested on word pairs rated by undergraduates, and learners can become more accurate monitors without learning more.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. A correct answer still gets a one-line recap of the route or cue that makes it right. Keep it short.
  2. A right name reached by the wrong route is a miss, and gets the fuller route explanation even though the name was right. Because name and route are scored separately, this is exactly the hidden-error case.
  3. Every wrong answer gets the full explanation (P20) and is scheduled to come back (P24). One correction is not the end of it: errors held with high confidence were corrected readily and still tended to return a week later. Where the app captures confidence (requires 4), a confident wrong answer is marked for an extra return.
  4. **(judgement call)** If the app captures a learner's confidence, it is used to give low-confidence correct answers the same fuller feedback as wrong ones and to schedule their return, never to decide that the learner has finished. The "how sure are you" design is inspired by the finding and untested here; if used, collecting it after a delay is the better-supported form.
- FORBIDS:
  - Showing "correct" with no reasoning.
  - Giving full credit for a right name by a wrong route.
  - Using confidence or ease ratings to skip review.
- Source notes: T F18, F19; P F13, F3.

### Later practice

### P22. Once the outcomes are introduced, practise confusable ones mixed together.
- Evidence:
  - Kornell & Bjork (2008), UCLA undergraduates, 12 artists, mixed study beat blocked (about 61% versus 35%, d about 1.0; 59% versus 36%, d about 1.3), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02127.x), `full`. Kang & Pashler (2012), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1801), `abs`.
  - Brunmair & Richter (2019), meta-analysis of 59 studies (238 effect sizes, 158 samples), g = 0.42; paintings 0.67, photographs 0.35, mathematics 0.34, not reliably different from zero for expository text (g = 0.21, 95% CI -0.06 to 0.47, k = 25), reversed for words (g = -0.39, k = 13); the benefit was reliable only when items followed each other immediately (g = 0.73, separated g = 0.22); larger when categories were more similar to each other; larger in younger samples (k = 85, b = -0.04 per year, p < .001) with no significant age effect within university-student samples alone (k = 49, p = .19); the authors advise caution with interleaving for expository texts and words; heterogeneity I-squared about 77%, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209), `full` (accepted manuscript). Firth, Rivers & Boyle (2021), systematic review of 26 studies, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1002/rev3.3266), `abs`.
  - Choosing the method from the case: Taylor & Rohrer (2010), children, blocked 99% and 98% during practice versus interleaved 68% and 79% (practice-session figures as given in Dunlosky et al. 2013, not in the abstract), then 38% versus 77% a day later, the extra errors being discrimination errors, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1598), `abs`. Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic (2015), 126 seventh graders, d = 0.42 at one day and 0.79 at 30 days, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000001), `abs`. Rohrer et al. (2020), preregistered cluster RCT, 54 classes, 61% versus 38% at a month (d = 0.83), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000367), `abs`. Samani & Pan (2021), field experiment in university physics, interleaved homework gave more correct solutions on surprise tests while students rated it harder and believed they had learned less, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-021-00110-x), `abs`.
  - Cognitive concepts: Sana, Yan & Kim (2017), three experiments, students studied six problems across three statistical concepts and then classified new problems: interleaving beat blocking; adding gaps between successive problems lowered performance in interleaved schedules but raised it in blocked ones; presenting problems in groups of three helped most for blocked schedules, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000119), `abs`.
  - Diagnosis: Hatala, Brooks & Norman (2003), two randomised experiments with medical students, mixed ECG practice 46% versus 30% on new ECGs with instruction held constant, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1022687404380), `abs`. Rozenshtein et al. (2016), 40 medical students, 57% versus 43% (p = .03), new-case advantage not significant, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacr.2016.03.031), `abs`. Kellman & Krasne (2018), developers' review of perceptual and adaptive learning modules, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/0142159X.2018.1484897), `abs`; Krasne et al. (2013), pre-post, no control group, [doi](https://doi.org/10.4103/2153-3539.123991), `abs`; Kellman, Massey & Son (2010), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1756-8765.2009.01053.x), `abs`.
  - When blocking wins: Carvalho & Goldstone (2014, 2015), [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-013-0371-0), [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-014-0676-4), `abs`. Noh, Yan, Bjork & Maddox (2016), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2016.06.007), `abs`. Sorensen & Woltz (2016), verbal categories, blocked-first groups did better, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-016-0615-x), `abs`. Zulkiply & Burt (2013), [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-012-0238-9), `abs`. Birnbaum et al. (2013), spacing hurt when it broke up the side-by-side comparison of two categories, see P12. Foster et al. (2019), both discriminative contrast and distributed practice contribute, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-019-00918-4), `abs`. Dunlosky et al. (2013), interleaving rated moderate utility; practise a new type first, then interleave it with earlier types, `full`.
- Strength: strong for picture-based categories and for choosing the problem type in mathematics; limited for verbal and conceptual categories, which are most of what this app teaches: words reversed (g = -0.39), expository text was not reliably different from zero, Sorensen & Woltz (2016) favoured blocked-first for verbal categories, and the one supportive study found is of statistical concepts (Sana, Yan & Kim 2017); moderate for introduce-blocked-then-mix as a sequence.
- Boundary conditions: Blocking wins for dissimilar categories, for passive labelled study, for rule-based categories, in one verbal-category study at first exposure, and when categories are easy to discriminate. The two sources disagree on within-category similarity (the meta-analysis found larger effects when items within a category were less alike, Carvalho and Goldstone found the advantage for categories high in both within- and between-category similarity); they agree that between-category similarity favours mixing. "Similarity" was mostly perceptual. The radiology new-case result was not significant. Brunmair & Richter's text describes one case-study experiment on psychological disorders with an interleaving effect and one text about the human immune system with none. The benefit needs adjacent items (g = 0.73 against 0.22 when separated), and interruptions in phone sessions may break adjacency; that is an inference, not a tested claim. P12 and P22 share one mechanism, discriminative contrast (Birnbaum et al. 2013; Carvalho & Goldstone 2017), and differ in timing: P12 teaches the first contrast, P22 schedules the later mixing. Dunlosky and colleagues' suggestion that some null effects arose because learners lacked instruction on the individual tasks is a hypothesis. Almost all samples are children, undergraduates or medical trainees; some key groups champion the technique; nothing tested interleaved verbal classification on a phone. The cross-subject Mixed drill probably earns spacing and retrieval benefits and little discrimination benefit (the notes' inference, not a tested claim).
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. The sequence is: introduce each outcome on its own (what it is, typical features, a clean case; P14), contrast it with its closest neighbour (P12), then practise mixed.
  2. Mixed drills mix the outcomes the key says are confusable (for example, those still possible after the gate question, or the sibling outcomes of one branch), adjacent and in an order the learner cannot predict. Do not interleave everything: unrelated or easily distinguished outcomes do not need it.
  3. Practice items are built around the contrasts the questions draw (for example, two outcomes that differ on one question), not only around each outcome's definition, and include items where the learner first has to decide what kind of case this is (the gate question), not "use the idea from the card you just read".
  4. From Unit 2 on, each unit drill includes a share of items whose right answer depends on an earlier unit, not labelled by unit, and the learner is told so.
  5. The specimen order alternates confusable outcomes rather than clustering them, within a clean-to-messy order (P13).
  6. The cross-subject Mixed drill is described as spacing and retrieval practice, not as discrimination training. The card that introduces any mixed practice says it is meant to feel harder, and why (P23).
  7. **(judgement call)** Because the evidence for verbal and conceptual categories is limited, the default above (blocked introduction, then contrast, then mixed practice of confusable outcomes) is a design choice to be tested, not a finding. The app logs first-attempt accuracy for mixed versus blocked drill items so the default can be checked on real learners. One-outcome-per-block drills after the outcomes have been introduced, and learner-selectable blocked-only practice as the default, are not adopted unless that data supports them; the evidence for this app's categories does not justify forbidding them outright.
  8. **Procedure units:** a new procedure is first practised on its own (P10 requires 6). Once two or more procedures have been taught, practice mixes the types of problem, so that the learner has to decide from the problem which procedure applies, and from the second unit on a share of each drill is problems from earlier units, not labelled by unit (requires 4). This is the use of mixing with the strongest evidence in this document (Taylor & Rohrer 2010; Rohrer et al. 2015, 2020: school mathematics, tests a day to a month later). Its limit is the population: school students taught by a teacher, not adults alone on a phone.
- FORBIDS:
  - Mixing in outcomes the learner has not yet met.
  - Calling unrelated-subject mixing discrimination training.
- Source notes: D F1-F5, F20; P F10, F11; I F19.

### P23. Judge practice by results, not by how it feels, and let the app, not the learner, decide what comes back.
- Evidence:
  - Wrong beliefs about what works: Kornell & Bjork (2008), 78% did better mixed, 78% rated blocked as good or better, see P22. Kornell (2009), flashcards, 72% believed massing worked better though spacing was better for 90%, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1537), `abs`. Tauber et al. (2013), learners chose to block, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-012-0319-6), `abs`. Karpicke, Butler & Roediger (2009), survey of 177 college students, most reread, few self-test, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658210802647009), `abs`. Koriat & Bjork (2005), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.31.2.187), `abs`. Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell (2013), review, judgements of learning follow current performance and fluency, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823), `abs`. Soderstrom & Bjork (2015), performance during practice is an unreliable index of learning, see P17.
  - Fluency and effort misread: Carpenter, Wilford, Kornell & Mullaney (2013), a fluent instructor raised perceived learning without raising actual learning, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-013-0442-z), `abs`. Deslauriers et al. (2019), randomised physics course, active classrooms learned more but felt they had learned less, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1821936116), `abs`. Kirk-Johnson, Galla & Fraundorf (2019), more effortful strategies were rated less effective and chosen less, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2019.101237), `abs`. Rozenblit & Keil (2002), twelve studies, an illusion of explanatory depth, strongest for how-things-work knowledge, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog2605_1), `abs`.
  - Fixing the belief is hard: Yan, Bjork & Bjork (2016), six experiments, explanation alone had modest effect, most learners changed their minds only when experience, explanation and separate per-schedule test results were combined, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000177), `abs`. Samani & Pan (2021), see P22.
- Strength: strong that the illusions exist and replicate; moderate for what to do about them (debiasing is hard).
- Boundary conditions: Mostly laboratory work on undergraduates and short ratings. The physics result concerns instructor-led active learning, not quizzing on a phone; the link is the mechanism (effortful practice is read as poor learning), not the setting. The misinterpreted-effort account has had mixed follow-up in work the note did not read. Explanation alone barely moved beliefs. The evidence measures learning, not whether people keep going; a drill that feels punishing risks dropout, and its size cannot be estimated from this literature (section 7); P28 requires that it be measured.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. The app, not the learner, decides what returns and when. No control removes an item from review or declares a unit finished, and a confidence or ease rating never decides that a unit is finished. (What the learner may control, such as pace, stopping and which unit to open, is P28.)
  2. Where mixed practice, a spaced return or a first-sight miss would otherwise cost the learner's confidence, one plain sentence says it is meant to feel harder and why. This is necessary and not sufficient.
  3. The learner is shown their own results: accuracy on mixed items, and accuracy on items first seen days earlier beside accuracy on items from today. Experience plus separate results is the form of the remedy the evidence supports.
  4. A card that reads smoothly, or that the learner says makes sense, is not counted as evidence that it taught. This is the same rule as the P27 forbid on "it reads well" and is checked once, under P27.
  5. Difficulty lives in the practice schedule and the item types. The teaching itself is as clear as possible: a lesson explanation is never made terse or inconsistent on the theory that struggle is good, because the verified difficulty research concerns practice schedules and retrieval, not comprehension of the explanation.
- FORBIDS:
  - Letting the learner remove an item from review or declare a unit finished.
  - Using ease or confidence ratings as quality evidence.
  - Making an explanation harder on purpose.
  - Rereading as the review mode.
- Source notes: P F12, F13, F17; D F4; T F25.

### Across units and days

### P24. Bring items back on later days until each has been answered correctly in several separate sessions.
- Evidence:
  - Spacing: Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006), 839 assessments in 317 experiments in 184 articles, the gap and the retention interval operate jointly, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354), `abs` (verbal recall tasks only). Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler (2008), more than 1,350 people, the best gap was about 20 to 40 percent of the delay at one week and about 5 to 10 percent at one year, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x), `abs`. Latimier, Peyre & Ramus (2021), meta-analysis of 29 studies, spacing out retrieval beat massed retrieval, no clear advantage for expanding gaps, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8), `sec` for the g values. Kornell (2009), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1537), `abs`. Karpicke & Roediger (2007), expanding versus equal gaps, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.33.4.704), `abs`. Kang (2016), review, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/2372732215624708), `abs`. Carpenter et al. (2012), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-012-9205-z), `rec`. Pashler et al. (2007), expose key content on at least two occasions weeks to months apart (moderate), `full`. Dunlosky et al. (2013), distributed practice rated high utility, `full`.
  - Successive relearning: Rawson & Dunlosky (2011), three experiments, 533 students, practise to 3 correct recalls then relearn 3 times at widely spaced intervals, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023956), `abs`. Janes, Dunlosky, Rawson & Jasnow (2020), two experiments in a real biopsychology course, at least 10 percent higher on a high-stakes exam (d from 0.54 to 1.10), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.3699), `abs`. Rawson & Dunlosky (2022), review, few students do it on their own, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/09637214221100484), `abs`. Karpicke & Roediger (2008), after a first recall, repeated study had no effect on delayed recall but repeated testing had a large positive one, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408), `abs`. Kornell & Bjork (2008b), letting learners drop flashcards they thought they knew had small negative effects, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658210701763899), `abs`. Rohrer, Taylor, Pashler, Wixted & Cepeda (2005), overlearning inside one session gave strong one-week recall that faded by later tests, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1083), `abs`. Rawson, Dunlosky & Sciartelli (2013), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-013-9240-4), `rec`.
  - Adults outside a classroom: Kerfoot et al. (2007), randomised trial, third-year medical students given weekly e-mailed cases after a urology rotation: spaced education improved end-of-year scores (p < 0.001), with the largest effects for students tested 6 to 8 and 9 to 11 months after the rotation (d = 1.01 and 0.73); 95 of 133 students (71 percent) completed the end-of-year test, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2929.2006.02644.x), `abs`. Larsen, Butler & Roediger (2009), randomised trial, residents, 39% versus 26% at six months (d = 0.91, 40 completers), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2923.2009.03518.x), `abs`. Meyer & Logan (2013), testing helped younger students, younger community adults and community adults aged 55 to 65 similarly over two days, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030890), `abs`.
- Strength: strong for spacing (hundreds of experiments); moderate for the ridgeline gap ratios (one large study, trivia facts); moderate to strong for successive relearning on verbal and conceptual material; limited for procedures and for any exact schedule.
- Boundary conditions: When the same relearning design was applied to probability problems (431 college students, three experiments, Rawson, Dunlosky & Janes 2020, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09528-y), `abs`) the benefit was small (pooled d = 0.28) and solving a problem correctly did not significantly raise the chance of solving the next, a boundary for procedures. The ridgeline is for trivia facts learned by web participants over months. The app has no exam date, so there is no principled single gap; the schedule below is the notes' inference from the ratios in the abstract (20 to 40 percent of a week is about 1.5 to 3 days; 5 to 10 percent of a year is about 2.5 to 5 weeks), not a published prescription. Spacing can hurt category learning when it interrupts the side-by-side comparison of confusable categories (P22). The trainee trials had external stakes; no trial of a phone app for general-interest self-study exists, so engagement, completion and the right set size are unknown. Learners do not space on their own.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. The app keeps a per-item record: which items were missed, when, and in which sessions each was answered correctly. The practice note found that the app recorded only counts of answered and correct (per drill, lifetime), so none of the rules below is possible without this record. For classification and procedure units the record is kept against what the learner has to tell apart (each outcome, each key question, each type of problem) as well as against the individual case, and a return uses a different case of the same outcome wherever the bank has one (P13 requires 5): bringing back the identical case tests memory for its story. For fact units the item is the fact itself.
  2. Within a session a missed item stays in the queue until it is answered correctly, and an item is not dropped after one correct answer on first sight.
  3. Items come back on later days rather than as extra same-day repetitions. **(judgement call, starting values)** First return after a day or two, so the retrieval is effortful; second return about a week later; third about three to four weeks later; adjust from the learner's results; err toward too long rather than too short.
  4. A spaced return brings confusable items back together, not one at a time (an inference from P22).
  5. Progress is per item inside the schedule; the next unit is not hard-gated on a mastery score (P17 requires 5, a judgement call).
  6. Returned sets are short and each item carries its answer and explanation (P19). Their size is not determined by the evidence.
  7. **(judgement call)** **Fact units:** a list the learner must know is grouped under the concept it serves (P3 requires 5), every fact is asked as a question with the answer shown after (P16, P19), and each is returned on the schedule in requires 3. The unit says plainly that it is facts to hold, so the learner can tell which parts are the skill and which are memorisation (the comprehension audit, K8, found the civics course never said so).
- FORBIDS:
  - Overlearning inside one sitting as the way to buy retention.
  - Deleting an item on first success, or leaving return timing to the learner.
- Source notes: P F7, F8, F9, F18, F20; D F26.

### P28. Let the learner control the pace and the stopping, never what returns, and measure persistence instead of assuming it.
- Evidence:
  - Choice and motivation: Patall, Cooper & Robinson (2008), meta-analysis of 41 studies with child and adult samples: providing choice enhanced intrinsic motivation, effort, task performance and perceived competence, among other outcomes; the effect on intrinsic motivation was stronger for instructionally irrelevant choices than for instructionally relevant ones, and stronger for children than for adults, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.134.2.270), `abs`.
  - The cost side: Yan, Bjork & Bjork (2016), see P23, explanation alone had only a modest effect on learners' belief that effortful schedules were worse, so one plain sentence does not remove the felt cost. Kirk-Johnson et al. (2019), see P23, more effortful strategies were rated less effective and chosen less. Wisniewski et al. (2020), see P20, feedback effects were weaker on motivational than on cognitive outcomes.
  - What the learner should not control: Kornell & Bjork (2008b) and Karpicke & Roediger (2008), see P24, letting learners drop items they thought they knew had small negative effects; Karpicke, Butler & Roediger (2009), see P23, learners reread rather than self-test.
- Strength: moderate that choice raises motivation and effort in general; limited for persistence in a self-study app, where no study was found.
- Boundary conditions: The learning-science evidence in this document measures learning, not whether people keep going. P15 (typed reasons), P16 (checks between cards), P22 and P23 (practice that is meant to feel harder) and P24 (returns on later days) each add effort for an adult with no teacher, no exam and no external stakes, and the size of that cost cannot be estimated from the literature. Patall et al. found choice helps less when it is instructionally relevant and less for adults, so the choices that are safe to give are the instructionally irrelevant ones (pace, when to stop, which unit to open), and the choices the evidence advises against are those that remove practice (P23). Whether brief affirming messages or streaks help persistence is untested here (the P20 boundary note says the same). Nothing in this principle limits how much a unit teaches: measurement informs how a unit is organised (where it splits, where a check sits), never a cap on how much an idea may be explained (section 5).
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. The learner controls what the evidence says can safely be controlled: when to stop a session, which subject or unit to open, the pace, whether to see the optional plan card (P26), and skipping an item for now, which does not count as learned and returns on the schedule. The app controls what returns and when (P23 requires 1, P24).
  2. Every session has a visible end the learner can reach (a set, or a returned set, that finishes), so that stopping is a choice and not a defeat. This chunks sessions; it does not cap what a unit teaches. **(judgement call)** The evidence does not fix the size of a set (P24 requires 6).
  3. The learner is shown their own results (P23 requires 3).
  4. Session starts, session completions and the rate at which learners return on later days are recorded as first-class data, because they are the unmeasured cost of P15 to P24, and are read beside first-attempt accuracy (P17 requires 4, P27 requires 3).
  5. **(judgement call)** Whether affirming messages or streaks help persistence is untested: they are tried one at a time against the measures in requires 4, not assumed to help and not banned.
- FORBIDS:
  - A control that removes an item from review or declares a unit finished (P23).
  - Answering a persistence problem by cutting the explanation a beginner needs: first look at where learners leave, then split, reorder or add a check (P3, P7, section 5).
  - Assuming that long explanations or effortful drills cost nothing, or that they cost a great deal: the cost is measured.
- Source notes: none; added in the 2026-10-04 revision from problem 5 of `docs/learning-science/review-claims.md`, with the evidence checked online.

### Transfer

### P25. Aim for transfer to new cases of the same kind, in the format real life uses, and do not promise general thinking skills.
- Evidence:
  - Far transfer is rare and conditional: Barnett & Ceci (2002), taxonomy of nine dimensions, transfer is substantial under some conditions and a single far-transfer effect size is misguided, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.128.4.612), `abs`. Sala & Gobet (2017), meta-analyses of chess, music and working-memory training in children, small effects that shrank with better designs, far transfer rarely occurs, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721417712760), `abs`. Perkins & Salomon (2012), transfer as three bridges (detect a link, choose to pursue it, work out the connection), driven by motivation and disposition, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2012.693354), `abs`. (Perkins & Salomon 1988 appears in the notes as a secondary mention only and is not relied on.)
  - Near-to-moderate transfer of a specific rule: Fong, Krantz & Nisbett (1986), four experiments, brief training in the law of large numbers, by rule or by examples, improved reasoning about everyday problems, including untrained domains, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(86)90001-0), `sec` (search excerpt of the abstract). Fong & Nisbett (1991), immediate transfer was strong and the untrained-domain benefit declined over two weeks but stayed above controls, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.120.1.34), `abs`. Nisbett, Fong, Lehman & Cheng (1987), review, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.3672116), `abs`. Abrami et al. (2015), meta-analysis of 341 effect sizes on teaching critical thinking, g = 0.30, helped by dialogue, authentic problems and mentoring, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654314551063), `abs`.
  - Debiasing by instances: Morewedge et al. (2015), two longitudinal experiments, a single game or video reduced bias immediately and at two months, games with practice and personalised feedback did better, effects appeared in untaught formats, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/2372732215600886), `abs`. Sellier, Scopelliti & Morewedge (2019), quasi-field study of 290 graduate students, 19 percent less likely to choose the confirming answer, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797619861429), `abs`. Aczel et al. (2015), analogical training improved only tasks measuring violations of statistical principles, [doi](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01120), `abs`. Ludolph & Schulz (2018), systematic review of 68 articles, most interventions at least partly successful, transfer to real life the main challenge, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0272989X17716672), `abs`.
  - Relevance to the learner's own life: Hulleman & Harackiewicz (2009), randomised field experiment with high-school science students: asking students to connect the course material to their own lives raised interest and course grades for students with low expectations of success, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1177067), `abs`. Canning & Harackiewicz (2015), three laboratory studies with a mental-arithmetic technique: being told why the technique is useful undermined performance and interest for people who lacked confidence, while writing their own reasons had positive effects; the harm went when learners could also give their own examples, and the less confident did better with everyday examples than with career or school ones, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/mot0000015), `abs`.
  - Integration as the last step: Merrill (2002), see P1. Pan & Rickard (2018), transfer needs matching answers, elaborated practice and high initial success, see P5.
- Strength: strong that far transfer is hard and conditional (the negative evidence concerns training thinking skills in children, which differs from teaching adults to classify cases); moderate that near-to-moderate transfer of a specific rule taught with varied examples is achievable; limited for the relevance step (school and laboratory studies of interest and grades, not of transfer).
- Boundary conditions: The positive results are narrow formal rules or specific biases, taught with several examples and practice, tested on the same kind of problem in a new setting. The untrained-domain benefit faded faster. The Fong abstracts do not describe participants. The debiasing results are concentrated in one research team and partly mixed. The two sets of findings do not conflict once "same kind of problem, new setting" is separated from "general thinking".
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. The transfer target is stated: the same kind of case, in a different story, setting and wording, in the format real life uses (a message, a headline, an offer, a statement).
  2. Specimens look like the real thing in the learner's world, not only like tidy textbook cases, and the words the learner will meet in real life are taught alongside the lesson's own vocabulary (the "also called" mapping in P5).
  3. For rule-based subjects (Statistical Claims, Basic Math, Scams) each rule is taught with examples from at least two different areas of life and then tested in a third. The test is repeated after a delay (P24), since the untrained-domain benefit decays first. Expect the strongest benefit for new cases of the same kind.
  4. **(judgement call)** Each unit has a short "where would you meet this?" step, because noticing the occasion is a separate step from knowing the idea. The learner is asked to name an occasion of their own, with two or three everyday examples supplied as prompts; the unit does not simply tell them the idea is useful, because being told did harm to less confident learners in the one study that separated the two. That evidence is about interest and performance in school and laboratory settings, and nothing tests the step as an aid to transfer (section 7), so tune it on whether learners complete the step.
  5. Where biases or fallacies are the content (Psychology, Statistical Claims), the learning is built around instances: several cases of each error with feedback, mixed with cases where the error is not present, with the learner doing something with an example in a new setting and being told how they did. A card that merely describes a bias is not enough.
- FORBIDS:
  - Claiming a unit makes people better thinkers in general.
  - Tidy-only specimens.
  - A card that describes a bias with no practice on instances.
- Source notes: D F22, F23, F24; I F9.

### P26. For subjects the learner will act on, teach telling the real from the fake, bring it back before it fades, and offer an optional if-then plan.
- Evidence:
  - Discrimination, not suspicion: Burke, Kieffer, Mottola & Perez-Arce (2022), preregistered randomised experiment with a nationally representative US panel (1,976 completed the baseline), a few minutes of video or text about five fraud techniques made people less willing to invest in adapted fraudulent solicitations while effects on legitimate offers were much more muted; with no reminder the effect had gone by six months, with a reminder at three months it persisted; for lower-sophistication readers the text version alone went with a worse outcome on one measure, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jebo.2022.03.028), `full` (read in the February 2021 working-paper version). Scheibe et al. (2014), field experiment with older consumers who had been scam victims, a warning two or four weeks before a mock telemarketing pitch reduced acceptance, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/01973533.2014.903844), `abs`.
  - If-then plans: Gollwitzer (1999), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.54.7.493), `abs`. Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), meta-analysis of 94 tests, d = 0.65 on goal attainment, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1), `sec` (search excerpt of the abstract); no longer the best single figure. Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer (2025), update meta-analysis of 642 independent tests: plans were effective for cognitive, affective and behavioural outcomes (effects from d = .27 to d = .66 depending on the outcome), larger when the plan had an if-then format, the goal was highly motivating and the plan was rehearsed, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563), `sec` (search-returned abstract). Webb & Sheeran (2006), a medium-to-large change in intention (d = 0.66) led to a small-to-medium change in behaviour (d = 0.36), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.2.249), `abs`. Sheeran, Webb & Gollwitzer (2005), plans helped when the goal intention was strong but not when it was weak, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167204271308), `abs`. Adriaanse et al. (2011), healthy eating d = 0.51 for promoting, 0.29 for reducing unhealthy eating, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.appet.2010.10.012), `abs`. Hagger & Luszczynska (2014), health-context review, recommended guided plans that name salient cues, give example cues and include boosters, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/aphw.12017), `abs`.
- Strength: moderate on average for discrimination-not-suspicion (a large preregistered trial and a small field experiment; the main outcome is stated willingness to invest, not behaviour with real money), limited for less sophisticated readers of text (see requires 3); moderate for if-then plans for simple behaviours with a clear cue and a held goal (the 2025 update reports d = .27 to .66 by outcome, against d = 0.65 from 94 tests in 2006); limited for their use here.
- Boundary conditions: The fraud result concerns investment fraud only. Its benefit was concentrated among people more likely to invest, particularly the financially sophisticated (the published abstract as returned by a search; the working-paper text, read in full, agrees): individuals with higher cognitive ability and financial literacy benefited more from the text version and from the reminder, and the effect of the reminder was small and not significant for the least financially literate. For participants in the bottom third of cognitive ability, the text-only version went with less probability weight on losing money six months on (by 5.9 and 4.5 percentage points on two measures), which is a worse outcome; for low financial literacy the estimates were negative but imprecise; the video-only version showed no such heterogeneity. The authors say the interventions were designed in relatively simple language. Plans did not help when the goal was weak, helped less for stopping unwanted behaviour and less in better-controlled studies, and heterogeneity is large. No study was found of if-then plans as a way to carry a classification skill into everyday life; the evidence is for behaviours such as eating. The plan card is therefore an inference from the planning literature, not a tested transfer aid. The only generated-example result (Zamary & Rawson) does not support replacing provided examples (P11).
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. Subjects where the learner acts in real life (Scams & Social Engineering, Wealth Preservation, Statistical Claims) test the learner on legitimate cases as well as bad ones inside the same drills, so what is learned is discrimination, not suspicion of everything.
  2. Those subjects bring scenarios back at roughly the point where the benefit would otherwise fade; the evidence is that a reminder is what keeps it. Use the return schedule (P24) with at least one later return at weeks to months.
  3. Burke et al. found that the benefit of text-only fraud education was concentrated among the more sophisticated and that, for the least sophisticated (low cognitive ability or financial literacy), the text-only version went with a worse outcome on a belief measure even though it was written in relatively simple language; the video-only version showed no such pattern. The app is text first, so for Scams and Wealth Preservation: (a) do not assume plain language fixes this (P4 is necessary, not shown to be sufficient); (b) include worked scenarios that show the sequence of a scam as it unfolds, in the same cards (P10); (c) log first-attempt accuracy on legitimate-versus-fraud items split by a short baseline knowledge check, and treat low accuracy in the low-baseline group as a lesson defect; (d) consider an audio or illustrated format for these two subjects only if (c) shows a gap.
  4. **(judgement call)** An optional plan card closes units whose subject calls for action. The learner names a cue in their own world (two or three example cues supplied, so it is guided, not blank) and writes one "if I see X, then I will do Y". It is offered only to learners who want that goal, and a later reminder is scheduled, which also supplies the rehearsal that the larger plan effects went with. It is never a substitute for discrimination practice. A personal-example invitation may sit in the same step but never replaces provided examples.
- FORBIDS:
  - A Scams lesson with only fraudulent cases.
  - Assuming every learner holds the goal the plan serves.
  - Substituting a plan card for practice.
- Source notes: D F25, F26, F27, F24.

### Checking that a lesson works

### P27. Test every unit on someone who does not know the subject, because the author cannot see the gaps.
- Evidence:
  - Hinds (1999), two experiments, people with more expertise were worse at predicting how long novices would take on a complex task and debiasing techniques did not help; people with intermediate expertise were most accurate, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/1076-898x.5.2.205), `abs`. Nathan & Petrosino (2003), 48 preservice mathematics teachers, those with more advanced mathematics education were more likely to misjudge what students find prerequisite ("expert blind spot"), [doi](https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312040004905), `abs`.
  - The feeling of clarity is not evidence of learning: Rozenblit & Keil (2002), see P23. Carpenter et al. (2013), see P23. Wittwer & Renkl (2008), see P3. Davison & Kantor (1982), Graesser et al. (2011), see P4.
  - First-attempt performance as the signal: Rowland (2014) and Rosenshine (2012), see P17.
- Strength: moderate that the bias exists in judgements of novice difficulty (the evidence found here is two small experiments, Hinds 1999 and Nathan & Petrosino 2003, although the curse of knowledge is widely discussed); limited evidence on how to fix it (Hinds's debiasing failed).
- Boundary conditions: Both studies measure predictions of novice performance, not the quality of text an expert writes, so applying the result to drafting lessons is an inference, though a close one. Intermediate users predicted best, which suggests the reviewer should be someone who learned the material recently, not the subject expert. The illusion studies concern everyday mechanisms, not classification.
- REQUIRES of a Fieldcraft lesson:
  1. Two checks, and neither stands in for the other. (a) **Coverage check** (automatable, and a simulated reader may run it): every option, question and outcome wording a drill uses appears in an earlier card of the same subject, and every practice item is answerable from the lessons alone (the learner-simulation check in the rebuild worklist; the two-way vocabulary check of P5 requires 2 is the same kind of check). It is necessary and never sufficient: it shows that a term appears, not that a person understood it, and a simulated reader has the subject knowledge a real novice lacks, which is the blind spot itself. (b) **Comprehension check** (a human): a person with no prior knowledge of the subject, or failing that a near-novice (requires 2), reads the unit cold, says in their own words what each card said, and attempts the drill without outside help. Only (b) signs a unit off.
  2. Where a real novice is not available, a think-aloud by a near-novice (someone who learned the subject recently) is the next best check. The author's own review, and review by the subject expert, are the least informative and never sign a unit off.
  3. First-attempt accuracy per item is recorded in the app (P17), and a unit with low first-attempt accuracy is treated as a teaching defect.
  4. A report that a lesson is confusing is treated as a high-value signal, because confusion is what thin text produces in readers who notice the gaps.
  5. A card is checked by making the learner use it: a drill item or a short "say it back" that cannot be answered by recognising words.
- FORBIDS:
  - Author or subject-expert review as the sign-off.
  - "It reads well" or "it feels clear" as evidence a card teaches.
- Source notes: T F6, F25; P F2, F12.

## 4. How the principles fit together

The principles overlap, and four of them pull against each other in the research literature. This section settles each pull, names the evidence that decides it and how confident the decision is, and then gives one sequence for a unit (4.3) and one for a subject (4.4) that follow from all of them.

### 4.1 The four named tensions

**Worked examples versus retrieval practice (P10 against P16).**
- The pull: worked-example studies say a beginner should not be made to solve; retrieval studies say learning comes from answering without the material in view.
- What decides it: they work at different phases. For a first exposure to a route, worked cases beat unaided solving (strong; g about 0.48; moderate for classification). For keeping what has been taught, retrieval beats rereading (strong; g about 0.5). A beginner who has not been shown the route cannot produce it, so the attempt yields only a wrong guess to correct (P10); this is a worked-example argument. (Rowland's finding that retrieval with no feedback at or below 50% success does nothing does not apply here, because the app always gives feedback, P19; within the feedback subset, low initial accuracy went with larger retention effects.) The testing effect is argued to shrink for complex multi-step material (van Gog & Sweller 2015); Karpicke & Aue and Rawson reply that the review omitted contrary studies and that the conclusion is not warranted (read at the level of abstracts and summaries), and in Pan & Rickard's meta-analysis testing transferred well to medical-diagnosis problems, so the dispute is unresolved.
- Resolution: show before asking, at each size of task. A single point is shown on a case before a check asks about it; the whole route is shown on a complete worked case before any question asks for it (P10 requires 5). After the worked case comes the ramp: route given, then part left out, then alone (P17). Pieces are retrieved before the whole route, and the whole route on specimens comes last.
- Confidence: high that a worked case comes first for beginners; moderate for the ramp shape; the complex-material dispute is unresolved.

**Interleaving versus blocked introduction (P22 against P14).**
- The pull: mixing confusable categories beats finishing one at a time, yet blocking wins for dissimilar categories, passive study, some verbal material and plausibly a first look at a category.
- What decides it: one mechanism. A sequence changes what the learner attends to: blocked study draws attention to what members of a category share, mixed study to what differs between neighbours (Carvalho & Goldstone). The app's task needs both halves: know what an outcome is like, and tell look-alikes apart. The benefit of mixing appeared only when the compared items were adjacent (g = 0.73 adjacent against 0.22 separated). One verbal-category study favoured blocked-first, and Dunlosky and colleagues advise practising a new type first and then interleaving it with earlier ones.
- Resolution: meet each outcome alone with its general features (blocked); put it directly beside its closest neighbour (contrast); then practise the confusable outcomes mixed. Do not mix outcomes the key does not treat as confusable, and do not mix before each has been taught.
- Confidence: strong that mixing confusables works for pictures and maths; limited for verbal and conceptual categories (words reversed, expository text not reliable, one supportive study of statistical concepts); moderate that blocked-first is the safer introduction; no study tests the whole sequence on adults with everyday verbal categories, so the app logs mixed versus blocked accuracy (P22 requires 7).

**Coherence versus elaboration, "less is more" versus "more is more" (P6 against P3).**
- The pull: removing seductive details improves learning, and a short summary matched a long text; yet novices learn more from fuller, more explicit text.
- What decides it: relevance, not length. Irrelevant interesting material harms recall and transfer (strong); repairing missing explanatory links helps low-knowledge readers (moderate: consistent in experiments, small in the one meta-analysis); in the rewrite meta-analysis clarity and elaboration helped while simplifying did not. The summary result was a summary built around the steps of a process, compared with a long diffuse passage.
- Resolution: every sentence has a job; every missing link is written in (P3, P6). Neither cutting nor adding is a goal in itself.
- Confidence: moderate. The reconciliation is the notes' reading of two bodies of evidence (seductive details and short passages on one side, coherence repairs on the other); no study varies relevance and length together.

**Problem-first versus explanation-first (P10, P12 against productive failure).**
- The pull: attempting an unfamiliar problem before being taught can beat instruction first (meta-analysis g about 0.36, larger with productive-failure designs), but unassisted discovery loses to explicit instruction (d about -0.38), and head-to-head tests with children favour instruction first (Fyfe, DeCaro & Rittle-Johnson 2014; Matlen & Klahr 2013), while one test of three guidance levels before instruction found no difference in delayed transfer (Likourezos & Kalyuga 2017).
- What decides it: the problem-first results needed learners able to generate attempts, a teacher or peers to consolidate, explicit comparison with the canonical method and usually contrasting cases, and were mostly STEM classes; the meta-analysis itself reversed for grades 2 to 5 and for domain-general skills; and each side's strongest evidence comes from groups that advocate it. A fixed phone lesson has no one to consolidate.
- Resolution: explain first. Try-first appears only in two constrained forms: ungraded preview questions answered and explained at once (P1), and a "predict which answer the key gives" step on a contrasting pair whose next card spells out the answer (P12, P15). Never an unguided puzzle as a unit opener.
- Confidence: moderate. This is the better-supported choice for beginners studying alone, but the adult-novice self-study case is untested on both sides.

### 4.2 Other tensions the lessons will meet

- **Self-explanation prompts versus worked examples.** Prompting raises learning on average (g about 0.55), yet the worked-example meta-analysis found a smaller advantage when prompts were added. Decided by: the effect on balance is positive and fading plus principle-naming prompts gave medium to large effects without extra time. Resolution: short, tightly cued prompts on the step that carries the idea, with the model explanation immediately after (P15). Confidence: moderate; the reason for the smaller advantage is unresolved.
- **Elaborated feedback versus feedback that is too long; immediate versus delayed.** Decided by content and structure, not length: relevant explanation beats a verdict; the research on the amount of feedback is inconsistent and some learners skip feedback altogether, so the decisive sentence goes first and the rest comes in manageable pieces (P20, the ordering a judgement call); immediate suits new difficult material and a later return supplies the benefit of delay, with the caveat that after correct first responses one large study favoured delayed feedback (P19). Confidence: moderate.
- **Make practice hard versus keep success high.** Retrieval at or below 50% with no feedback does nothing, yet effortful retrieval and mixed practice are better for retention. Decided by: difficulty is desirable when the learner can still succeed or gets feedback. Resolution: difficulty goes into the schedule and the item types (P22, P24); success comes from good teaching (P3 to P12); feedback is always given (P19); a unit that is hard to answer because it was hard to understand is a teaching defect (P17, P27). Confidence: moderate; no validated target accuracy.
- **Fixed wording versus varied cases.** Transfer wants practice answers to match use (response congruency) and also wants the surface to vary so the story does not become the cue. Resolution: labels are fixed everywhere; the variation lives in the cases and is mapped back in the feedback (P5, P13). Confidence: moderate for the split, limited for the direct evidence.
- **Concrete versus abstract.** Fading from concrete to general beat either alone in several experiments; abstract-only transferred to abstract tests and concrete-only to concrete ones. The target decides: this app's target is concrete real cases, so concrete first and the decisive feature named generically (P11). Confidence: moderate.
- **Brevity in minimalist manuals versus length for conceptual learning.** Minimalism won for procedures the learner could try straight away, with real tasks and error recovery; principle-based instruction wins for learning and transfer. A classification skill needs transfer, so principles stay, while the minimalist lessons (reach real cases early, give error-recovery help) still apply (P9, P20). Confidence: moderate.
- **One screen versus an idea kept whole.** A scrolling format lowered understanding of complex text in one desktop study; separating related information lowers learning (a meta-analysis, P8); cutting relevant explanation hurts beginners (P3). Resolution: screen fit is a check for whether a card makes two points, never a limit. A single point that needs the room continues on a second card with the heading and a restating line, or scrolls, and nothing is cut to fit (P7 requires 3). Confidence: moderate that nothing should be cut; limited for a second card over a scroll.
- **Terms up front versus a case first.** Pre-training says teach the names of the parts before the lesson; P11 and P4 say a case and ordinary words come before any term. Decided by: the pre-training studies taught what each part is and does before a fast narrated animation, not a list of definitions before a text the reader paces, and the vocabulary evidence favours a definition met in context. Resolution: each part is taught (case, ordinary words, term) before any card that combines parts; the orienting card may preview the terms in a few plain words; there is no glossary card (P4 requires 3). Confidence: limited to moderate; no head-to-head test in text.
- **The fixed term in the heading versus ordinary words first.** P5 and P8 want one label, used as the heading of every card; P4 wants the meaning before the term. Resolution: the heading of the card that introduces a term is in ordinary words (the term may follow them); every later heading uses the fixed term (P4 requires 1, P7 requires 5). Confidence: a design choice; the evidence on each side is indirect.
- **The key first versus the outcomes first.** Untested on any key (section 7). Decided by neighbouring evidence: an overview first helps a little (P1); studying one category at a time draws attention to what its members share and was the safer start in the one verbal-category study (P22); parts taught alone before they are combined helped novices (P4); comparison taught more when the principle was stated after it (P12); and a beginner should see the whole route worked before running it (P10). Resolution: the key as a map first; then each outcome on its own; then each key question, taught as the difference between the outcomes on either side of it; then the whole route on a worked case (section 4.3). Confidence: moderate for each link, limited for the order as a whole.
- **Segmenting for beginners versus for high-knowledge learners.** The segmenting meta-analysis found larger retention gains for high-knowledge learners. Resolution: segment because it is not harmful and fits phone reading, but do not treat segmenting as the thing that supports beginners; P3, P4, P10 do that (P7). Confidence: moderate.

### 4.3 The recommended sequence for a unit

This is the one sequence the principles support. It is written for a classification unit; the sequences for fact units and procedure units follow it. Each step cites the principles it comes from. A step is one card or several (P7): the sequence fixes the order of the steps, not the number of cards. A unit teaches part of the subject's key (usually one key question, or the questions of one branch) and the outcomes that part leads to.

0. **Orient** (P1, P2, P30). One orienting card: what you will be able to do, said as a task on a real case; the key as a map with this unit's part marked; the unit's outcomes named, each with a few plain words; the everyday situation the unit starts from. Terms taught in earlier units get a one-line reminder, not a re-teach (P2). Optional: up to three ungraded preview questions, each explained at once, and a "check what you know" for a learner who wants to skip the worked cases.
1. **Each outcome on its own, one at a time** (P22 requires 1; parts before the whole, P4 requires 3). For each outcome the unit teaches:
   1. *Meet it.* One line linking back (P3). A clean concrete case, with the deciding phrase marked (P8). The decisive feature in general words, then the rule in plain words, then the term (P11, P4).
   2. *See it again in a different story.* A second case of the same outcome that looks different, with the instruction to find what the two share (P12 requires 1(a), P13). The rule is now confirmed as general. Then what the outcome is like beyond the deciding feature: its typical features and how it shows up in real life (P14 requires 1).
   3. *Use it.* A check: one new case and one question about this feature, answered from memory and explained at once (P16, P19, P20).
2. **Each key question, taught as the difference between neighbours** (P9, P12). As soon as the outcomes on both sides of a key question have been met:
   1. *Compare.* A look-alike pair, one case from each side, with the feature to compare named. The learner commits to which case gives which answer, and why (P12 requires 1(b), P15).
   2. *Name the question.* The difference stated in the key's words: the key question itself, what it separates, which outcomes each answer keeps and rules out, and why that distinction decides (P9). The neighbours set side by side in a table (P30).
   3. *Exceptions and wrong ideas.* The near-misses, hybrids and exceptions, each taught as a named case (P14 requires 3, P13 requires 4). Where a wrong everyday idea is known: a card that names it, marks it wrong and gives the correct reasoning (P29, P18).
   4. *Use it.* A check: one new case and this one key question (P16, P19, P20).
3. **The route as a whole** (P10). A complete worked case run through every key question taught so far, in the key's words, with the deciding cue marked at each step (P10, P8). Its last part, why the nearest alternative fails, is held back: the learner is first asked why this outcome and not its neighbour, commits to a reason, and then sees the model reason (P15 requires 3). No question asks the learner to run the route before this step.
4. **Unit drill as a ramp** (P17). The route shown and the name asked; then part of the route left out; then the whole route alone. New cases, not the lesson's examples (P13). Confusable outcomes mixed and next to each other (P22); in action subjects, legitimate cases among the bad ones (P26); from Unit 2 on, a share of items from earlier units (P22 requires 4). Faulty-claim items last (P18). Every item gets an immediate explanation (P19, P20), and a right name reached by the wrong route counts as a miss (P21).
5. **Close** (P6, P25, P26). A recap in the key's wording, the "where would you meet this?" step, and, for action subjects, the optional plan card.
6. **After the unit** (P24, P22, P23, P28). What was missed returns on later days on the schedule, with a fresh case where the bank has one; the determination on specimens is the cumulative mixed test (section 4.4); the learner sees their own results; each session has a visible end, and persistence is measured.
7. **Before release** (P5, P27). A coverage check (vocabulary in both directions between cards, key and drills, and every item answerable from the cards; it may be automated or simulated) and a comprehension check by a human (a cold read and a drill attempt by a person with no background, or a near-novice); first-attempt accuracy per item recorded. Only the human check signs a unit off.

**Why this order.** The map first gives the learner a frame to hang the parts on (P1, limited to moderate). Outcomes come one at a time before they are compared because studying one category at a time draws attention to what its members share, and was the safer start in the one study of verbal categories (P22), and because parts taught alone before they are combined helped novices (P4). The key question arrives at the comparison, and after it, because comparison taught more when the principle was stated afterwards (P12) and because a question about outcomes the learner has not met has nothing to attach to (P3). The learner answers something at the end of every step, so that no explanation goes unused (P16; section 5). The complete route comes after its parts (P17, limited) and before any request to run it (P10, strong for novices). No study tests this whole order on a key (section 7). Each link is supported at the strength its principle states; the order as a whole is a design to be tested with the first-attempt, mixed-versus-blocked and persistence logs that P17, P22 and P28 require.

**Fact units.** (0) Orient: what the list is, why it is worth holding, and the plain statement that this unit is facts to remember, not a skill to apply (P1, P24 requires 7). (1) For each group of facts: the concept the group serves, in plain words with one case (P11); the facts under it, each with how it relates to the concept (P3 requires 5), in a table where the facts share the same attributes (P30); then a check that asks each fact as a question (P16). (2) Drill: every fact asked from memory and explained at once (P16, P19); a missed fact stays in the queue until answered correctly (P24 requires 2). (3) After the unit: each fact returns on the schedule (P24 requires 3).

**Procedure units.** (0) Orient: the kind of problem the procedure solves, in a real situation (P1). (1) The idea behind the procedure in plain words with a concrete case, and what each step is for (P9, P11). (2) A worked example with real numbers, every step shown and named by its purpose (P10 requires 6, P8); a second worked example in a different setting (P13); a prompt on the step that carries the idea (P15). (3) A problem with the last step or two left to the learner, then a whole problem alone (P17). (4) Once a neighbouring procedure has been taught: two look-alike problems that need different procedures, compared, with the feature that tells them apart stated (P12, P30). (5) Drill: types of problem mixed, so that the learner has to choose the procedure (P22 requires 8). (6) Close and after the unit: as for a classification unit. Relearning on later days gave only a small benefit for probability problems in the one set of experiments that tested it (pooled d = 0.28; P24 boundary conditions), so in a procedure unit the worked examples and the mixed drill carry more of the load than the returns do.

### 4.4 The sequence across a subject

The key belongs to the subject, so some things are decided once for the subject, not unit by unit. Each follows from the principles cited; no study tests the order of a whole course built on a key.

- **The map comes first** (P1, P30). The subject opens with the whole key as a map in plain words and a list of every outcome the subject teaches, each with a few plain words. In Psychology the comprehension audit found that the learner first met the key's branch questions and their options inside the scored determination.
- **The lessons teach the key's own questions: all of them, and no others** (P5, P9 requires 1). The same audit found the course teaching one set of questions while the key asked another. Each could be sound and the subject would still fail P5.
- **The gate question is the first unit** (P3 requires 6: every route starts with it, so everything else depends on it). It runs through the unit sequence like any other key question; the families it sorts cases into are that unit's outcomes.
- **Each later unit teaches one branch of the key**, or part of a branch where the branch has more outcomes than one unit can carry: its key questions and its outcomes. It uses the gate question without re-teaching it (P2 requires 1). When a branch unit is finished, the learner has every question needed for that branch's outcomes, so the worked case of step 3 in that unit is a complete determination.
- **The determination follows the teaching** (P10, P22). By default the learner is asked to run the whole key only on specimens whose outcomes have all been taught in units they have been through, and a complete worked determination is shown before the first scored one (P10 requires 1). A learner who wants to try earlier does so through the opt-in check of P2 requires 2.
- **Earlier units keep returning** (P22 requires 4, P24). Every unit drill after the first carries items from earlier units, and the determination mixes every branch taught so far.

## 5. Explanation length

**The short answer.** The evidence sets no maximum length for an explanation, a card or a unit, and none was tested: across all four notes no study sets, tests or supports a length cap. Three of the notes say so in as many words, and the fourth says the evidence does not answer how long a drill or a run of checks should be. What the evidence constrains is function (what each sentence does) and chunking (how much is shown at once), not word count. That supports the owner's standing instruction: an idea that needs two paragraphs gets two paragraphs, and a short explanation is not a goal. Squeezing an idea that needs two paragraphs into two phrases removes relevant material, which the evidence on novices says hurts learning (P3, P9, P10). It does not show that unlimited length is free: the cost in learner time, and in learners who stop, is unmeasured (section 7). So the app measures completion and return rate (P28) rather than assuming either way, and it answers a persistence problem by splitting, reordering or adding a check, never by cutting what a beginner needs. That there is no limit on content is the owner's decision, and nothing in the evidence argues against it.

**Three things that "short" blurs together.**

| What is being cut | Effect on a beginner | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| Material that is irrelevant to the goal (anecdotes, trivia, tangents, decoration) | Helps: better recall of main ideas and better transfer | P6 |
| Material that is relevant (the link to the last idea, a definition, the reason a question is asked, an example, the near-miss) | Hurts: low-knowledge readers learn less from text that leaves gaps; rewrites that added clarity and elaboration helped while simplifying did not; steps without principles help only first-time performance | P3, P4, P9, P10, P11, P12 |
| How much is shown at once (a long continuous block) | Helps when it is cut into learner-paced segments: small to medium gains, lower load, more time on task | P7 |

So the thing to keep small is the step: one new point at a time, at the learner's pace. The total amount of teaching is not the thing to keep small. For context only, and not evidence about the length of text: in one observational maths study the most effective teachers spent about 23 of 40 minutes presenting, demonstrating and working examples against about 11 for the least effective (Rosenshine 2012; school teachers, presentation time).

**What squeezing looks like.** The comprehension audit of Psychology found each concept given 40 to 110 words: one definition and a "tell", set in the smallest type on the card, with no example before the label. The tell is where the difference between two outcomes was taught. One reads, in full: "Tell: histrionic wants an audience. Narcissistic wants a mirror. Watch what happens when attention shifts to someone else - histrionic escalates for attention; narcissistic devalues whoever now has it." Three short sentences are doing the work of several cards. The line does contain a usable test (watch what happens when attention moves to someone else). What it lacks is everything a beginner needs in order to use that test, measured against the table below: a case of each outcome in which attention moves and the two reactions are shown; the metaphor "mirror" explained (the audit, finding 4.2, notes that it never is); the two cases side by side; the key question that this difference answers; and a new case to try. A beginner can repeat the line and still not tell the two apart in a real case. The repair is not a longer sentence. It is the missing parts, each on its own card.

**What an explanation of one idea must contain.** A novice must be able to restate the idea and use it on a new case. Not every idea needs every item, but each item below that applies must be present. The rows are in the order in which section 4.3 teaches them, and they are spread over the cards of its steps 1 to 3, not packed onto one card.

| Part | Why it is there | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| The link back: why this idea follows the last one | Readers with little background cannot supply links | P3 |
| One concrete case showing the feature, with the deciding cue marked | Novices learn from worked cases, and the signal says where to look | P10, P8, P11 |
| How to recognise it: the decisive feature in general words | General wording is portable | P11, P13 |
| The idea in ordinary words, then the term | Terms define the target but cost fluency if they arrive first | P4 |
| A second case in a different story | One case teaches its story; two cases that share only the feature teach the feature | P12, P13 |
| What the outcome is like in general, with its exceptions and hybrids | Classification practice narrows attention to separating features | P14 |
| The nearest neighbour and the exact difference (the near-miss) | Contrast teaches the boundary | P12 |
| What it decides in the key: which question, which outcomes each answer keeps and rules out | A key followed without its rationale gives first-time performance but not transfer | P9 |
| What a result means ("so you can rule out X") | A reader should not have to infer it | P3 |
| The other names real life uses ("also called ...") | Variants must be taught, not left to chance | P5 |
| A check after each step, so the explanation is used | Explanation helps when the learner acts on it, not by being present | P15, P16 |

**What gets cut.** Anything whose only job is interest: anecdotes, trivia, history tangents, name-dropping, jokes, decorative images, vivid example detail that competes with the decisive feature, incidental jargon, a second synonym for a term already taught, and restating a point already made on the same card. If something is kept for interest it is marked optional and nothing later tests it (P6).

**Is there an evidence-based cap?** No cap on total explanation. The evidence does set these limits, none of which is a word count:
- One new point per card (P7). A card that makes two points is split into two cards. A card that makes one point is never cut down, whatever its length.
- Feedback arrives in manageable pieces with the decisive sentence first (P20). Some learners skip feedback entirely, and how far phone readers read is unmeasured, so the ordering is a judgement call. A wrong answer may need as much as it needs.
- **(judgement call)** Screen fit is a check, not a limit. A card that runs past one phone screen is checked for a second point. If it makes only one, it continues on the next card with a restating line, or it scrolls; nothing is cut (P7 requires 3). The preference for a second card comes from one desktop study of scrolling pages, not from a validated threshold.
- No timers or auto-advance, because the screen-reading penalty grows under time pressure (P7).
- Explanation does not teach by being present: instructional explanations added little on average in one meta-analysis. The remedy the evidence supports is to make the learner use each explanation (a check at the end of every step of section 4.3; P15, P16, P17), not to shorten it. A unit with full cards and a token drill has too little practice, not too much explanation (P16).
- The conversational-voice benefit was small and unreliable in lessons longer than about 35 minutes. This bounds one effect (P4), not the amount of content a learner may study across sittings.
- The cost of length in learner time, and in learners who stop, is unmeasured (section 7). P28 requires it to be measured. It is a reason to look at order and structure, never a reason to cut what a beginner needs.

**How to decide when an explanation is long enough.** Length is an output, not an input. It follows from the number of ideas, the links between them and what a real beginner can do after reading:
1. A person with no background reads the unit cold, says in their own words what each card said, and passes the check on a new case (P27 requires 1(b); the automated coverage check does not stand in for this).
2. If they cannot, the explanation is missing something. Add it (P3); do not add drill repetitions (P17).
3. If a card makes two points, split it at the boundary between them. If it makes one point and runs past a screen, continue it on the next card with a restating line, or let it scroll (P7 requires 3). Never shorten it to fit.
4. If a sentence has no job, cut it (P6).

## 6. What research does not support

Each row is a belief that is common in lesson design and is not supported by the evidence in the four notes. The right-hand column says what Fieldcraft does instead.

| The belief | What the evidence says | What the lessons do |
|---|---|---|
| Teach each learner in their "learning style" (visual, auditory, and so on) | Pashler et al. (2008), review, found no adequate evidence base for tailoring instruction to assessed styles and virtually none for the pattern the idea needs (the best method differing between style groups), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x), `abs`. Rogowsky, Calhoun & Tallal (2015), college-educated adults, found no relationship between preferred style and learning from audio versus text, immediately or two weeks later, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037478), `abs`. Strong evidence of absence | No style modes. Offering another format for accessibility is a separate question the notes do not cover |
| Rereading, highlighting and summarising are good ways to study | Dunlosky et al. (2013), `full`: summarisation, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text and rereading rated low utility; practice testing is the replacement. Note-taking was not among the ten techniques they rated, so nothing here supports or opposes it; the app's choice not to ask for notes is a judgement call (P8, P16). Learner-generated highlighting helped memory but not comprehension, while instructor-provided highlighting helped both (Ponce et al. 2022, `sec`). Callender & McDaniel (2009) on rereading, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2008.07.001), is `rec` only | Review is answering, not rereading; the author supplies the emphasis (P8, P16) |
| Let learners work it out for themselves | Unassisted discovery loses to explicit instruction (d about -0.38, Alfieri et al.); assisted discovery with feedback, worked examples, scaffolds or elicited explanations does better (d about 0.30). | If it feels hard, it is working ("desirable difficulties") | The framework is post hoc and does not predict which difficulty pays off. Difficulties that pay (spacing, retrieval, mixing) pay when the learner can still succeed or gets feedback; a large mismatch in a multimedia study was as harmful as exact duplication (Yue et al. 2013); nothing verified shows that a harder-to-follow explanation helps | Difficulty lives in the practice schedule; the explanation is as clear as possible (P23) |
| Learners know which way of studying works best | They rate the worse method higher: massing over spacing, blocking over mixing, rereading over testing; predictions of their own performance did not correlate with actual performance (P23) | The app schedules and shows results (P23, P24) |
| Short is better (summaries beat text, bite-sized microlearning, minimalist manuals) | The summary advantage is old, narrow and about memory for main points; microlearning reviews test no length; the minimalist manuals were short but task-first with explicit error recovery for a procedure (P6, P7, P9) | Cut irrelevant material only; length follows the idea (section 5) |
| A card must fit one phone screen | One desktop experiment found that a scrolling format lowered understanding of complex text; no study sets a threshold for a phone, while separating a rule from its case and cutting relevant explanation are both known to hurt (P7, P8, P3) | One new point per card. A point that needs more room continues on a second card or scrolls, and nothing is cut to fit (P7 requires 3) |
| A readability score or a sentence-length rule makes text clear | Formula-driven rewrites deleted the logic and sometimes made text harder; common readability indexes such as Flesch-Kincaid scored cohesive and incohesive versions differently in a way that did not track cohesion (McNamara et al. 2010); no experiment isolates sentence length for adult novices (P4) | Gate on defined terms, written-out inferences and a novice restating the card (P4, P27) |
| Tell people to slow down or to watch for their biases | Cognitive forcing training changed nothing in 191 medical students; being told to be careful gave the same accuracy as being told to be quick; teaching bias recognition has not reduced errors, while structured steps and organised knowledge have small consistent benefits (P14) | Teach what outcomes are like and how they differ; the key is the structured step |
| Teaching thinking skills makes people better thinkers in general | Far transfer rarely occurs, and effects shrank as designs improved (Sala & Gobet); near-to-moderate transfer of a specific rule taught with varied examples is achievable (P25) | State the transfer target and teach for it; no general-thinking claims |
| Praise motivates learning | Feedback that draws attention to the self reduces its benefit; reinforcement and punishment had the lowest effects (d = 0.24); effects on motivation were weaker than on learning (P20) | Information, not evaluation. Whether affirming messages keep people studying is untested here |
| More feedback is always better | The review of feedback calls the findings on amount of information inconsistent: one meta-analysis found no relationship, one study found complex feedback lowered efficiency, and many learners skip feedback (Shute 2008; Timmers & Veldkamp 2011; P20) | Decisive sentence first, manageable pieces |
| Interleave everything | Blocking wins for dissimilar categories, passive study and some verbal material; mixing needs adjacency; words reversed (g = -0.39) (P22) | Block to introduce, then mix the confusable outcomes only; in procedure units, mix the types of problem once each procedure has been practised alone |
| Practice to a high criterion in one sitting is the way to make learning last | Overlearning in one session bought one-week recall that faded (P24). Mastery learning itself is supported: Kulik et al. (1990) found positive effects on examination performance, larger for weaker students, but also that self-paced mastery programmes often lowered completion in college classes | Per-item mastery inside a spaced schedule. No hard unit gate by default, which is a judgement call about completion risk, not a finding against mastery; a soft "review these first" prompt instead (P17 requires 5, P24) |
| An exact accuracy target (85 percent) is established | The 85 percent rule is a derivation for neural-network learners; Rosenshine's 80 percent is an observational association; no validated target exists for adults learning classifications (P17) | Starting values only, tuned on real learner data |
| A subject expert or the author can tell whether a lesson is clear | Experts misjudge what novices find hard and debiasing did not help (P27); a fluent explanation raises perceived learning without raising actual learning (P23) | Cold read by a novice; first-attempt accuracy |
| Cognitive load theory is proven, so any technique it predicts is proven | Its founder acknowledges replication failures were part of its history and argues they were resolved by refining the theory (Sweller 2023, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09817-2), `abs`); an independent critique raises open conceptual, methodological and application issues (de Jong 2010, [doi](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-009-9110-0), `abs`) | The practical findings used here (worked examples, segmenting, signalling, expertise reversal) rest on meta-analyses that stand without the theory; the theory is treated as an explanation, not as evidence |

## 7. Where the evidence is thin

Labels: **GAP** = nothing found; **JUDGEMENT** = a design choice made where the evidence is silent or split (also marked in the REQUIRES lines); **CONFLICT** = the research notes or sources disagree; **DROPPED** = a lead the notes could not confirm, not used.

### Gaps

- **GAP: the app's own task.** Nothing found studies reading a case, answering a branching set of narrowing questions, naming the outcome, and scoring name and route separately. The nearest evidence is category learning (birds, paintings), diagnosis from ECGs and radiographs, and maths problem-type discrimination. Whether the key should be taught before or after the outcomes, whether routes are practised better whole or in steps, and whether separate scoring improves learning are untested. Sections 4.2 and 4.3 choose an order from neighbouring evidence (map, then outcomes, then key questions, then the route); it is a design to be tested, not a finding.
- **GAP: everyday verbal and contested categories.** Ideologies, scam types, psychological effects, fallacies and civics concepts have not been studied for interleaving, comparison or transfer. The interleaving meta-analysis found no clear benefit for expository text and a reversal for words, so the visual-category results cannot be assumed to carry over; the nearest positive result is one study of statistical concepts (Sana, Yan & Kim 2017).
- **GAP: adults studying alone on a phone with no stakes.** Almost all studies are classroom or laboratory work with children, undergraduates or medical trainees. Nothing tests typing effort for explanation prompts, whether learners stay with a drill that feels harder, or how many items fit a sitting. A phone-versus-laptop comparison seen in a search summary could not be verified and is not used.
- **GAP: engagement and dropout.** The research measures learning, not whether people keep going. Whether complete explanations and effortful schedules cost users cannot be sized from this literature, and it could cut against the recommendations. P28 turns this from a footnote into a requirement: persistence is measured (session starts, completions, return rate), and the answer to a persistence problem is order and structure, never a cap on content.
- **GAP: fact and procedure units.** The principles were derived for classification units. Fact units rest on spaced-retrieval evidence (mostly facts) and procedure units on worked-example evidence; no study found tests either in this app's setting (section 2).
- **GAP: misconceptions in these subjects.** Refutation text is tested mostly on science concepts; nothing found tests it on political, financial or scam beliefs (P29).
- **GAP: terminology consistency.** Only indirect evidence (P5).
- **GAP: length.** No study sets or tests any optimal length. Any specific length rule is a design choice (section 5).
- **GAP: feedback on the route.** No experiment retrieved tests feedback that corrects the reasoning route when the final answer was right (P21).
- **GAP: durability.** Most tests were minutes to a few days. Debiasing effects were followed for two months and fraud education for six months with a reminder. Whether a learner later recognises the category unprompted in their own life is almost unmeasured.
- **GAP: own-life application.** No evidence on using if-then plans, own-life examples or reflection to carry a classification skill into daily life (P26). The relevance studies cited in P25 concern interest and grades in school and laboratory settings, not transfer.
- **GAP: quantities.** Not established: cards per idea, checks per lesson, items per drill or per returned set, cases per outcome, the ratio of clean to messy cases, how long to block before mixing, how many outcomes to mix in one drill, and the right spacing for knowledge a person wants to keep indefinitely (the ridgeline ratios are for trivia facts with a known test delay).
- **GAP: evidence quality.** Several findings come from single groups that champion the method (interleaved maths, productive failure, perceptual learning modules, debiasing games, pre-training). Heterogeneity is large (I-squared about 77% in the interleaving meta-analysis). Pan & Rickard's publication-bias-adjusted transfer estimates were much lower than the headline, and bias adjustments for the other meta-analyses were not checked. Classroom retrieval work is mostly from Western, educated, wealthy countries.
- **GAP: pictures and diagrams.** Reviewed only as far as P30 needs (graphics that show the structure of the content). Illustrated cases, photographs, animation and audio were not reviewed; this app is text-first. Nothing found tests a key shown as a map, or any table, on a phone screen.
- **GAP: unread arguments.** The two replies in the testing-effect-and-complexity dispute (Karpicke & Aue; Rawson) were read only as search-returned abstracts and summaries, not in full; the conditions under which interim testing impairs new learning (Chan et al.), and the "balance of study and test trials" result (Jacoby et al.) were not read beyond titles or abstracts.

### Conflicts between the notes or sources

- **CONFLICT: source status.** Catrambone (1998) is "existence and title only" in one note and "abstract read, four experiments" in another. Alpizar et al. (2020) is "existence only" in one and "abstract read, 29 experiments" in another. Alfieri et al. (2011) was read as secondary numbers in one note, as an abstract in another and in full text in a third; the figures agree across notes. This document tags each with the strongest reading any note achieved and says so where it matters.
- **CONFLICT: pretest feedback.** Feedback on pretest answers lowered final performance in one study and made no difference in another (P1).
- **CONFLICT: feedback timing.** Delayed feedback won in laboratory and some classroom studies, immediate in applied studies and after failed tests (P19).
- **CONFLICT: within-category similarity.** The interleaving meta-analysis and Carvalho & Goldstone disagree; both agree that between-category similarity favours mixing (P22).
- **CONFLICT: multiple choice versus recall.** The IES panel prefers recall formats; a secondary description of Adesope et al. says multiple-choice practice tests gave larger effects. Rowland's table is not a clean "recall beats recognition" ordering either (cued recall g = 0.61, recognition and free recall each 0.29), so the abstract's wording is the safer summary (P18).
- **CONFLICT: self-explanation prompts.** Strong on average (g about 0.55), yet the worked-example meta-analysis found a smaller advantage with prompts, and the cheap phone-friendly format (multiple-choice prompts) is the weakest-supported (P15).
- **CONFLICT: feedback after correct answers.** Simple confirmation helped low-confidence correct answers, while adding an explanation after correct answers added little in a large study (P21).

#### Section 4.3 replaced

The earlier sequence ran every "outcome or key-question group" through one seven-step cycle. It did not say which of the two the cycle was for; it asked for a look-alike pair and a complete worked route inside the first outcome's cycle, before the neighbour or the other key questions had been taught; and it let the learner read up to six steps before answering anything. The new sequence fixes the order: orient with the map; each outcome on its own (meet it, see it again in a different story, use it); each key question taught as the difference between the outcomes on either side of it (compare, name the question, exceptions and wrong ideas, use it); the whole route on a worked case; the drill ramp; close; returns; release checks. A paragraph states the evidence behind each link and says that the order as a whole is untested. Section 4.2 gained four entries for the tensions settled here.
